## Early Spring Oil

March 2026, Lina Choi gazed out from the 52nd floor.

Spring rain in Seoul's downtown trickled down the glass window. Below, electric vehicles slid silently past, and the line at charging stations was twice as long as the old gas stations used to be. The Seoul office of **XOM**—not that the company was named after the ticker, but that ticker had always clung to her mind—showed her this landscape every day.

Today was board report day.

## When the Numbers Begin to Speak

It was page 17 of the report. Lina read that page three times.

According to data released by the International Energy Agency at the end of 2025, the rate of increase in renewable energy's share of electricity generation was progressing 3.2 years ahead of the existing model's predictions. Solar panel unit prices had fallen to below half compared to 2020, and battery storage capacity was growing at an average of 31% annually. The actual data points were plotted above the curve that consulting firms called the "optimistic scenario." All those points were above the curve.

Her hand stopped.

This wasn't the rhetoric shouted by environmental activists. It was a report co-written by BlackRock and Vanguard. It was figures signed by Goldman Sachs analysts. Wall Street was already remaking its portfolio.

"CEO, the board is waiting in five minutes."

Her secretary's voice came through the intercom.

## Silence in the Boardroom

The board had sixteen members. Most were men in their sixties, and some had spent over thirty years at this company. Their wristwatches were expensive, and their eyes shone with familiar certainty.

"Lina, the dividend plan for this year remains unchanged, yes?"

Chairman Park, the board chairman, asked. He was 78 and still walked without a cane. His father had developed oil fields in the Middle East in the 1970s, and he had inherited and grown them. For him, oil was history, family, and pride.

"The dividend will be maintained."

Lina spoke. The moment the words left her mouth, she felt strangely lighter. It wasn't a lie. It was possible this year. It would probably be possible next year too. Five years from now would be another story.

Investors wanted short-term dividends. 42,000 employees wanted job security. The board wanted the status quo. And her daughter Jisoo was sixteen years old and preparing a climate crisis presentation at school.

## Jisoo's Question

It was two months earlier.

Jisoo opened her laptop at the dinner table and asked.

"Mom, tell me your company's name. I want to put it in my presentation."

Lina paused for a moment, then answered.

"It's just an energy company."

"What kind of energy?"

"Oil."

Jisoo continued typing without lifting her head. That brief silence, that refusal to lift her head, lodged somewhere in Lina's chest. It wasn't a question or criticism. It was merely a confirmation. But that confirmation felt like a verdict.

A sixteen-year-old child hadn't judged anything yet. But someday, she would.

## Oil's Last Weather

After the meeting ended, Lina returned to the window.

The rain had stopped. Between the downtown buildings, the sunset spread long, and the solar canopy above the electric vehicle charging station reflected that light and shone. Someone might have called it beautiful. To Lina, it felt unfamiliar.

She didn't think the company would collapse right away. Oil demand would decline slowly. For perhaps ten or twenty years, there would still be money to be made. The board was right. Dividends were possible. But somewhere in between, the numbers would quietly change direction.

No one knew exactly when that turning point would come. The models kept being wrong, and reality moved faster than the models. And that was precisely the problem. If she waited until the company was ready, the moment when readiness was needed would have already passed.

Lina closed the documents.

The board's decision will be announced tomorrow. Dividends will be maintained. Stock prices will stabilize. Employees will feel reassured. Investors will be satisfied.

And someday, as an adult, Jisoo will read the newspapers from these days.
