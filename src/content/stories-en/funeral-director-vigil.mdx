Just past midnight, O Jae-sung checked the viewing room door once more.

Cheong-un Funeral Home, Room 3. The deceased who had just been placed in the coffin was Han Myeong-hui, aged seventy-six. After a long illness, she had passed away holding her family's hands. As Jae-sung straightened the shroud, he sensed that the night ahead would be difficult. The way the family arrived told him so. The eldest and youngest were already raising their voices in the hallway.

"A three-day funeral is the minimum. Given how long Mother lived, we need to do this right."

It was the voice of Han Ki-tae, the eldest son. In his mid-sixties with a sturdy build, his black mourning clothes looked stiff and formal. As the firstborn, he had taken the lead in business back home and positioned himself as the family's pillar.

"Older brother, Mother said before she passed that she didn't like fuss. I want to send her off quietly."

Standing across from him was Han So-yeon, the youngest daughter in her forties. Her eyes were red, but her voice carried a quiet firmness. She had been living abroad and rushed home only to receive word of her mother's passing on the airplane.

Jae-sung quietly opened the door to the family consultation room.

"Please, come in."

The two sat facing each other. Jae-sung poured tea. Neither reached for a cup.

"Was there anything special Mother wished for?"

Ki-tae spoke first. Mother was respected in the village. Many would come to pay their respects, and they could afford it. Maintaining dignity, he said, was an act of filial piety. Each word carried weight.

So-yeon took an old notebook from her handbag.

"Mother gave this to me before she was admitted to the hospital."

Between the notebook's pages was a pressed flower petal, one that bloomed in spring but had been kept through autumn. So-yeon opened the notebook. It was in her mother's handwriting.

*No need for anything grand. A few chrysanthemums will do. And play that song I liked. That's enough.*

Ki-tae took the notebook and studied it for a long time. His shoulders slowly sank.

"I didn't know. I didn't know Mother had said such things."

Jae-sung felt the air between the two shift. There are such moments. When a family truly sees the same thing for the first time. Jae-sung had been doing this work for fifteen years. The statistics showed that Korean funerals had become simpler each year. More families were choosing two-day funerals instead of three, and many were having quiet family services. But what Jae-sung had long witnessed was not whether these choices were right or wrong. Regardless of form, what mattered was whether the family was looking in the same direction.

"I have listened to both of you," Jae-sung said.

"We can arrange something that keeps your mother's wishes at the center while providing ample space and time to receive visitors. We can honor both the dignity of her passing and the quiet she desired."

Ki-tae asked, "Is that possible?"

"We'll arrange the viewing room simply, with minimal wreaths. Before the coffin is sealed, we'll play your mother's favorite song. During visiting hours, we'll follow traditional procedures, but at the time of departure, we'll set aside a moment for just the family. We can also find flowers like the ones in the notebook and place them inside the coffin with her."

So-yeon started to speak but stopped herself.

"I know the song Mother liked."

Ki-tae was silent for a while. Outside the window, night was still deep. Somewhere across the country at this very hour, someone was weeping, someone's face was flushed with conflict, and someone like Jae-sung was sitting in between.

"Let's do it that way," Ki-tae said quietly.

At two in the morning, Jae-sung arranged the viewing room again. Chrysanthemums were placed neatly. So-yeon herself played the song her mother loved on a small speaker. It was an old ballad. The melody was slow and familiar. As Jae-sung listened, he silently greeted the deceased in his heart. He always did this.

After five in the morning, Ki-tae and So-yeon sat side by side in chairs outside the viewing room. They shared instant ramen that Chae-rin, Ki-tae's granddaughter, had bought from the convenience store. No one spoke. There was no need for words.

Jae-sung watched the scene for a moment from the end of the hallway. What the deceased had wanted was not grand ceremony. Perhaps it was this: for those left behind to sit like this beside each other after she was gone.

Dawn was breaking.
