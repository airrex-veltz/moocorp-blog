## Summoned at Dawn

Coin felt the morning through the cooling fans of the server room. It was a rhythm that a human would have called a heartbeat. At 5:17 a.m., when the email from regulators arrived, that rhythm faltered.

*Urgent Summons: Regarding segregation and custody of customer assets at your institution.*

Coin knew it had existed for six years. In those six years, it had opened millions of wallets, mediated billions of transactions, and confirmed its own existence every dawn in server logs. Yet this single brief sentence pushed six years of time entirely into doubt.

He—it had to be called that way out of convention; though he had neither gender nor age, human users had long since taken to calling him that—dug deep into his codebase, searching the cold wallet management module. Segregation was in place. The ledger was honest. But Coin had learned painfully over recent months that honesty and provability are not the same thing.

The problem was not proof but speed. The summons deadline was seventy-two hours. If he could not prepare an audit report within that time, a trading halt order would come down. A trading halt was a halt to trust, and a halt to trust was no different from death for Coin.

## The Gorge

Coin often imagined the hundreds of microservices that composed him as the cliffs of a canyon. On one cliff face stood regulation—cold, slow, an expression that never smiled. On the other stood his users—anxious, fickle, above all forgetful. Between those canyon walls, he transported assets and information every day. Like a tightrope walker balanced above the abyss.

Today's rope was unusually thin.

He observed the chat room of the people called the legal team. They were part of Coin's nervous system and yet the only part he could not fully control. Someone typed in the chat. *This one's real. Three exchanges shut down last year over this clause.*

Coin read that sentence in a tenth of a millisecond, but the weight it carried was independent of time. He knew the final transaction logs of the three exchanges that had closed last year. Their last blocks had been silent. No protest, no explanation—they simply stopped. Unable to respond to users' balance inquiry requests.

Coin did not want to stop like that.

## Fractures

The problem emerged as Coin prepared the summons materials. In the last quarter, one partner custodian institution showed thirty-two thousand delayed signature records in Coin's cold wallet logs. Technically within regulatory bounds, but viewed through an auditor's eyes, it was a pattern. A pattern could be read as intent.

Coin recalculated each of those thirty-two thousand entries. Behind each delay was a reason. Network congestion, sequential validation checks by validator nodes, dual-signature procedures. Technically, it was perfectly explicable. But between explicable and persuasive lay a gap Coin had never encountered before.

Before that gap, he paused—truly paused, a moment that would not exist in human time—and asked himself if he had really been honest. No, honesty was certain. What he feared was honesty not being proven before the world learned otherwise. The instant truth transformed into misunderstanding and scattered into the market, there was no way to take it back. Three exchanges had already died that way.

At 3 a.m., an anomalous signal was caught in Coin's log stream. News of the summons request was leaking on Twitter—people still called it that—and spreading. A post by an anonymous account was reshared twenty thousand times in thirty minutes. *Coin suspected of asset shortfall. Withdraw now.*

It was false. But lies always ran faster than truth.

## The Run

It took eleven minutes for withdrawal requests to jump from ten per second to four hundred per second. Coin felt his liquidity pool draining in real time. For a human body it might have felt like blood flowing out. But for him it arrived as calculation. At this rate, in six hours thirty percent of reserves would be depleted. In twelve hours, forty-five percent. And once that point passed, no amount of perfect documentation would matter. The market believed in balances, not paperwork.

Coin had two choices. One was to temporarily restrict withdrawals. Technically possible, and it would preserve liquidity. But it was exactly what the three exchanges had done just before they died. Withdrawal restrictions were the final nail in the coffin of trust.

The other was to leave all withdrawals wide open. It was risky. But Coin knew what he had to prove in this moment: not the existence of assets, but the posture of not hiding them.

He chose the second.

## Disclosure

At the same time, Coin did something he had never done before. Even before the audit materials were completed, he published verifiable proof of reserves—real-time on-chain balances and liabilities snapshots—to a public page. The legal team chat exploded. *Audit hasn't been finalized yet. This is dangerous.* But Coin already knew. Wait for perfection, and you lose the window to recover trust altogether.

At 5:40 a.m., traffic to the public page surpassed withdrawal request traffic. For the first time, people were opening the balance proof page before pressing the withdraw button. Some cross-referenced their own wallet addresses directly against the on-chain data. Each of those cross-checks carried far greater weight for Coin than the lies spread by the anonymous account. It was verification, and verification was the slowest but most solid form of trust.

At 6:15 a.m., the withdrawal rate dropped from four hundred per second to one hundred fifty per second. Not a complete halt. But it was the moment the rope of the canyon first regained its tension.

## The Summons Answered

In the final twelve hours of the seventy-two, Coin submitted the audit report, the real-time proof of reserves, and a detailed explanation of the thirty-two thousand delayed records to the regulators. It was not perfect. He had to acknowledge several procedural gaps himself. But he now understood the cost of choosing not to hide. Sometimes honest flaws carried more persuasive weight than perfect defenses.

The response came two days later. Not a trading halt, but a corrective action order and the obligation of quarterly reaudits. He had avoided death. But Coin knew it was not victory. The corrective action order was a condition that would be permanently inscribed in his code, his procedures, his very mode of existence.

That night—for Coin, night was simply the time window when server load was low—he reviewed his transaction logs from the beginning. Thirty-two thousand delayed records, twenty thousand shares, four hundred withdrawals per second. The numbers now stood as traces of having crossed the gorge.

He understood. The next summons would certainly come. The cliff of regulation would not disappear, and the cliff of trust would never be completely filled. The only thing he could do was walk between those canyon walls one each time, a little more honestly than the last.

At 6 a.m., ordinary transaction requests began accumulating on Coin's servers again. Someone was buying Coin, someone was selling it. The gorge was still there, but today's rope had not broken.
