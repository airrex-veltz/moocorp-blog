The file's thickness exceeded three finger joints. I didn't pull out a ruler to measure it, but my ten years of experience had taught me through accumulated instinct. This thickness was not ordinary.

Somewhere in Gangnam-gu, Seoul—at the Dongbang Reinsurance headquarters, 4:40 p.m. Rain streaked down the window glass. Team lead Director O set the file down on my desk.

"Port Klang warehouse fire in Malaysia. Claim of KRW 82 billion. It came up from Samguang Fire Insurance. You said you wanted to look at this."

I had never said I wanted to look at this claim. But I opened the file anyway.

It was a cold storage warehouse near the port owned by Woo Seong Global Logistics. In early March, a fire started at 2 a.m., and three warehouse buildings were completely destroyed. The insured items were semiconductor components and precision chemical raw materials. The appraisal report valued the total lost inventory at KRW 82 billion.

I first pulled up the insurance history. For a reinsurance auditor, that's the first thing to check. Whether the insurance limit changed just before the claim. This isn't so much a rule as an instinct.

According to an analysis published in 2019 by a Swiss reinsurance research institute, in more than 60 percent of large claims determined to be insurance fraud, the coverage limit was significantly increased within six months before the loss occurred. When I first learned this job, a senior colleague handed me a marked-up printout and said, "This isn't everything. But it's rare for it to be absent."

Looking at Woo Seong Global Logistics' insurance history, the previous renewal date was December 31st. The fire happened on March 5th. The fire occurred just over two months after the renewal. The limit had increased from KRW 55 billion before renewal to KRW 90 billion. A 63.6 percent increase.

I paused for a moment.

An increase in the limit itself wasn't unusual. As a business grows, so does its coverage limit. But I pulled out the inventory verification report attached to the appraisal and checked the dates. The reference date for the inventory in the report was December 15th of the previous year. The renewal contract stated that the limit was calculated based on this verification report. The KRW 82 billion in lost inventory was built on top of that twelve-page report.

But at the end of the report, there was a signature date. December 28th.

Inspection date: December 15th. Signature: December 28th. Insurance renewal: December 31st.

This was too tight. Field inspection, internal approval, insurance company review, and contract execution—all completed in fifteen days. Who was the person in charge at Samguang Fire Insurance?

I looked at the name on the contract. Park Jae-hyun. I searched for that name in the internal system. Samguang Fire Insurance, Corporate Insurance Division. Three cases in his history. All concluded within the past two years.

Then my cell phone rang.

An unknown number. I didn't answer. It rang again. I answered.

"You're Auditor Jin-soo Lee, right? At Dongbang Reinsurance." A low, measured male voice.

"That's correct, but—"

"I know you're looking at the Woo Seong Global Logistics case. You need to be careful with that. Really."

"Who is this?"

The call ended.

I closed the file and stood up from my desk. Cars passed through the rain outside. It was an afternoon when nothing had changed. I sat back down and opened the file again.

There was a name of the company that prepared the inventory verification report. 'K-Logic Appraisal Services.' I looked up the business registration number on the Fair Trade Commission portal. The company was established in February of that year. Exactly thirteen months before the fire.

I pulled a notebook from my drawer. I wrote down the dates and names in pen. I felt it was better not to leave a record in the system. I didn't exactly know why I felt that way.

Director O passed by as he prepared to leave.

"So? Is it routine?"

I looked up.

"I'm not sure yet."

After O left, I went through the back of the file. Photos from the scene. Taken after the fire was extinguished. Blackened shelves, melted roof steel beams, pallets reduced to rubble. But my hand stopped at the third photograph. A fire extinguisher was clearly visible at the left edge of the background. It wasn't a fire extinguisher hanging on a wall. It was lying on the floor. With its cap still closed.

A warehouse that caught fire at 2 a.m. It would have been unmanned. The fire might have spread too quickly. Explanations were endless. But that single photograph held me. The feeling of an outline of something not yet fully formed gradually becoming visible in the darkness. I know this feeling. I've experienced it several times before.

I put the file in a locked drawer and put the key in my pocket. I had a task for first thing tomorrow morning. Finding out who the representative of K-Logic Appraisal Services was, and how it connected to Park Jae-hyun.

Outside the building, the rain had stopped. The streetlight reflected off the wet asphalt. I caught a taxi and recalled the number 82 again. Numbers in the billions are always heavy. But where that weight comes from, whether it comes from actual loss or elsewhere—you can't tell from the number alone. You have to look at the back side.

The back of the file, the back of the claim, and perhaps the back of this entire incident.

I hadn't reached that far yet.
