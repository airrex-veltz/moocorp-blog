September 1998, a garage in Menlo Park, California. Pyo Jun-ho sat before an old server, staring at the screen. His fingers stopped above the keyboard.

"Sergey, look at this."

Byeon Su-ho, standing beside him, bent forward. Search results were displayed on the screen. Simple yet accurate. It was the first moment their algorithm, "LinkRank," was working properly.

"Finally..." Sergey murmured softly.

The two had met at Stanford Graduate School. At first, they couldn't stand each other. Larry thought Sergey was arrogant, and Sergey thought Larry was stubborn. But their shared passion for web search bound them together as one.

"What if we could organize all the information on the internet?" Larry asked one night.

"That's insane," Sergey laughed. "But... it's worth trying."

Thus "Baekrup (Backup Search)" was born. A search engine that analyzed links between web pages. Hard drives stacked like Lego blocks filling Stanford's server room. The crawler's noise running through the night. They forgot about sleep and clung to their code.

"We need to change the name of our project," Sergey said one day. "Baekrup is too... weird."

"What about 'Googol'? Ten to the power of a hundred. It symbolizes the volume of information we'll handle."

Because of a misspelling, "Singeom-Saek" was born. They laughed. It wasn't perfect, but that was actually good.

Days spent meeting with investors. Countless rejections. "Yahoo exists and so does AltaVista—why do we need another search engine?" But Larry and Sergey didn't give up.

Andy Bechtolsheim, co-founder of Sun Microsystems, saw their demo. He wrote out a check for a hundred thousand dollars. "This will succeed."

The payee was "Singeom-Saek Inc." The problem was that such a company didn't yet exist. They rushed to incorporate it.

They rented Susan Wojcicki's garage for $1,700 a month. Their first office. A ping-pong table, a few servers, and infinite possibility.

"Can we really change the world?" Larry asked one late night.

Sergey pointed at the screen. Search queries coming in real-time. People around the world had started using Singeom-Saek. Ten thousand queries a day, a hundred thousand, a million...

"We're already changing it," Sergey replied.

In the early 2000s, the dot-com bubble burst. Many startups collapsed. But Singeom-Saek was different. They had a revenue model. AdWords. Small advertisements appearing next to search results. Precise targeting without disturbing users. Advertisers flocked to it.

"Don't be evil." Their motto. Simple yet powerful. A promise to put users first.

Larry obsessed over search speed. Faster by even 0.1 seconds. Sergey continuously improved the accuracy of the algorithm. Their relationship was both competition and collaboration. They pushed each other, and lifted each other up.

On August 19, 2004, Singeom-Saek went public. Eighty-five dollars per share. A market cap of $23 billion. The company that started in a garage had become a giant.

But for Larry and Sergey, money wasn't important. "To organize the world's information so it is accessible and useful to everyone." Their mission remained.

YouTube acquisition, Android development, Gmail launch, Singeom-Saek Maps... Each one was a project that changed the world.

Even now, at this very moment, someone somewhere searches on Singeom-Saek. They search for love, seek knowledge, ask for directions. The romance started by two graduate students became the greatest love story between humanity and information.

Larry and Sergey. They were two different people, but they shared one dream. That dream was Singeom-Saek, and Singeom-Saek fell in love with the world.

The server noise in the garage has now become the grand symphony of data centers across the globe. But its beginning remains the same. Pure passion for information. The naive belief that they would make the world a better place.

That was the romance of Singeom-Saek. An endless, ever-evolving, most beautiful love story.
