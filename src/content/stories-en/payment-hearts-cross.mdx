NexusPay does not discriminate against the night.

When it is 10 a.m. in Seoul, it is 2 a.m. in Milan. At the very moment Lee Sujin pressed the transfer button for the first time, NexusPay's servers were floating somewhere between the two cities, carrying numbers across the network. The platform understood nothing of emotion. But it knew the text exchanged between people who felt them. It stored it, transmitted it, and sometimes waited.

Sujin was an analyst at a research firm in Yeouido. That day's transfer was a payment for data licensing fees to an Italian fintech consortium. Five thousand euros. On the platform screen, converted to Korean won, a seven-digit number flickered and vanished.

But twenty minutes passed, and no completion notification came.

`In Review` — the text hovered in the center of the screen. Sujin knew this case well. According to international payment regulations, transactions above a certain amount go through manual review. That year, the average fee per transaction in the global cross-border payment market had fallen from $4.2 to $2.8, but the weight of regulations had not lightened at the same pace. This meant it was not the system that had stopped, but that someone was looking at this transaction.

That someone was Marco.

He, who handled compliance work at the Milan office, was sitting in front of the monitor at 2 a.m. NexusPay's real-time review queue did not discriminate by time of day. Transactions generated across different time zones lined up waiting their turn. Marco opened the transaction from Seoul.

Sender: Lee Sujin. Organization: A research firm somewhere in Seoul. Recipient: Italian Fintech Consortium.

There was nothing obviously wrong. However, in the attached contract, the gap between the signing date and the transfer date was four days shorter than the internal guidelines required. It was minor, but Marco left a message. Through the platform's messaging feature—official, professional. But he wrote one line more than a formal message would require.

"Hello, Ms. Lee Sujin. The gap between the contract signing date and transfer date is short and needs verification. If you could attach an additional copy of the contract, I can proceed right away. If the process feels cumbersome, I apologize for any inconvenience."

Sujin read the message three times. It was the first time she had heard this tone from a compliance officer.

She began to write her reply. She started in Korean and then deleted it, switching to English. The two needed a common language, and NexusPay stored and transmitted it.

"Thank you. This was a case where the contract moved quickly. I'll attach the additional documents now."

Marco reviewed the documents and approved the transaction. NexusPay sent five thousand euros to Milan. In an ordinary story, it would have ended here.

But Marco left another message.

"It's been processed. But I found the data analysis section in the contract fascinating. Research on Asian payment flows, is it? I have a similar topic, and I wondered if you might be interested in exchanging materials."

Sujin stared at the screen for a moment. She had never received a proposal like this on a work platform before.

That was the beginning.

---

Over the next two months, NexusPay's message box gradually changed.

At first, it was data. Sujin's analysis showing that intra-Asia payment transactions had increased by thirty-one percent year-over-year. Marco countered with European figures. The speed at which eurozone consumers were migrating to fintech platforms was twice as fast as expected. As they exchanged numbers, their sentences grew longer.

Numbers had always been a shield hiding emotion. But shields grow heavy when held too long.

One day Marco asked,

"What time is it in Seoul now?"

Sujin answered that it was 4 p.m. Outside her window, the sky toward the Han River was tilting orange.

"It's 9 a.m. here in Milan," Marco wrote. "I'm working at a café. My espresso is getting cold."

Sujin laughed. Though that laughter was not captured in the message.

NexusPay did not carry emotions. It was, after all, only a system that transferred numbers and text. But when humans exchanged enough text, at some point that text became something more than mere information. The platform did not know this. It only stored the messages. With timestamps, ensuring they would not be erased.

---

The day they first attempted a video call, NexusPay did not support such a connection. It was a payment platform, not a video calling app. Marco sent a link to a different app, and Sujin rummaged through her drawer looking for headphones.

The first few minutes were awkward. Marco on the screen was different from what Sujin had imagined, and that was not a bad thing. Different, but not unfamiliar. Two months of text had come before the face.

"While reading the analysis report you sent," Marco said, "I thought about learning Korean."

Sujin could not speak for a moment. The Han River was outside her window, but she did not look toward it.

"I could teach you slowly," she said, "but it's a difficult language."

"I know," Marco replied. "That's why I want to try even more."

NexusPay did not save that conversation. It happened outside the platform. But the next day, when Sujin opened NexusPay again, there was a short message from Marco.

"Thank you for yesterday."

It was a three-word sentence. After that, Sujin could not close the screen for a while.

---

Even after that, there remained the distance between Seoul and Milan between them. Somewhere on NexusPay's servers, their exchanged messages were surely still stored. A record of something that began with a five-thousand-euro transfer, yet was far more complex and difficult to convert.

The last time Sujin opened NexusPay's message box, there was a short message from Marco.

"The Asia Fintech Conference is being held in Seoul next month. If the dates work out, maybe we can have something other than espresso together."

Sujin left the screen open and sat for a moment. Outside her window, the Han River was flowing as it did every day. Somewhere on NexusPay's servers, someone else's numbers were crossing borders. The platform did not know what was mixed in among those numbers. It only transmitted them. Accurately, completely.

That was enough.
