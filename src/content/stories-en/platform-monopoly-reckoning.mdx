Lee Jun-hyuk founded the company at thirty-four.

That winter, five of them crowded into a semi-basement office in Mapo-gu, staring at laptop screens and skipping meals. The company was called Cheongyeon. It meant "blue connection," and within those two words lay a simple dream: to link people with one another.

Ten years passed. Cheongyeon had become a platform every Korean used. Messages were sent through it. Things were bought. Food was ordered. Videos were watched. Money changed hands. All of it happened inside Cheongyeon. The market cap exceeded KRW 50 trillion. The workforce approached eight thousand. The press called Lee Jun-hyuk "the father of Korean platforms."

But now he stood alone before a glass window.

---

The Fair Trade Commission began moving that autumn. Authorities opened an investigation into the sprawling platform, suspecting cross-subsidization and market predation as it operated simultaneously as a messenger, payment processor, and commerce marketplace. Cheongyeon's share of the messaging market had reached 89% by then. The number was both a badge of success and the largest target imaginable.

The morning investigators arrived at the headquarters in Seocho-dong, Lee Jun-hyuk happened to be in Jeju. It was his wife's birthday. When he landed at Gimpo Airport, cameras crowded in front of the building. The head of the legal team greeting him in the lobby bowed a touch lower than usual. Lee Jun-hyuk did not miss that subtle shift.

---

There were three parliamentary hearings. Lee Jun-hyuk sat in the witness seat all three times. Lawmakers took turns asking. Had Cheongyeon forced merchants to use its payment service? Had it secretly removed competitors' apps from users' devices? Where had those orders come from?

He answered every question. Some answers were untrue. Some were true but lacked context. Some described things he himself did not know. The company had grown beyond what he could personally manage. That fact tormented him throughout the three-hour hearing. Up on the dais, he felt for the first time the distinct sensation that what he had created had already slipped from his hands.

---

Word of the board meeting came in early December. Among the agenda items was "governance improvement plan." Lee Jun-hyuk read it and sat in silence for a long time. He understood well what governance improvement meant. Twenty years in the industry had taught him.

The meeting lasted two and a half hours. The presenter was a partner from an outside consulting firm. The deck had fifty-five slides. It laid out how global platform companies had managed regulatory crises, how deeply a founder-CEO was tied to regulatory risk, and why a professional management system was now the best way to protect shareholder value at this moment.

Lee Jun-hyuk said nothing after the presentation ended. One director turned to look at him. A co-founder who handled finance and had been with Cheongyeon from the beginning. When he spoke, he used a name instead of a title.

"Jun-hyuk."

That one word pierced deep into Lee Jun-hyuk's chest.

---

That night, after all the employees had left, Lee Jun-hyuk remained alone in the office. Seoul's night skyline glowed quietly beyond the window. Lights from across the Han River seemed to flicker sometimes, though there was no wind.

A text arrived on his phone. It was from Choi Su-jin, an old colleague who had left Cheongyeon long ago. She now worked at a nameless small startup.

*Saw the news. You okay?*

Lee Jun-hyuk stared at the screen for a long time. Saying he was okay would be a lie. Saying he was not okay would not be quite right either. After a long pause, he typed only this:

*Keep thinking about when Cheongyeon first started.*

---

Ten years earlier, Choi Su-jin had been Cheongyeon's second employee. She handled service planning and had helped build the initial skeleton of user flow. Lee Jun-hyuk trusted her instincts. Every time she offered an idea, she said the same thing.

"People use this. We're not selling technology."

That saying became Cheongyeon's philosophy. On the day they first pitched to early investors, that sentence appeared on the first slide of the presentation. Lee Jun-hyuk did not write it. Su-jin had quietly added it the night before.

Su-jin left the company in the year Cheongyeon received its first Series C funding. She said the way decisions were made had changed as the company grew rapidly. She said there was less and less for her to do. Lee Jun-hyuk did not persuade her to stay. He thought he did not have the energy to try. In truth, he probably never even gave it that much thought.

A reply came from Su-jin.

*Those were good times.*

Lee Jun-hyuk laughed alone in the darkness. The laugh felt strange, but it came.

---

He looked out the window again. Seoul still shone. The Cheongyeon icon would be lit up on millions of screens at this very moment. People would be sending messages, connecting with someone, sharing something.

It did not depend on the board's decision. It did not depend on the investigation's outcome. It was not contained in fifty-five slides.

Lee Jun-hyuk stood for a long time. The fact that what he had created now lived on without him, on this night, did not feel like loss. He could not yet say exactly what it felt like.

He put his phone in his pocket. He switched off the office light. While waiting for the elevator, he tried to think of nothing.

But there was one thing he could not help but recall.

That winter, in the semi-basement office, five of them sharing a triangle kimbap from a convenience store.

That was good.
