It was 3:12 a.m.

Lee Ha-jun straightened up from his desk. Turbidity sensor #3 had suddenly spiked from 0.3 NTU to 2.7 NTU. His eyes had caught the number before the alarm sounded. Seventeen years of experience. His body had learned to react faster than any alert.

"I hope it's just a malfunction."

He muttered to himself while gazing out the control room window toward the storage tank. It was pitch-black outside. This water purification plant supplied an average of 280,000 cubic meters of tap water to the city per day. Right now, somewhere, someone was turning on a faucet.

He ran to the site.

---

Between the intake pump station and the raw water intake basin. The beam of his flashlight swept across the water's surface. There was no visible abnormality. The water was still black—not because it was dawn, but because all water looks black under nighttime lighting. He didn't dip his finger into the water. There was no reason to, and he knew better than to.

He collected water from the raw water intake basin sampling port and tested it with the portable turbidimeter. 2.4 NTU. The sensor hadn't been wrong.

The water quality standard for tap water required turbidity of 0.5 NTU or less. The current reading was nearly five times that. If the coagulation and sedimentation process was working properly, this reading shouldn't be possible.

He ran toward the coagulant injection pump.

---

Coagulant pump #3 was not moving. Power was on, but the shaft wasn't rotating. Lee Ha-jun pressed his ear to the pump casing. There was no vibration sound. Whether it was an overload trip, a debris-clogged impeller, or an electrical fault didn't matter now. What mattered was what to do immediately. He increased the injection flow rate of pumps #1 and #2 by two-thirds.

As the load kicked in, the pump noise changed. Listening to that sound, he rushed back to the control room.

3:28 a.m. The turbidity readings were dropping. 2.1, 1.7, 1.3.

But it still hadn't met the standard.

The problem was time. There was no way to know how much water had already flowed from the storage tank into the city's water distribution system. He opened the display for the storage tank outlet valve control. Since it was the lowest demand period of the night, the flow rate was low—0.4 cubic meters per second. At this rate, the amount of water that had flowed out since the pump failed wasn't much. There was a possibility it hadn't yet reached consumers' faucets.

Possibility. Not certainty.

He opened the emergency contact list. City Hall Water Works Bureau night duty officer. The phone rang twice.

"This is Lee Ha-jun. Wol-am Water Plant. We have an abnormal turbidity reading. The values are recovering now, but I need your judgment on whether we should temporarily shut down the water distribution."

Through the phone, he could feel the sleep-heavy voice turning tense.

---

4:20 a.m.

The turbidity had dropped to 0.38 NTU. It was now within the standard. The emergency measure had worked. The decision to shut down distribution was not made—the night duty manager chose to hold off based on the water quality recovery trend. Lee Ha-jun didn't know if that decision was right or wrong. It wasn't his call to make.

He squatted down in front of pump #3 again. He loosened the flange bolts one by one. When he opened the casing, something was wedged between the impeller blades. It was a piece of plastic. Whether it had passed through the intake screen or was already inside the piping was unclear. It took twenty seconds to pull it out by hand.

He restarted the pump. Normal operating sounds returned.

---

5:40 a.m. Lee Ha-jun was drinking cold canned coffee while drafting an incident report. Detection of pump #3 malfunction, emergency flow rate adjustment, turbidity recovery, foreign material removal, pump restart complete. Written in order, it looked like a neat, tidy story.

But he knew better. Over these two and a half hours, not every decision he made had been right. If the remaining capacity of the coagulant pumps hadn't been enough to handle the load, if the turbidity had kept rising, if the shut-down decision had been delayed even slightly—if any one of those paths had gone differently, it would have been a news story by 6 a.m.

The sky outside was growing lighter. The city was waking up. Somewhere, someone was turning on a faucet to brew coffee, to make baby formula, to fill a cup.

Today, water just came out.

---

Pump #3's regular maintenance cycle was once every six months. Lee Ha-jun added one more line below the final line of his report.

*Intake screen mesh specification requires review.*

He had no idea if his recommendation would be accepted.
