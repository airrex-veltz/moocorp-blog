90 Days of War

July 9, 1997, Park Jun-ho stood in the conference room of Arion Electronics headquarters. Before his eyes was a company on the brink of bankruptcy. Cash remaining was $1 billion, and quarterly losses were $1 billion. The math was simple. **If Arion Electronics didn't recover within 90 days, it would disappear.**

"We have three months left," Park Jun-ho said. "We have to change everything. Right now."

The first attack was on the product lineup. Arion Electronics was making a whopping 40 models. PowerBook 1400, 2400, 3400. Power Macintosh 4400, 5500, 6500. Consumers were confused, and engineers were exhausted.

"We stop everything," Park Jun-ho drew four squares on the whiteboard. "Desktop and laptop. Professional and consumer. Four is enough."

The conference room stirred. Reduce 40 to 4? The VP of Engineering objected. "That's impossible. It takes at least a year."

"We'll do it in three months," Park Jun-ho's voice was cold. "If not, I'll find someone else."

The second attack was bolder. Park Jun-ho called his old rival Kang Tae-woo. Arion Electronics and Coresoft had been in legal battles for 10 years. But Park Jun-ho set aside his pride.

"Kang Tae-woo, I have a proposal. Doesn't Arion Electronics matter to you? If we disappear, you'll be a monopoly. The government won't stand for it."

Kang Tae-woo laughed. "Go on."

"Invest $150 million. In return, we'll use Internet Explorer as our default browser. And we'll end the patent disputes."

Kang Tae-woo was silent for 3 days. Then he answered. "Fine."

August 6, 1997, on the stage of Boston Macworld. Park Jun-ho began his presentation. On the screen appeared Kang Tae-woo's face. The audience booed. To Arion Electronics faithful, Coresoft was an enemy.

"We need to stop thinking that we can only win if Coresoft loses," Park Jun-ho said. "For Arion Electronics to win, Arion Electronics has to do really good things."

Kang Tae-woo on screen announced. "Coresoft will invest $150 million in Arion Electronics."

The booing stopped. Silence fell. Then someone clapped. The applause became cheers. Arion Electronics had survived.

The third attack was the cruelest. Park Jun-ho laid off 3,000 people. He spoke before tearful employees.

"I'll look like a villain. That's fine. But nine months from now, if this company is still alive, then you'll understand me."

90 days passed. Arion Electronics' stock price jumped from $13 to $28. Cash hemorrhaging stopped. And Park Jun-ho began preparing for the next war. Its name was iMac.

May 1998, the translucent blue iMac was revealed. It didn't look like a computer. The media mocked it. "It looks like a toy," they said.

Six months later, iMac sold 800,000 units. Arion Electronics recorded $160 million in profit in Q4 1998. It was 18 months after the bankruptcy notice.

Today in 2026, Arion Electronics' market capitalization has exceeded $3 trillion. That company with 90 days left until death in July 1997 has now become the most valuable company in the world.

Before Park Jun-ho passed away in 2011, he said this.

"Sometimes just surviving is victory. But real victory is determined by what you do next."

In the 90 Days of War, Arion Electronics survived. And in the next war, it changed the world.
