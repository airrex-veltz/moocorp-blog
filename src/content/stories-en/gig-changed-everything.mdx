Lina Choi first noticed the numbers at two in the morning on Tuesday.

The spreadsheet glowed on her screen. Red figures flickered between the white cells, and she stared at them three more times to confirm it wasn't a mistake. Her February payout was exactly 22.7 percent lower than January. Yet the number of gigs had actually increased.

"There's no other explanation than FlexRun."

She spoke to herself. Her lips moved but the sound dissolved into the quiet studio apartment. FlexRun was the app where platform workers picked up contracts. Delivery, translation, data labeling, sometimes field research. Lina mostly did translation and labeling. She managed three languages, which had kept her at a fairly high "Star Rate" badge in FlexRun's internal ranking system.

But her payout dropped. Even though she did more work.

That night she pulled data going back to January. The contract history tab in the FlexRun app allowed CSV downloads. It seemed like a feature designed without much thought, but thanks to it, Lina could create a spreadsheet of six months of contract data in an hour. Contract rate, hours, completion rate, client rating, and final payment.

The problem was in the last column.

"Final payment" differed subtly from "contract rate x hours." The error was always in the negative direction. 0.3 percent, 0.8 percent, some days up to 2 percent. Small figures, but when she summed six months, the numbers added up differently. Lina opened her calculator. Her fingers stopped.

"380,000 won."

That was a little more than her monthly transportation costs. Just for her, it was 380,000 won.

---

The next day she searched through the FlexRun community forum. At first, she just wanted to find someone who had posted a similar complaint. She found one in less than an hour. The username was "Money Mosaic," and the self-introduction said they were a translator with seven years of experience.

"Is it just me whose payout is off? The rate calculation doesn't add up."

There were 12 comments. Half said "Me too," and the other half said "Isn't that just an app bug?" The FlexRun official account left an automated message saying they'd respond within 24 hours, and that was it. The post was four days old.

Lina sent a direct message.

"I have the same problem. Did you pull the data? Could we talk?"

The reply came three hours later.

---

Money Mosaic's real name was Kan Yamil. Lina suggested they meet in person, and Kan thought about it for ten minutes before sending OK. They met face-to-face at a café near Seokchon. Kan looked younger than Lina expected. Mid-thirties, with tired-looking eyes but a firm grip on the laptop case he held tight.

As soon as Lina opened the CSV file, Kan spoke.

"It's the same."

He opened his own file beside it. The error patterns were almost identical. The lower-rate contracts had larger error percentages. Higher-rate contracts were relatively accurate.

"This isn't random bug," Lina said.

"It's an algorithm," Kan said quietly. "Either intentional or poorly designed."

The two sat in silence for a while. Kan spoke first.

"There are more people like that on the forum. I've talked to a few of them, and they all just let it go because it's a small amount. They can't prove it."

"We can prove it."

Lina pointed at the screen. The CSV data didn't lie.

---

The data they collected grew quickly. They created a private chat room within the FlexRun community. The name was "Calculator Club," and it started with 5 members but grew to 23 in ten days. Delivery riders, graphic designers, data labelers, interpreters. People making a living on FlexRun joined one by one.

Each brought a CSV. Lina wrote a Python script to standardize the format. It was basic code, but Kan refined it. Neither was a developer, but if they needed to, they could learn.

When the aggregated data came out, the room went quiet for a moment.

Total amount unpaid to 23 people over six months. Approximately 96 million won.

"That's over 4 million won average per person," Kan said.

"That's just 23 people," Lina said. "There are tens of thousands of FlexRun workers just in this country."

The chat fell silent. Everyone understood what that number implied.

---

The person who contributed most to understanding the algorithm's structure was Sophia Len.

Sophia was a statistics professor by profession and took data analysis contracts on FlexRun as a side gig. When she organized the data from 23 people, the pattern emerged clearly.

"FlexRun's internal scoring system, the 'Reliability Index,' applies an adjustment factor to the payout rate when it falls below a certain threshold. The problem is that the calculation method for this index is proprietary."

Sophia drew a formula on a whiteboard. It was a video call in the chat room, and she held a piece of paper up to the camera.

"From what I can infer, recent contract cancellation rate is a key variable. But they don't distinguish by reason for cancellation. Whether I canceled, the client canceled, or FlexRun's system error caused the cancellation—they all count the same as negative."

Lina pressed her forehead.

"So my score dropped because..."

"Do you remember when the client suddenly canceled your contract in February?"

She remembered. It was a translation contract where the client said they'd dropped the whole project and canceled within a day. It wasn't Lina's fault. But FlexRun's system had included it in Lina's cancellation rate and deducted from her Reliability Index.

"That alone caused over a 22 percent drop in my payout?" Lina asked.

"Once the index drops below the threshold, the adjustment factor increases sharply. It's non-linear," Sophia explained. "It's a cliff structure. Push it slightly and it drops dramatically."

After the meeting ended, Lina kept her screen off for a while.

Cliff. That word stayed with her.

---

The problem was how to make the evidence public.

What they had was CSV data and Sophia's statistical analysis. No one had FlexRun's internal algorithm code. To fight it legally, they'd need internal documents, and those only came after filing a lawsuit and going through the evidence disclosure process.

"There's a rumor FlexRun is planning a patch," Kan said.

That changed the room's temperature.

The rumor came from a member who had worked as a contractor inside FlexRun. They didn't reveal their name but said that the next quarterly update might include "Reliability Index calculation logic improvements."

"If they patch it, our evidence disappears," Sophia said. "Proof that this algorithm method existed."

"We have to go public now," Lina said.

Three seconds of silence.

"Where?"

---

Lina took on reaching out to media. She sent pitches to two places. One was an independent media outlet called GigWatch that consistently covered platform labor issues, and the other was a slightly larger economic newsletter.

Ray Park from GigWatch responded first.

"Can you send me the data? I'll verify it myself."

Lina sent the analysis report Sophia had prepared and anonymized CSVs from 23 people. Ray ran his own analysis for two days. Then he got back in touch.

"The pattern checks out. One more thing—did any of these people request an explanation from FlexRun? Formally?"

Lina relayed the question to the chat room. Three said they had contacted customer service in the past and only received responses saying "System processed normally." She gathered those conversation screenshots too.

Ray's third message came.

"I'm writing the story. But I need to send a query to FlexRun first. The day it runs, they might accelerate the patch. You're aware of that risk, right?"

Lina called Kan and Sophia.

"Yes," Lina typed back. "That's fine."

It might not have been fine, actually. If the patch came out, past data would remain but current damage would disappear. The structural problem's timeline would shift to past tense. Harder for media to cover. Lina did that calculation in her head, then paused, then deleted it.

All they really had was this data, right now. The things that would disappear if they didn't use them now.

---

GigWatch's article ran on Thursday.

The headline was "FlexRun Algorithm Appears to Auto-Reduce Payouts for Low-Rate Contracts." The subtitle read "23 People's Data Analysis Shows Average 17% Payment Discrepancy."

Lina watched the article link post to the chat room. Reactions from members rolled in. Kan just wrote "Done." Sophia left a comment verifying that the statistics were quoted accurately.

FlexRun's official social media account released a brief statement in the morning.

"We prioritize accuracy in our payout system. We are currently reviewing the relevant matter internally and examining improvements for greater transparency."

"Examining improvements for greater transparency," Kan read aloud. "Not an admission."

"Still, they didn't ignore it," Lina said.

---

That Friday, FlexRun deployed an app update. The Reliability Index calculation method changed. Contract cancellations were now categorized as "worker-attributed" or "non-attributed," and non-attributed cancellations no longer affected the index. The payout adjustment factor formula was also partially changed. The exact formula remained proprietary, but more explicit explanations were added to the app's help section.

FlexRun made no mention of retroactive payments.

The chat room briefly erupted then went quiet. Someone asked.

"Is that it?"

Lina thought about the answer for a while.

She hadn't recovered the payout difference. FlexRun hadn't formally admitted the algorithm error. They'd "examined" improvements and "deployed" an update, but whether that was an apology, a fix, or prevention remained unclear in official language. Ray's follow-up article covered the update and used the phrase "de facto admission," but it had no legal force.

Yet something had changed.

"Things will be different going forward." Lina typed and deleted. Too conclusive sounding.

Instead, she wrote:

"Let's compare again when next month's payout comes out."

---

Ten days later, Lina opened her March payout data.

The number of contracts was similar to February. The payout amount returned to January levels. One month was slightly worse than the difference suggested, but that was because she'd accepted a few contracts with lower client ratings. It wasn't an algorithm problem.

She closed the spreadsheet.

Outside the window, it was already dark. Evening business news played on the television, and there was some talk about a **FLEX** stock for a platform company, mentioning a short-term pullback ahead of earnings. A brief analyst comment said investors were starting to question the platform's fee structure.

Lina set the remote down at that point.

There was always a tension between platform profitability and worker payouts. Algorithms tended to be designed to hide that tension. One small adjustment factor touched the monthly income of thousands quietly, and no one might know. Most actually didn't.

Kan sent a message to the chat room.

"Should we keep collecting CSVs on a regular basis?"

Lina thought for a moment.

People came and went. Data accumulated. Sophia's script ran. It wasn't particularly grand work. When you found a strange number, you got together and looked at it again. That was all.

She typed into the chat room:

"Yes."

It was a brief reply, but a small light flickered on again in the room.
