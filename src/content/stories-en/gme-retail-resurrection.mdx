Oh Chang-su stood before the empty shelves again.

A single sheet of paper taped to the shop entrance — 'Retro Game Specialist, Chang-su's Game World' — fluttered in January's cold wind. Across the street, a convenience store window displayed a New Year's sale poster in bright colors, but Chang-su's shop held only dust-covered game cartridges and ledgers older than the dust itself. It was January 2021.

He had opened the shop in 2003. Back then, arcade halls were dying, and consumers sought places to buy and sell used console games. Chang-su filled that void and survived for eighteen years. But then streaming arrived, digital downloads became the norm, and the pandemic delivered the final blow. Fewer people even walked past his storefront anymore. Where middle schoolers once lined up on Saturday afternoons, now only spider webs remained.

The rent was three months overdue.

Chang-su sat behind the counter and opened his laptop. He was trying to calculate expenses when a news alert pierced through his screen.

'US Game Distribution Chain Surges 57% in a Single Day.'

At first, he thought he'd misread it. A game distribution chain? The dying giant he knew, facing the same fate as his own shop, had suddenly grown wings. He opened the article. A US-based offline game retailer chain, ticker symbol **Hanbyeol Game Distribution**, had been swept up in an internet community buying frenzy and tripled in value in just three days.

Chang-su stared blankly at the screen. His shop's sign reflected in the glass before him.

"Are these people insane?"

---

News spreads fast. It had started in an internet community somewhere — a forum for US stock talk. The people there called themselves 'apes,' called each other 'diamond hands,' and urged everyone not to sell Hanbyeol Game Distribution stock. They had discovered that major Wall Street hedge funds had massively shorted the company's stock. The structure was simple: if the stock fell, they profited. If the company collapsed, those giant piles of capital would win.

Seeing that, the community rallied.

The logic was straightforward. If we buy, they lose. If they lose more, they'll be forced to buy back at higher prices to cut their losses. Then the stock rises. This cycle compressed like a massive spring until it springs back all at once. They called this 'short squeeze.'

Mid-January, Hanbyeol Game Distribution was trading around 20 dollars.

By the end of January, it had surpassed 400 dollars.

Chang-su was on his second day of repeatedly opening and closing browser tabs to watch the US stock chart. Not because he wanted to see it rise, but because he wanted to understand how a shop like his own had suddenly become the center of the world. He wanted to confirm that this situation was real.

That afternoon, a customer arrived. A young man who looked about twenty-three, holding a handheld game console cartridge, approached the register.

"Do you have any Hanbyeol Game Distribution stock, sir?"

Chang-su looked at him. The young man smiled brightly.

"I bought another 500 shares yesterday. So what if I lose money? This is a movement."

The word 'movement' lingered in Chang-su's mind for a long time. Handing over the price of a game cartridge, he thought about what that meant. Buying not for profit. A battle prepared to lose. Not entering the game board with new rules to a field where Wall Street had always won, but the determination to flip the board itself.

Chang-su couldn't laugh. Though he felt he should.

---

On January 28, 2021, the situation reached its peak.

A US stock trading platform halted purchases of Hanbyeol Game Distribution. Officially, the reason was margin requirements. But the internet community was furious. Blocking retail buyers while allowing hedge funds to sell looked like blatant market manipulation. The US Congress announced hearings. Democratic and Republican lawmakers expressed outrage on the same day in the same direction — something that rarely happened.

Chang-su watched that news and retrieved an old game cartridge. A Super Famicom cartridge. The traces of having been sold and resold dozens of times remained on the stickers. Objects wear away, but they survive as they pass from hand to hand. They become someone's property, then someone else's memory, then things that seemed destined for museums. Yet here they were, still being traded.

Wasn't the offline game shop the same? Even when you thought it dead, somewhere someone still needed it.

Chang-su placed that cartridge in the most visible spot on the display shelf.

---

By February, Hanbyeol Game Distribution's stock began falling again. From 300 dollars to 100, from 100 to 50. Some in the community sold. Others held. The hedge funds suffered massive losses but didn't collapse. The world seemed to return to normal.

But it didn't go back completely.

Hanbyeol Game Distribution survived afterward. The company announced a transition to e-commerce, and the community didn't scatter — they continued circling the stock. The price rose and fell, but the short-sellers never again felt comfortable attacking the company as easily. They had learned that the 'apes' could reunite at any moment, anywhere.

Chang-su's shop survived March.

The landlord gave another month's grace, and in that time Chang-su sold five used handheld gaming consoles at once. It was thanks to the twenty-three-year-old who brought three friends. They talked about Hanbyeol Game Distribution while looking at retro game cartridges, and eventually they bought.

"It's good this shop is still here," one of them said. Chang-su didn't know how to respond. Should he say thank you, or laugh bitterly at still not having gone under?

---

Summer came. Chang-su still sat in the same spot, opening his laptop. The Hanbyeol Game Distribution stock tab was now bookmarked. Not because it meant anything, but out of habit. That company name had grown strangely familiar to him. It felt like the name of things that had faced what he faced.

The shop didn't collapse. But it wouldn't be accurate to say it survived either. Customers came sometimes, sometimes they didn't. The rent was no longer overdue, but that didn't mean there was breathing room.

Chang-su wiped down the display shelf and thought. Did those community people want to make money? Yes. Was that all? Probably not. They had an opponent they wanted to beat. An opponent that had won for a long time. An opponent that accepted their loss as inevitable.

Chang-su had an opponent too. A nameless current. A tide called digitalization. The premise that offline is old and online is new.

Hanbyeol Game Distribution had cracked that tide for a moment. Maybe it sealed back up again. Or maybe it's still open.

Chang-su took the Super Famicom cartridge from the display shelf and held it in his hand. Worn-down plastic casing. Inside, data still lived. Still playable, still sellable, still meaningful to someone.

He placed the cartridge back on the shelf.

A small sound came from the direction of the door. The entrance sensor. A customer was coming in.
