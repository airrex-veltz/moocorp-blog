The lights of the midnight factory streaked across the glass. Min-jun stood at the window of the conference room, looking down. Shinseong Semiconductor's cleanroom did not rest, even at two in the morning. Beyond the glass, technicians in contamination suits moved like ants, their figures shimmering in the distance.

In his hands were two documents. One was a strategic advanced industry support agreement from the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy. KRW 3.2 trillion. The other was a preliminary export control notice from the U.S. Department of Commerce's Industrial Security Bureau (BIS). It stated that additional review would be initiated regarding the transfer of manufacturing equipment for 14-nanometer processes and below to third countries.

The two pieces of paper pointed in exactly opposite directions.

---

Min-jun had become chairman of Shinseong Semiconductor four years ago. The company was founded by his father, but his father had never ventured into foundry operations. His work had been entirely in systems semiconductor design. When Min-jun expanded into foundry, he was called reckless. Competing against Dongbang Fab? Half the executives opposed it.

Yet he pushed through. As a result, SSMK's stock price rose 2.7 times over three years, and the company earned the title of Korea's only 7-nanometer foundry. But Min-jun knew that those numbers now constrained him in turn.

EUV—extreme ultraviolet lithography. That was the next hurdle.

2-nanometer process was impossible without EUV. EUV equipment was made by only one company in the world, a leading Dutch equipment manufacturer. Without that machine, which cost well over KRW 400 billion per unit, you could not produce the next generation of chips. Shinseong Semiconductor had already ordered three units, with two scheduled to arrive in early next year.

Then America stepped in.

The BIS export control tightening had not appeared suddenly out of nowhere. Signals had come from Washington for months. The equipment export embargo on Dongbang Fab had set a precedent, and now that scope was expanding. It was not a direct hit. But one of Shinseong Semiconductor's three EUV units was planned for transfer to a third-country manufacturing facility. That plan was now under review.

What would happen if BIS blocked the transfer after arrival? All three EUV systems would have to remain domestic, and the original strategy of overseas foundry bases would evaporate. Conversely, accepting government support would impose restrictions on overseas production transfers. Both paths were blocked.

That was why Min-jun was considering a third way.

---

The next morning, the board met. The conference room table was filled with twelve people. Min-jun raised the matter himself.

"We will not accept the government support agreement. Instead, we will raise capital through our own funds and external strategic investors. At the same time, we will keep all EUV equipment domestic and conduct a complete review of our overseas foundry plans."

Silence followed. Lee, the vice chairman, spoke first. He handled finance. A man in his early sixties with graying hair, he always spoke in numbers.

"Chairman, you're declining KRW 3.2 trillion? Do you know how much our Capex will fall short without that money?"

"I do."

"Is self-financed funding possible?"

Min-jun paused for a moment.

"It's not impossible."

Lee removed his glasses and set them on the table. That gesture was his way of expressing displeasure.

Another voice came. Kim, the director of strategic planning, someone Min-jun had personally recruited.

"You mentioned strategic investors. Which ones are you considering?"

"Two: a Middle Eastern sovereign wealth fund and a Japanese materials company. We've already made preliminary contact."

The atmosphere at the table shifted. Someone stopped taking notes and set down their pen. Min-jun read each small signal one by one.

"The BIS notice is still in the preliminary stage. It's not confirmed yet. But accepting government support will only complicate things later. We're settling one obligation before two collide."

Lee put his glasses back on.

"If that strategy fails, can the company survive?"

Min-jun did not answer directly.

"The board will decide."

---

The vote was seven to five. Seven in favor, five opposed. It passed, but only narrowly. As he left the conference room, Min-jun recalled the faces of the five who had voted against. They were not wrong. They simply saw different risks than he did.

Lee caught up with him in the hallway.

"Chairman."

Min-jun stopped walking.

"I opposed it."

"I know."

Lee started to say something, then closed his mouth. Something passed between them in that silence. It was difficult to explain.

"I hope it works out."

That was all. Min-jun nodded and kept walking.

---

Four days later, Min-jun called the deputy minister at the Trade Ministry directly. It was a notice that he would return the agreement. The deputy minister's surprise was evident.

"Chairman, may I ask why?"

"The regulatory environment is too complex. I decided that moving freely right now was the right call."

"You can reapply next year."

"Thank you."

After hanging up, Min-jun held the receiver in his hand for a while. Outside the window, Seoul's afternoon flowed by without concern.

Contact with the Middle East was ongoing. Nothing had been concluded yet. The Japanese materials company was still in the operational discussion stage. Shinseong Semiconductor now floated suspended in midair, having let go of one thing and not yet grasped the other.

Min-jun knew that feeling well. It was not the first time. It had been the same when he switched to foundry. You had to release what you held to grasp what came next. But that moment in between always felt like this. That instant when it seemed like you held nothing.

---

That night, Min-jun looked down at the factory again. The lights remained. The cleanroom did not stop. When the EUV equipment arrived, it would need space inside. For that machine to operate, hundreds of skilled technicians would be needed. They were already there.

Min-jun knew they did not know of his decision. They did not need to. Their work was to etch wafers, and his work was to ensure they could continue that work.

There was no other reason.

No one yet knew what tomorrow would bring for SSMK. Middle Eastern funding might come through, or it might not. The BIS notice might harden into confirmed sanctions. If that happened, Lee's concerns would become reality.

Min-jun rested his forehead lightly against the glass. Cold touched his brow.

The next EUV equipment delivery was three months away. A conclusion would be reached before then. Or a longer story would begin after that.

Either way, the decision made tonight was already final.
