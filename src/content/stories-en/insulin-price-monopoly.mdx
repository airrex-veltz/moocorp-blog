A Mother's Calculation

Eun-hee Oh's heart would sink every time she handed over a prescription at the pharmacy counter. One month's supply of insulin cost 420,000 won. Money that disappeared at the pharmacy counter instead of a birthday gift for her child, instead of her child's meals, instead of her child's school supplies.

Her child had been diagnosed with Type 1 diabetes when she was eight years old. An autoimmune disease. It wasn't the child's fault, nor was it Eun-hee's fault. But the responsibility fell on Eun-hee's shoulders. Every time she picked up a prescription each month.

At first, insurance covered 70 percent. The remaining 30 percent was still burdensome, but Eun-hee thought, what could she do? This was reality, and mothers lived within this reality. But this year, the insurance policy changed. The cost burden dropped to 50 percent. The government called it "medication cost efficiency." From a mother's perspective, it was simply half more money slipping away.

Eun-hee worked as a nurse. Her monthly salary was 3.5 million won. Rent was 700,000 won, maintenance fees 150,000 won, phone and electricity and water bills 150,000 won. Her child's school supplies, clothes, snacks, and tutoring fees. The insulin pen. Blood glucose test strips. Insulin pump batteries. Breathing cost money. Living cost money. Loving her child cost money.

So the mother began her calculations.

It took a month or two to determine exactly how much insulin was needed each month. The required dosage varied based on her child's height, weight, and activity level. The doctor said to keep a buffer. There could be emergencies. But Eun-hee thought differently. A buffer was the next month's savings.

Eun-hee divided a month into ten bundles of three days each. She divided the insulin into ten portions. Every Monday, she would place only the insulin needed for that week in a small freezer. The rest waited in a small insulated box that maintained a constant temperature like a pharmacy refrigerator. When her child used it, Eun-hee was strict about using only the prescribed amount. The child followed whatever her mother said. The child learned to adjust her body to her mother's calculations.

Doctor visits were also planned. Once every three months, officially checking her HbA1c levels. Two weeks before each visit, Eun-hee administered insulin more carefully. To make the blood sugar levels look good. The doctor smiled at Eun-hee. "You manage it so well." Eun-hee smiled back. She felt the moment a lie left her lips, but she looked at her child. Her child was alive, going to school, sleeping well at night.

She didn't forge blood glucose readings in the medical records. A record wasn't a record itself, but a choice. Which time of day's blood glucose to record, what diet to prepare. The doctor didn't know this, and couldn't know it. A mother's world existed outside the doctor's office.

But the world outside the office was slowly cracking.

When the child turned thirteen, her insulin requirement increased. She was in her growth phase. Her child's body demanded more insulin. Eun-hee's calculation system began to break down. Dividing into three-day portions became impossible. There were days when even three days felt unbearable.

That winter, the child experienced low blood sugar shock for the first time. At 2 a.m., Eun-hee woke to her child's crying. Her fingers were numb, drool running from her mouth. When Eun-hee checked the blood glucose meter, it read 47. Less than one-third of normal. Eun-hee quickly fed her child glucose and rushed to the emergency room. Her child cried. "Mom, I'm scared."

The doctor said to increase the dosage. To adjust the insulin plan. Eun-hee listened to the doctor. And she began her calculations again.

This time it was more rigid. Colder. Not ten portions but fifteen. Dividing into week-and-a-half portions, but separating the days her child went to school from the days she stayed home. She also regularized meal portions. Her child ate only the food her mother prepared—rice and soup. She rejected the cake at a friend's birthday party "for blood sugar management." She skipped snacks sold at school field trips. The ice cream her grandmother bought for her.

By the time the child turned fifteen, she had begun to resent her mother.

The child would turn her eyes away when she saw her mother leaving the pharmacy. Every time her mother took out the insulin pen, she would sigh, "Do I have to get another shot?" Every time her mother checked the diet, she asked, "Is this really all there is?" Her mother's love was like an insulin needle. Necessary, but painful, drawing blood, needing to be inserted again every day.

One night, Eun-hee watched her sleeping child. Thin arms, darkened injection marks, legs that twitched occasionally. This was her child's life. This was what it looked like for her child to live within her mother's calculations.

Eun-hee cried. But when morning came, she went to the pharmacy again. She began her calculations again. This month: 420,000 won. Next month: 450,000 won. The month after: 480,000 won. Insulin prices kept rising. The government didn't control prices. The pharmaceutical company kept raising them. Her mother's salary was set again this year.

Eun-hee came to understand. This was no longer a matter of calculation. This was a problem of a world where a child's survival became the subject of a cost-benefit analysis. Without insulin, her child would die. If insulin was too expensive, her mother would go into debt. Mother and child were game pieces on the pharmaceutical company's board.

Eun-hee signed a petition for policy change. She wrote a letter to a member of parliament. She posted her story on a medical forum. Nothing changed. The world kept turning.

So Eun-hee calculated again. Her child had to survive, her mother had to endure, and insulin would keep getting more expensive. This was reality. Whether her child resented her or not, whether Eun-hee cried or not.

A mother's calculation continues. It will continue for as long as her child is alive.
