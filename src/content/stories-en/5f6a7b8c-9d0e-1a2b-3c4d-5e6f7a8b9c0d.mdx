The record starts without fail at six in the morning.

Tank number 3, COD level, flow rate, color. I've been filling in the same form for fifteen years. At first, I did it because it was my assigned duty; in the middle, because it had become a habit; now, it's hard to explain why. Perhaps it's because I have a feeling that the water is telling me something.

Upstream there is an industrial complex. One electroplating factory, three textile dyeing factories, and several smaller chemical plants. I don't know most of their names. But I read in numbers what they use and how much—or what they don't use.

In the autumn of 2019, I first noticed something odd.

The characteristic yellow hue that had always appeared in the textile dyeing wastewater was gradually fading. At first, I blamed the season. Then I thought the production process had changed. But this change didn't come back when winter passed. By spring, the color had completely vanished. Instead of going upstream to investigate, I just kept entering numbers in the form.

Early the next year, the news reported that several textile factories in that industrial complex had relocated their production bases to Southeast Asia. The article said the decision was made "in the second half of last year."

I pulled out the form and checked the date. The color had first begun to fade in July of that year.

The water had known first.

---

After that, I began reading the form differently.

There's a pattern where the heavy metal levels from the electroplating factory spike only during weekday afternoons. On weekends, they nearly return to baseline. But this year, that pattern has begun to break. Now the spike comes only on Tuesdays and Wednesdays, while the other days are quiet like weekends.

I cannot yet say for certain what this means. It could be shortened operating days, a seasonal dip in orders, or perhaps the beginning of something larger.

The job of the water treatment plant is to make water clean. That's all I'm supposed to do. But when you spend fifteen years staring at the same numbers, you learn that the data arrives before the economic news. Water changes from the moment a factory decides to stop. Months before the relocation announcement, before the article hits the news.

I've never told anyone about this. I simply write it down in the form and keep it on file.

This morning too, I stood before tank number 3 at six o'clock. The electroplating readings are the same as yesterday. The pattern needs more time to reveal itself. Water has no hurry. Neither do I.

Write it down and someday it makes sense. That's almost the only thing I've learned in fifteen years of this work.
