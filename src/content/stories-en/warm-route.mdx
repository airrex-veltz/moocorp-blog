The Chook-Bit-ho is an eleven-year-old container ship. Sailing under Hang-ro Marine, it has always plied the same route from Hamburg to Singapore. People called that route the Northern Passage—a journey across the Mediterranean through Suez, down the Red Sea, and across the Gulf of Aden. It was the shortest, and most crowded, route in the world.

It would be hard to say the Chook-Bit-ho liked the route. More than affection, it was a sense of familiarity. The heat above the Red Sea, the long queues waiting in the Suez Canal, the occasional squalls that swept through the Gulf of Aden—these were inconveniences, but they were predictable. After twelve voyages, the Chook-Bit-ho had memorized the wave heights and wind directions at each point. A predictable world, though grueling, was safe.

In mid-January 2024, that world shook.

Ships began coming under attack at the gateway to the Red Sea, one after another. The Chook-Bit-ho did not learn the full story—it only knew that departure clearance from Hamburg kept being delayed. The message from the operations department was brief. Wait. Political reasons.

The Chook-Bit-ho did not know what politics were. Neither did the refrigerators in the cargo hold, nor the boxes of semiconductor equipment.

Four days later, Hang-ro Marine's operations department made a decision. A new route: south around the African continent through Cape of Good Hope. The distance would increase by more than 6,500 nautical miles, and the journey would take more than eleven additional days. The cost calculations came immediately—fuel costs, port waiting charges, delay compensation to shippers. Hang-ro Marine's stock fell 8 percent that week. The Chook-Bit-ho did not know that number directly, but it knew the captain's face had hardened.

The departure occurred in the third week of January. The first several days were simply long. Long as predicted, and expensive as predicted. The waves in the middle of the Atlantic were rougher than those in the Red Sea, and the heat near the equator raised engine room temperatures by three degrees.

But then came the passage around Cape of Good Hope.

At latitude 34 degrees south, where the Atlantic and Indian Oceans meet. A place the Northern Passage never visited. It was there that the Chook-Bit-ho encountered westerly winds for the first time. The winds on the Northern Passage were always head-on or diagonal—resistance or indifference. The winds at Cape of Good Hope were different. They came steady and even from the side of the hull, with a long, sustained breath.

The fuel gauge began dropping gradually. Consumption started falling below the anticipated rate.

On the tenth night sailing north along the Namibian coast, whales appeared on deck. Blue whales—a species that does not live at the latitudes of the Northern Passage. The first mate, alone on the bridge, forgot to turn on the radio and watched for a long time. The Chook-Bit-ho had never seen him remain idle for so long. In thirteen years of sailing, he was always doing something.

Twenty-two days after leaving Hamburg, Singapore port authority sent berthing clearance. An arrival eleven days late. The cargo was safe, and fuel consumption was 7 percent less than projected. Hang-ro Marine's operations department carefully recorded that figure on screen—notes for the next voyage.

While unloading cargo at Singapore port, the Chook-Bit-ho tried to consider what it meant. Eleven days of cost. Seven percent of fuel. The time one navigator spent forgetting his radio.

None of these three seemed to be calculated yet to determine which was greater.

The Northern Passage remained closed during those days.
