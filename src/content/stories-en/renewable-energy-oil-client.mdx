Hatgyeol Energy welcomed its thirty-first winter in the company's lobby. By human years, it was the age of fresh adulthood. While the solar panels on the rooftop lay buried under snow, she waited on a sofa on the first floor of Namryeong Chemical's headquarters for three hours.

Namryeong Chemical was an old name from the Yeocheon industrial complex. It was the company that boiled naphtha and broke it apart to create the roots of plastic, and its smokestacks breathed white vapors year-round. Hatgyeol didn't dislike that breath. To her, it smelled like the sweat of working people. She simply had never said it aloud. For a long time in the industry, the two companies had pretended not to see each other. One said it would make the sky clear, and the other made its living from the sky.

"The CEO is coming down now."

At the secretary's words, she straightened her collar. The person stepping out of the elevator was Seo Jung-hun. He was the head of sustainability management at Namryeong Chemical, and he was the one who had raised the matter first. Instead of shaking hands, he set down a folder of documents.

"Starting this year, customers on the European side won't place orders without carbon documentation. What used to be called 'carbon border adjustment' is now an invoice, not just talk. Carbon credit prices rise every year, and the portion we used to receive for free keeps shrinking. We're not calling you because we've become virtuous. To be honest with you, that's the situation."

"I know." Hatgyeol laughed. "I haven't met many people who call you because they've become virtuous."

He looked at her for a moment. His eyes held no mockery.

The core of the contract was simple: installing solar panels on idle rooftops and over the water surface of a reservoir across the factory grounds—hundreds of thousands of square meters in total—with Namryeong Chemical buying the electricity for twenty years. But beneath the numbers lay things that weren't numbers. Hatgyeol's board opposed it, saying, "Aren't we becoming an absolution for fossil fuels?" Namryeong Chemical's union opposed it, asking, "Why would we bring in a partner that steals our jobs?" To both sides, this union looked like betrayal.

Negotiations continued throughout the fall. Seo Jung-hun and Hatgyeol frequently sat across from each other in a shabby restaurant in Yeocheon. They haggled over unit prices with braised cutlassfish, debated who would bear the cost of output fluctuations. The factory couldn't stop on sunless days, and Hatgyeol couldn't promise the sun. The role of storage facilities to fill that gap remained until the very end.

One evening, he set down his soju glass and spoke.

"My father was a first-generation worker at this factory. The smokestack smoke was his salary. But what I do is reduce that smoke. I don't know what Father would say if he saw it."

"I'm against it," Hatgyeol said quietly. "I don't hope that someone comes out of shame. I want to think of it as placing a roof on what your father built. The sun sits on top of it."

Seo Jung-hun looked out the window for a long time without answering. The lights of the industrial complex wavered over the sea. That day, the two parted for the first time without discussing the contract.

As winter deepened, the market reacted first. On the day of the announcement, Hatgyeol's stock price surged sharply, then retraced halfway within three days. Analysts were divided. Some said, "the risk of relying on a single customer," while others said, "the smokestack industry is the largest market." She heard the numbers arguing about her. Neither praise nor blame was actually directed at her. She found that easier somehow.

The groundbreaking ceremony was unusually clear. The union chairman of Namryeong Chemical stood at the front row with his arms crossed. Seo Jung-hun approached him first and brought up a proposal to prioritize hiring workers from the factory for rooftop installation and maintenance. It was a clause his board had approved only the night before the final day. The chairman said nothing for a while, then nodded.

"Let's see how it goes."

That was enough. There were no cheers. The loudest sound that day was the crane lifting the first bundle of panels.

In the evening, Hatgyeol stood on the still-empty rooftop, sitting next to Seo Jung-hun. The sun descended beyond the industrial complex, and the white breath of the smokestacks turned orange. For the first time, Hatgyeol thought the light was beautiful, and she didn't hide that thought.

"Twenty years," she said. "Will I still be here then?"

"I'll probably be retired by then," he laughed. "But strangely, no matter what we do in the meantime, I feel like this roof will still be here."

Instead of answering, she placed her palm on the rooftop. The warmth accumulated during the day still remained. That warmth would soon flow through the wiring into the factory, starting its work beside the flames boiling naphtha. One sought to make the sky clear, the other made its living from the sky, yet the same electricity flowed through the same factory.

Months later, Namryeong Chemical's first quarterly report listed reduced emissions in one line. Hatgyeol's report listed twenty years of revenue in one line. Both lines were never called love anywhere. Neither had changed the other, they had only filled one column of each other's ledger.

Still, Seo Jung-hun sometimes climbed to the rooftop and gazed for a long time in the direction of the sunset. Hatgyeol now knew the time he would come up. The hour when the sun declined, when the panels drew in the last light. Neither of them ever asked whether it was love or a transaction.
