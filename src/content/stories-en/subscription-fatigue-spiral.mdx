There is a ritual that arrives without fail each month: opening the credit card statement.

Jin-woo scrolled down the screen slowly. A coffee, lunch, convenience store. He remembered those. But from the next line on, problems began. "Narustream monthly subscription." Last month, he'd opened the home screen just once before falling asleep, couldn't decide what to watch, and fell asleep. Below that was "Sound Garden Premium"—a service he'd upgraded at the start of the year because he planned to use it on his commute, forgetting by then that he'd started walking instead of taking the bus two years ago.

The statement continued. "Bookworm Club Standard." The logic was simple: read one book per month and break even. He'd never broken even in a single month. "Fitness Plaza annual pass, monthly installment." It was a New Year's resolution from last year. Since it was an annual pass, he couldn't cancel. The gym was a twenty-minute walk from his place, and he'd been there three times. Three times was a very expensive session per visit.

He added it all up. The number appeared. Jin-woo sat frozen for a moment.

In truth, this situation wasn't unique to Jin-woo. One statistic repeatedly surfaces in various consumer surveys: people estimate the number of subscriptions they're using at less than half the actual number. They think they have four or five when they're actually paying for ten. The rest are quietly disappearing from the statement each month.

The platforms know this. Or more accurately, they engineered it.

Take "Narustream" as an example. The sign-up button is large and centered on the home screen, while the cancel button is buried in the settings, then the settings within settings. Once you click cancel, a popup appears: "Wait, we'll give you a free month." If you don't waver and click "cancel anyway," it shows five thumbnails and says, "These titles are only available here." One looks interesting. That evening, Jin-woo watched it. It was good. Cancellation got postponed to next month.

"Sound Garden" took a more classic approach. After you request cancellation, three days later an email arrives: "We made a playlist we think you'll love." And that playlist genuinely matched his taste. Jin-woo hesitated. The algorithm knew him so well—was it right to stop? Another month passed.

Jin-woo looked at the statement again. This time he squinted, reviewing the numbers without emotion. As if he were looking at someone else's bill. It looked different that way. These numbers were all additions of "things I'll watch someday," "things I'll read someday," "workouts I'll do someday." A bill not to his current self but to his future self every month. That future Jin-woo would be someone who enjoyed reading, exercised, had excellent music taste. And the present Jin-woo was paying in his place until that future version materialized.

Jin-woo searched for a cancellation app. There was something called a subscription management app. 4.7 stars. He downloaded it. Once he set it up, a notification came: "To optimize your subscriptions, try our premium plan."

It was a monthly fee.

Jin-woo closed the app. He looked out the window for a moment, then opened the refrigerator. Inside were two unopened meal kits that had been delivered from a subscription service. Their expiration date was tomorrow.

He hadn't decided what to do. But one of those meal kits would probably need to be opened tonight.
