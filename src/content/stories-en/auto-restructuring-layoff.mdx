Jung Manho opened his eyes at five-thirty in the morning.

It was before the alarm sounded. His body remembered. For thirty years he had woken at this hour, and today was the day he would have no reason to wake at all.

As he lifted the blanket, the streetlight outside the window leaked through the curtains. His wife was still asleep. Jung Manho walked on his tiptoes to avoid making a sound. He stood before the sink and looked in the mirror. A man with white hair looked back. When had that happened, he wondered, then stopped. Such calculations did not suit a day like this.

On the walk to the bus stop, frost lay on the ground. Though he knew it was still early for frost on this late September dawn, to Jung Manho it looked like frost. It was probably just dew. But his feet moved carefully.

Bus No. 27 came on time, as it always did. When the door opened, he caught something like the familiar smell of oil at the tip of his nose. No, that wasn't the smell of the bus. It was the smell soaked into his work clothes. The smell of Hanbit Auto Tech factory that would not fade even after washing. The scent of cutting fluid, metal dust, and oil mixed together.

He sat and looked out the window. The city was not yet awake. One convenience store with its lights on, one traffic light, and somewhere a garbage truck rushing off. When he first took this bus thirty years ago, it was also a morning like this. He had been twenty-eight years old. Two years after discharge from the military, and he could not be more grateful for the factory job.

When the factory doors opened, Park Kidong was already there.

"You're here."

Park Kidong said only that one sentence. Jung Manho understood that one sentence was enough. The two walked side by side into the changing room. Locker numbers 37 and 38. Jung Manho was 37, Park Kidong was 38. It had not changed once since 1998.

As he changed into his work clothes, Jung Manho felt his hands tremble. He could not tell whether his hands trembled because he had become an age where they trembled, or whether today made them do so. As he tied his safety shoes, he looked down at his feet. The cement floor bore the worn imprints of shoes. Someone's thirty years, someone's twenty years, the accumulated time of dozens of people must be pressed into that floor.

When he entered the production line, he saw the machines.

Line Six of Hanbit Auto Tech was a piston ring specialty line. It was where Jung Manho was first assigned in 1995. It was the part that made internal combustion engines breathe. When a car moves, this ring moves. For thirty years he had been proud that parts he made were running somewhere across the country. He had never spoken of it aloud.

The world has changed these days. From around 2024, as domestic automakers accelerated their shift to electric vehicles, demand for internal combustion engine components fell rapidly. Electric vehicles have no piston rings. They have no exhaust systems either. Parts suppliers that had endured for decades closed one by one. Hanbit Auto Tech was not the last. But today was the day this place would close.

Ryu Sunghee came over.

"Mr. Manho, I brought you coffee."

Ryu Sunghee had worked at this factory for fifteen years. Of the colleagues Jung Manho knew, she was the fastest worker and the quietest laugher. Her hands as she handed over the paper cup were red. She must have come early and cleaned the machines.

"Thank you."

The coffee was from a vending machine. This machine too was seeing its last day. That vending machine that made a clattering sound when you inserted a coin and the cup came out. Jung Manho thought he would never hear that sound again.

The whole morning continued with finishing work. They removed materials from the machines, wiped down the equipment, and returned the tools. As he organized his tool box, Jung Manho found a jig he had made himself. It was something he had devised in 2009 when there was talk that line efficiency was falling. The factory manager had praised him then. The jig bore the dark marks of his hands.

There was no one to ask if he could take it with him. He just put it in his pocket.

At lunch, everyone gathered in one place. It was a boxed lunch prepared by the management team. No one ate much. The conversation did not flow right. Was it because the years they had worked together were too long, or because today did not feel real? Or was it because it felt too real?

Park Kidong suggested they have a drink.

"This evening."

Jung Manho nodded. Ryu Sunghee nodded too. No other words were needed.

At three in the afternoon, the final official line operation began.

It was the final batch of piston rings. They would not actually be shipped, but someone had decided. Rather than just stop, let's run it one last time. Jung Manho took his place. The machine awakened. The vibration traveled up through his feet.

It was the same vibration as thirty years ago.

Processing began, and rings were made one by one. The sound of metal being cut rang out. Cutting fluid sprayed out. Jung Manho checked the gauges, inspected for defects, controlled the flow. His body moved on its own. His hands went before his mind thought. Thirty years had made him that way.

When the line stopped, Jung Manho thought he would not cry. And indeed he did not. He simply placed his hand on the machine and stood for a moment. Cold metal. In summer it was hot, and in winter it was like this.

He received his discharge papers. He stamped them. He emptied his locker. He placed his safety shoes in the return basket. As he left the factory doors for the last time, Jung Manho decided not to look back. But halfway through the parking lot, his feet stopped, and his body turned first.

It was a large building. The sign reading "Hanbit Auto Tech" was still on the roof. Several windows were already dark. The lights had been turned off.

Park Kidong came and stood beside him.

"Let's go."

"Yeah."

They walked side by side. Toward the bus stop. The sun had not yet set, so the sky was orange. Jung Manho put his hand in his pocket and felt the jig inside. The metal was cold to his fingertips.

What he will do next, he does not yet know. He is fifty-eight years old, and he knows there is nowhere for an internal combustion engine components technician to go. There is a severance package, there is his wife, and his two children are already grown. He knows those things exist.

But when his eyes open tomorrow at five-thirty in the morning, what should he rise toward? That did not exist yet.

A bus came. It was not No. 27. It was a different number. A bus he did not know where it went, but Park Kidong got on first, and Jung Manho got on too. He paid his fare and sat down, and streetlights outside the window began to come on one by one.

The jig in his pocket was still cold.
