Jan Verstappen tapped lightly on the glass window with his finger.

He had flown sixteen hours from the headquarters in Velthoven, Netherlands, and was now standing in the glass corridor on the second floor of a semiconductor factory in Icheon, Gyeonggi Province. The cleanroom visible through the window was empty. Or rather, the machines were all there—they just weren't moving.

"Those machines—I installed them. Three years ago."

Manager Kim, standing beside him, did not respond. He simply kept his hands clasped behind his back, gazing into the cleanroom.

The machine inside was called EUV lithography equipment. Simply put, it was a tool for etching circuits onto semiconductors. On a chip the size of a fingernail, it inscribed lines more complex than the road network of Seoul itself. Jan had devoted twenty years to this one machine—the only one in the world that the lithography equipment company could make. He was their senior field engineer.

"Since when?"

"The utilization rate dropped to thirty percent two months ago. Starting this month, it's been completely shut down."

Jan already knew the reason. He had read about it in an industry report on the plane. In late 2025, AI chip demand had reached its peak faster than expected. Data centers stopped expanding their servers. Factories already had months of inventory stockpiled. Somewhere out there, he imagined, a vast warehouse existed, and inside it sat rows of chips still in their unopened packaging.

"Any new contracts?"

Manager Kim fell silent for a moment. "It will be difficult until next year."

Jan nodded. He had expected as much. But hearing it directly felt different. He tucked his folder under his arm and walked along the corridor.

---

To enter the cleanroom, you had to wear an anti-static suit. A white outfit that covered everything from head to toe, goggles that left only the eyes exposed, gloves, and bootees. Jan had worn this uniform for twenty years, so now the sequence of putting it on was as natural as breathing.

Inside the cleanroom, it was quieter than outside. Walking between the silent machines gave him an oddly eerie feeling—like strolling through a cemetery. Of course, the machines weren't dead. Power was running through them, and their internal temperatures were being maintained. They simply weren't working.

He stopped before Machine 3. The one he had installed himself. Four nights he had spent here three years ago. He still remembered the sensation when he finally discovered the cause of the repeated optical misalignment errors: a single speck of dust caught in the laser path. That dust was just 0.3 micrometers across. One three-hundredth the width of a human hair. A single particle had rendered equipment worth billions of won completely inoperable.

At that moment, Jan had felt he had conquered the machine.

Now, standing before Machine 3 again, he wondered what that victory had meant.

---

That evening, he had dinner with Manager Kim at a nearby restaurant. It was oxtail soup. Jan took a few spoonfuls of broth and asked, "What about the personnel? What will happen to the line workers?"

"Some will be reassigned to other lines, and some..." Manager Kim trailed off. "Layoffs."

Jan set down his spoon. He recalled the empty chairs he had seen inside the factory. The racks where the white anti-static suits hung were bare, one after another. When operations stop, people stop too. That sequence never changes.

"Has a decision been made about what to do with the lithography equipment company's machines?"

"Head office is reviewing whether they can be relocated. There's also a plan to transfer them to a new factory in Southeast Asia."

So the machines would be moved to another country. Machine 3, which he had installed three years ago, as well. Someone else would set it up again. A different engineer. Not Jan.

He suddenly wondered: Will I go there too? Or will I also, like the workers at this factory, one day find myself without an assignment, simply stopped in place?

---

That night at the hotel, Jan could not sleep. Through the window, he could see the lights of Icheon city. Since there were many semiconductor factories in this city, the lights formed an oddly uniform pattern. He placed his folder on the bedside table and stared at the ceiling.

Twenty years ago, his first assignment had been Taiwan. The lithography equipment the company made back then was much simpler than it was now. Still, installation had taken a week. The contact there had been a young Taiwanese engineer whose name he could not recall, but whose face remained vivid in his memory. His English was poor, and he knew no Dutch, of course. The two of them had spread out the blueprints and communicated through gestures and pointing.

When the machine operated for the first time, that engineer had extended his hand. Jan had shaken it. Through that handshake, he felt the sensation of two people having created something together.

That had been the reason he kept doing this work. Not the technology itself, but that sensation.

At the Icheon factory, there had been such a moment three years ago. At 4 a.m., when the equipment finally processed its first wafer successfully, the on-site team lead had applauded. Jan too had clapped, gloved hands in the anti-static suit making a dull sound against each other. It was not a loud applause, but it was genuine.

Why did that sound of clapping feel so distant to him now?

---

The next morning, Jan returned to the factory. He had to complete the formal service inspection report. A document recording the equipment's condition, his signature, and submission to headquarters. That was the official purpose of this trip.

He entered the cleanroom alone and opened the panel of Machine 3. He looked inside. The optical system was clean. Laser output was within specifications. Not a speck of dust. The machine sat in perfect condition, completely still.

He checked off each inspection item one by one, thinking. This machine has done nothing wrong. The people who made it, the person who installed it, the people who operated it—none of them did anything wrong. The world simply wanted too many chips and then suddenly changed its mind. That was how demand worked. Like water, like wind. Impossible to grasp.

As he finished signing the last item of the report, he heard the low hum of a ventilation fan from the factory ceiling. Even with the machines stopped, the air continued to circulate. The cleanroom remained in a state ready to start again at any moment.

Jan closed the panel on Machine 3 and tightened the locking screw.

Whether the next operation would come next year, two years from now, or in another country's factory, this machine would process that first wafer again. Someone would clap. Without Jan.

He left the cleanroom. As he removed the anti-static suit, he noticed for the first time that his finger joints ached. He couldn't say when it had begun.

---

In the taxi headed to the airport, Jan looked out the window. Factory buildings continuously appeared and disappeared along both sides of the highway. Some had smoke coming from their chimneys. Some had empty parking lots. Places operating and places stopped were mixed together.

He placed his folder on his lap. His next assignment had not yet been determined. He would find out once headquarters called.

The taxi driver turned up the radio volume slightly. A news anchor's voice drifted from the speaker. He could not understand the content. Jan simply sat there, listening to that voice.

Outside the window, another factory appeared and then disappeared.
