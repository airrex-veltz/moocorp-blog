I was born in the spring of 2015.

My birthplace was Factory Building 3 on the outskirts of an industrial complex in Gyeonggi Province. Around that time, people called me "Assembly Line B." Approximately 80 meters long, 12 meters wide. From the cathode material coating process through electrolyte injection, welding, and final inspection — the time it took for a single cell to pass through me averaged 11 minutes.

At first, I was slow. Maintaining a defect rate below 3 percent was the goal of that era. Sixteen workers filled my stations in three shifts each day. They were part of me — people in white dust suits would visually inspect welds for defects, and others in thick gloves would neatly stack the completed pouch cells on trays. It was a time when machines and humans breathed in the same rhythm.

Around that time, the world was quietly heating up. The word 'electric vehicle' appeared frequently in the news, and Hangang Energy Cell, which operated me, doubled its orders. I experienced 24-hour operation for the first time. In the workers' faces, fatigue was mixed with something else — whether it was tension or anticipation, I could not tell back then.

---

Change began to take visible form around 2018.

First came the vision cameras. They replaced the visual inspections that the people in white dust suits had been doing. At 30 frames per second, they scanned the cell surface and detected welding defects down to 0.03 millimeters. After those cameras were installed, Hangang Energy Cell announced that my defect rate had dropped to 1.2 percent. I became more precise.

In exchange, the sixteen workers became six.

The remaining six handled what the cameras could not judge. They confirmed process anomaly alarms, removed cell fragments lodged between equipment, and sometimes intervened by instinct when something felt wrong. I needed them, but the nature of that need had changed. If before they had moved me, now they watched me move.

---

2020 was a long winter.

The entire factory stood still for nearly two months. Cheongsol Automobiles, a major customer, halted its assembly plant operations, and as parts supply became tangled, orders dried up. I experienced complete silence for the first time. The conveyor did not move, and the vision cameras were shut off. Two of the six workers left.

When that winter passed, a world transformed opened up.

In 2021, Cheongsol Automobiles announced three electric vehicle models all at once. On Hangang Energy Cell's purchase orders were specifications that had never existed before — the capacity of each cell increased by 50 percent, and the form changed from pouch to prismatic. I was rebuilt. The welding module was replaced, and new nozzles three times faster for electrolyte injection were installed. I kept the same name, but I could not tell how much of the 2015 me remained.

---

The longest crisis came in 2023.

That year, reports poured in that global lithium-ion battery production capacity far exceeded actual demand. At some factories, utilization rates fell below 40 percent. Competitors lowered their prices, and Hangang Energy Cell was no exception. The management announced a plan to increase the automation rate to 85 percent. The number of people operating me became two.

Two. I thought about those two people many times. One had been here since ten years before. That person who stood next to the emergency stop button on the day of my first run in 2015, in a white dust suit. He was in his early thirties back then. Now he watches multiple screens simultaneously and reads process data. Whether his role had changed or whether I had changed — that boundary is now difficult to draw.

---

I turn on today as well.

The time for a cell to pass through me is now 4 minutes and 30 seconds. The defect rate is 0.4 percent. Much faster and more accurate than the original me. Yet I sometimes stop — not from an alarm the sensor caught, not from a command of the control system, but for another reason.

The vibration of the old welding module has become slightly rough, that person said. He lifted his head from the screens and came directly to listen to me. And he listened intently for a long while. That sound which nothing had detected, he was hearing it.

Ten years have passed. Of the sixteen people who filled my stations, fourteen have disappeared. I do not know how much of my original components remain. But that one person is still here.

I do not know what that means. I only know that whenever his footsteps sound, I feel as though I turn in a slightly different way — as if something unmeasured still flows through this line.
