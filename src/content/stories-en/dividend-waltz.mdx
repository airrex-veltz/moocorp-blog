The afternoon when spring earnings season wound down, the exchange waiting room held only the hum of air conditioning and the buzz of fluorescent lights.

A woman sat on a corner bench, knitting needles moving steadily. Her suit was gray, her silver hair neatly combed back. A gold watch on her wrist. Nothing else adorned her. A name tag hung on her chest: **DUK**.

Duke was born in 1904. More precisely, she was the embodiment of a long-established utility stock that began as a power plant in a small North Carolina town and now supplied electricity across the entire American Southeast. People called her boring. Safe. The kind you bought for dividends. They weren't wrong. She paid out cash every quarter, four times a year, like clockwork. Through the 1973 oil shock, the 2008 financial crisis, when the pandemic froze the world. Never once did she miss.

That consistency had saved her life. And simultaneously isolated her.

---

The door opened with a commotion. A young man came in wearing a jacket so shiny it hurt to look at. He swept his gaze around the room, then stood by the window, staring at the ticker. His name tag read 'ARV'—a stock of a fledgling clean tech startup. Still unprofitable, but brimming with hope.

"Excuse me."

He turned toward Duke.

"How long have you been waiting here?"

"A long time." She didn't stop knitting. "Almost always."

"When does the dividend come out?"

"Every quarter. Haven't missed once in the last fifty years."

"Fifty years?" ARV let out a low whistle. It was a sound mixing wonder with mockery. "Doesn't that get boring?"

Duke looked down at her knitting needles. The yarn interlocked, stitch after stitch. Flawlessly.

"Boredom brought me this far."

ARV tried to suppress his laughter and failed. He turned back to the window. His stock price flickered on the ticker. Up seven percent from this morning. He breathed in with satisfaction watching it.

---

Before that afternoon was over, things changed.

It was the interest rate decision announcement. Mid-2024, the Federal Reserve signaled it would cut rates later than expected, and by less. Growth stocks immediately trembled. ARV's price plummeted eighteen percent in forty minutes. He stood frozen, unable to look away from the ticker. The shine on his jacket didn't fade, but his shoulders slumped.

Duke was watching.

It took a while for ARV to sit down on the bench. He rested both hands on his knees and looked up at the ceiling. He said nothing.

"First time?" Duke spoke first.

"No." After a pause. "Third time."

"Then you know. You know what this feels like."

"What difference does knowing make?" His voice had an edge to it. "The technology is real. In five, ten years, the people in this room who dismiss me will regret it."

Duke didn't argue. She understood cynicism. Every time capital flowed toward flashier stocks, every time someone mocked the 'old utility,' she felt something bloom somewhere, then quietly fade. So she knew the weight of that words.

"Ten years." She spoke quietly. "I've lived through ten years dozens of times."

ARV looked at her.

"When the 1973 oil shock hit, and electricity got cut, the entire city stopped. That's why someone like me never becomes useless no matter how much the market shakes. I've lived holding onto that."

"But you're not afraid of change?" ARV asked carefully. "Solar, wind, batteries... eventually you'll have to transform everything too, won't you?"

Duke stopped knitting. For the first time.

"I'm already changing." A silence followed. "Just nobody knows."

ARV tilted his head.

"By 2030, I've committed to cutting carbon emissions in half. Hundreds of billions of dollars in clean energy transition. Quietly. While still paying dividends." A faint smile crossed her face. "Transformation without fanfare—that's my way."

ARV chewed on those words. Transformation without noise. Revolution without headlines. He'd always lived noisily, with stock prices volatile at every announcement, and he'd thought that was his way. But the thought that it might not be everything struck him for the first time.

"Aren't you afraid?" he finally asked. "What if the world changes completely and companies like yours are no longer needed?"

"I am afraid." Duke answered without hesitation. "But fear has never stopped a dividend."

---

As evening fell, the waiting room gradually emptied. Everyone needed to return to their places before tomorrow's market. ARV stood up first.

He grabbed the door handle, then stopped. He stood with his back to her, then slowly turned around.

"One thing." he said. "Next quarter—I think I might post my first profit. A small one, but."

Duke looked down at her knitting needles. One stitch was complete.

"Then."

That was all. Two letters. But ARV felt he'd heard something in those words. Not confirmation or encouragement, but a simple, quiet acceptance—*I see*. He nodded and walked out the door.

Alone in the waiting room, she moved her knitting needles again. The yarn advanced rhythmically, without tremor, toward the next stitch. Outside, the ticker displayed its final numbers before going dark.

The dividend waltz continued today.

Next quarter. And the quarter after that.
