The Last Broker

Floor 33 of the 63 Building in Yeouido, Seoul. The Han River across Ganbyeon North Road sparkled with a dull metallic sheen under the afternoon sun. Dust caught in the window's corners looked like gold powder depending on how the light hit it. Lee Min-jae sat at her desk with her hands folded, staring out that window. For decades, this office had been renovated three times, and twenty-six colleagues had retired before her. Today was the first day of her last remaining week.

Two monitors sat on her desk. The left one displayed the quote screen. Numbers flickered tens of times per second. The right one showed the dashboard for **AlgoSync**, the AI rebalancing system the company had implemented last year. Red and blue bars crossed over each other, updating the risk score in real time. Min-jae barely glanced at the right screen. That was not her language.

She had spent thirty-two years in this seat. When she first joined in 1993, orders came in over the phone. When a client raised their voice on the other end of the line, the temperature of that voice told her the size of the position. A voice panicked, a silence run out of patience, and occasionally, a quiet, heavy voice full of certainty. They were not data. They were people.

AlgoSync does not hear people's voices.

---

Tuesday morning, Park Ki-ho's call came in.

Park Ki-ho was the oldest name among Min-jae's clients. Over twenty years. When he first came to her, he was forty-three and running a printing company in Bucheon. Small but stable. He knew nothing about investing and was not ashamed of that fact. Min-jae liked his honesty.

"It's Ki-ho, ma'am."

His voice was low. Min-jae held the phone closer to her ear.

"What is it?"

"I sold the printing company. Last month."

A brief silence followed. Min-jae pulled out a notepad from her drawer and picked up a pen. It was a habit. Things recorded digitally somehow felt different in weight.

"How much did you get?"

"Just over KRW 1.1 billion after taxes. That's everything. Including severance."

KRW 1.1 billion. Min-jae wrote down the number. She wrote his age next to it too. Sixty-five.

"So."

"I want to try once more. With my own hands."

Min-jae looked out the window. A cruise boat was slowly passing over the Han River.

"Do you know what the market is like right now?"

"I do. That's why I called you."

---

Park Ki-ho came to the office the next afternoon. He was thinner than before, and his hair had turned gray in many places. But his eyes were still the same. Eyes that looked at something head-on. Eyes that did not avoid.

Min-jae poured him coffee and sat across from him.

"What would you like to put it in?"

Park Ki-ho pulled out a newspaper article clipping from his bag. Min-jae almost laughed to herself. Still paper.

"I read this. The secondary battery materials sector."

The article was about the reorganization of the electric vehicle battery supply chain after the second half of 2025. A domestic cathode material company had signed a preliminary contract with a North American automaker. The contract size was not disclosed, but industry estimates put it at KRW 3 trillion or more. Min-jae had read the same article.

"Which stock?"

Park Ki-ho pointed to the company name at the bottom of the article with his finger. It was **QuantumMat**.

QuantumMat was a name Min-jae knew well. The company had attracted significant attention once during the lithium supply crisis in 2022, then grew quiet afterward. It was a mid-cap materials stock. Its recent price hovered near the 52-week low. On the AlgoSync dashboard, this stock had a high risk score. Volatility index, liquidity ratio, short interest. The numbers all said to be careful.

"What does the machine say?" Park Ki-ho asked, pointing at the right monitor.

Min-jae looked at the screen once. AlgoSync was giving QuantumMat a judgment of "top 12% risk." No buy signal.

"The machine says no."

"What about you?"

That was the problem.

---

Min-jae stayed at the office late that night. No one else was there, only the AlgoSync dashboard quietly updating numbers. She opened an old file cabinet. It was packed with paper documents. Handwritten memos from the 1990s and early 2000s, call logs, client consultation notes.

There was a memo from the 2003 card crisis. Everyone had dumped financial stocks back then. VKFN was a small cap company. By the numbers, it had no hope. But there was a record of a call with the company's CEO. A memo noting his voice did not waver. Min-jae had trusted that instinct and quietly suggested it to two clients. Two months later, that company survived.

AlgoSync had learned 2003. But it had not learned that CEO's voice.

Min-jae closed the file. The Han River outside was dark now.

She thought about Park Ki-ho. Twenty years. When he first brought her the printing company contract, his hands shook slightly. That tremor was not fear but a sign of seriousness. She had recognized it. And in those twenty years that followed, that judgment had never been wrong. When he took losses, he was calm. When he made gains, he was cold. He was not someone who came to gamble.

The same was true now. He had not come to gamble. He was thinking seriously about his last capital.

The problem was that the market does not reward seriousness.

---

Thursday, Min-jae called Park Ki-ho.

"Ki-ho, I cannot go against what the machine says."

The other end of the line was quiet for a moment.

"I know."

"But there is something I can tell you."

Min-jae looked at the notepad on her desk. There were things she had written the night before.

"It's true that QuantumMat's numbers are bad. There's a lot of short interest, and the price movement is unstable. It's a stock the machine dislikes."

"But."

"But the counterparty to that preliminary contract is waiting for factory approval from a subsidy issue in North America. If it comes through, it will come within the third quarter. If it doesn't, it's just over."

"Do you think that approval will come through?"

Min-jae paused for a moment.

"I don't know. Honestly."

Another silence.

"But the machine is calculating based on the assumption that the approval won't come. Because approval coming is something that's not in the numbers."

"And you, ma'am. Do you think it will come through or not?"

Min-jae looked out the window. The Han River shone dimly.

"I cannot know whether the company will get the approval. But I know what kind of person Ki-ho has been for twenty years."

"What does that have to do with anything?"

"Ki-ho is not someone who touches money you cannot afford to lose. That's why you called, isn't it? To check whether you could still live without this."

A long breath came through the phone.

"...You're right."

"Then the machine can give you that answer. Do it within the range you can afford to lose. The machine is good at that calculation too."

Park Ki-ho was silent for a while. Min-jae waited.

"Ma'am, this is your last week, isn't it?"

"Yes."

"I'm grateful for all the time."

Min-jae opened her mouth, then closed it. Instead of saying thank you, different words came out.

"So am I, Ki-ho."

---

Friday afternoon, Min-jae cleaned up her desk. She threw away most of her handwritten memos. She packed only a few pages in an envelope. The AlgoSync dashboard was quietly updating numbers as always. QuantumMat's risk score was slightly lower than yesterday. Min-jae looked at that number and tried not to think about it.

While waiting for the elevator on the way out, a junior colleague stood next to her.

"Ma'am, are you really leaving?"

Min-jae nodded.

"Won't algorithms do everything from now on?"

The elevator doors opened.

"I suppose so."

Min-jae stepped inside. As the doors closed, her face appeared in the mirror. It was more at peace than she expected. For thirty-two years she had been with the numbers of this city. In those numbers she had read people's fears and hopes, their foolishness and seriousness. AlgoSync would do that faster, more accurately. Perhaps it would be right in places where Min-jae had been wrong.

Still, things like the temperature of Park Ki-ho's voice would probably take a bit longer.

She passed through the lobby and stepped outside into the evening wind of Yeouido. It was not cold. It was the wind coming from the Han River. Min-jae did not stop walking. She just walked.
