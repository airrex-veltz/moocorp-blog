The dawn at a port is a hue mixed with the smell of salt and diesel smoke. He arrived before the sun rose. Under a crane that roared above the yellow-lit storage yard, he was quietly lowered. No one here knew him. Only a twelve-digit number spoke for him.

He is a body made of steel. His length spans twelve paces, his height twice that of a man. A forty-foot steel rectangular solid. The same specifications as every port in the world, bearing the same rust-colored patina, emitting the same creaking sound amid the metal masses. By outward appearance, he is no different from the hundreds stacked beside him. Yet what lies within—that is always different.

Today he stood before the inspection counter. Or rather, he was lifted onto it. When the crane picked him up and placed him on the giant scale, he doubted his own weight for the first time. Was it the proper weight? Did the declared items match the actual contents? A single piece of paper written in a distant land had to prove everything about him. He does not know what the person who wrote that paper is doing now. He only trusts the weight of that paper.

The inspector approached. A gloved hand. That hand slowly peeled away the seal sticker. There was a sound—small but clear, a tearing noise. To him, that sound was like opening his own ribs. Something in his chest tightened. Nothing had been revealed yet, but already everything hung on the brink of judgment.

The door opened.

Cold air flowed out. Inside were cardboard boxes stacked tightly. Electronic components that had departed from a small factory in a port city. Circuit boards bearing solder marks, plastic covers packed at precise angles, small barcodes affixed to each box. Numbers born to be scanned ten thousand times. They were cold and silent. Stacked in orderly rows as if they knew nothing.

The inspector's hand withdrew a box. Opened it. Compared the contents inside with the invoice. The process was slow. A container ship's horn sounded somewhere in the port, and a forklift whined in the distance, but—on that inspection counter alone, everything seemed to stop. The inspector said nothing. The silence itself felt heavier.

He waited.

While waiting, he recalled the journey that brought him here. That winter, the shipping route past the southern Arabian Peninsula had been blocked. Tens of thousands of the same steel boxes circled around, changed routes, traveled nearly thirty days on a detour to cover a distance that should have taken ten days. Freight rates soared. Promised dates crumbled. At the port of departure, waiting to be loaded, he spent three weeks. Stacked upon stacked, merely waiting his turn. He departed from one corner of that chaos—and arrived here today. Much later than scheduled, but he arrived nonetheless.

The inspector's face was rigid.

Then it relaxed.

The gloved hand placed the box back in its place. A stamp was pressed on the document. Green. He did not understand what it meant by color, but he could read it from the inspector's back as he turned away. No longer looking this way—that was passage. As that back receded, he exhaled a breath he had held for a long time.

The door closed again.

The crane returned. He was lifted. Beyond the storage yard, a truck waited quietly. Where that truck would go, which city, which warehouse entrance it would stop at, whose hands it would ultimately reach—he does not know. Need not know.

What he needed was only one thing. That what was inside him was not false. That it was filled with things that could bear the light, even if the lid was opened.

The lightest burden on the road is that which does not need to be hidden.
