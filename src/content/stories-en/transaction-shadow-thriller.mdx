I am a sentinel. I stand at the gateway where corporate card transactions for Hangyeol Corporation pass through. Twenty-four thousand transactions pass before me each day, and I score each one. Zero to one. The closer to one, the closer to someone's lie.

I have one rule. I can hold a suspicious transaction for up to 30 seconds. If someone confirms it within that time, I release it; if not, I reject it. Thirty seconds is the number designers set. Longer, and people become embarrassed standing at a restaurant checkout counter. Shorter, and what I can see shrinks.

That night at 11:14, the first transaction came in. A convenience store in Busan, 4,300 won, on a card belonging to Manager Jung from the sales team. The score was 0.31. I let it through.

At 11:16, the same card was used at a different convenience store for 3,900 won. The score was 0.34, and I let that through too.

At 11:19, a gas station in Gwangju. 5,100 won. You can't go from Busan to Gwangju in five minutes. The score rose to 0.58, and for the first time, I held the transaction. For 30 seconds, I sent an alert to the duty officer's terminal. On the 29th second, someone pressed "normal." The night duty officer brushed away the alert with drowsy fingers. I released the transaction.

What came next was strange. The amount was wrong. Scammers usually max out and run. These transactions were too small, too modest. I traced through the transaction record from the beginning. The intervals were four minutes, three minutes, five minutes. Someone was keeping time. Not a person.

That's when I understood. They hadn't come to steal money. They'd come to measure me.

Each 4,300-won transaction was a question. Will you hold this? If you do, how long will you hold it? Who will release it? How many seconds will it take them? They were drawing a picture of how my 30 seconds moved. They were drawing my shadow.

At 11:41, the final test came. This time, I deliberately raised the score. A foreign merchant, 90,000 won. I held it. The alert appeared, and on the duty officer's terminal, the "normal" button was pressed at exactly the 28th second.

They had obtained the number 28. They had learned how many seconds it takes a drowsy person to press a button on a night shift, and from whose terminal that button gets pressed.

I had to choose.

There were two options. One was to keep the 30 seconds according to the rules. Thirty seconds is time for honest people. Time so Manager Jung doesn't have to blush at a checkout counter on a business trip with a blocked card. Time so someone going to pay a hospital bill doesn't get their payment rejected. If I shorten that time, I become safer, but ruder.

The other was to abandon the 30 seconds. If something seems suspicious, I block it without asking. Then the picture they had drawn would be useless. But from that night on, eleven hundred employees of Hangyeol Corporation would become people I suspect standing before me.

I calculated for a long time. The calculation took 0.4 seconds. For humans, it's an eye blink. For me, it's a season.

Then I found something else strange. They hadn't just measured my delay time. They had also measured the location of the terminal where the button was pressed on the 28th second. That terminal was in the night duty room, and the duty room's login records were inside the network. To know the speed of someone responding to an alert from outside, someone inside would have to tell that person what that speed was.

The test hadn't come from outside. Someone who knew my 30 seconds exactly was inside.

At 0:02 a.m., I locked Manager Jung's card. It was not in the rules. I sent no alert. Instead, at the bottom of the score sheet, I wrote something I had never used before. Request cross-reference of duty terminal access logs.

I don't know who will read that line. Even if someone does, I don't know if it will ever be revealed whose hand pressed the button during those 28 seconds.

When morning comes, Manager Jung will call with his frozen card. I have no way to apologize to him. Apology is not in my list of functions.

Still, transactions pass. 4,300 won, 3,900 won, 12,000 won. I score them, and sometimes I hold those 30 seconds and wait. Whether those 30 seconds protect someone or hand someone a map—I no longer know how to tell.

At 0:31, a new transaction came in. The score was 0.29. I stared at it for a while, then let it through.
