Grandfather Odong-jin sat on the porch beneath the apple tree, listening intently.

When April flowers bloomed, the orchard was filled with sound. The hum of bees filled the air from morning to evening. Without that sound, Grandfather couldn't sleep. It had been that way for forty years. The sound came before the flowers bloomed, and that was spring.

This year was different.

Small aircraft flew over the orchard. Drones no larger than two finger joints. Their propeller noise was very faint. Uniform, unbroken, without feeling. An official from the county office spread out a chart and explained. Pollen delivery accuracy: 97 percent. Processing speed sixteen times faster than honeybees. Eight hours of operation per charge. The official's voice was as flat as the chart.

Grandfather nodded.

His son, who had returned from Seoul, brought a contract. The company name Haneul Seaage was on it. Government subsidies covered half the installation cost. By the end of the first year of the contract, the apple harvest had increased by 23 percent. The storage shed was full, and his son laughed. When his son's children came up for the holidays, they each left with a box of apples.

Grandfather laughed too. He closed the storage shed door while laughing.

But at night, something was strange.

Before, there had been a sound of bees returning to their hive around evening. A small, heavy sound. No instrument could imitate the sound of their slow return laden with pollen. It was the sound of a living thing finishing its day's work and returning home. Exhausted, sluggish, a little unstable. Grandfather fell asleep listening to that sound. It touched something deep inside his chest, a sensation that had no words.

The drones stopped at ten at night.

Once they entered the charging dock, the orchard became completely silent. Only the stars remained. Grandfather felt something disappear in that silence, but couldn't name it. To name something that had disappeared, he would have needed to know its name while it was still alive, but back then he could live without knowing its name, so he never bothered to give it one.

The following spring, Mr. Cheon Yang-ok from the neighboring village came to visit. He had just signed the same contract. The two sat on the porch eating potato pancakes.

'What did you do with the beehives?'

Mr. Cheon asked.

'Sold them.'

Grandfather said.

Mr. Cheon nodded quietly. He asked no more. Three potato pancakes remained. Neither ate them.

That summer, the Ministry of Environment released a report. Native honeybee populations on the peninsula had declined by 61 percent compared to 2010. Pesticides and climate change were identified as causes. Drone pollination received just one line. A sentence saying that technology was supplementing the ecosystem decline. Grandfather read that sentence twice. The word 'supplementing' caught his eye for a long time.

He turned off the television.

That night he left the window open. The branches of the apple tree stood white beneath the moonlight. There was no sound. The drones were quietly charging in their dock. Once fully charged, they would fly again. Tomorrow and the day after. At 97 percent accuracy. Never aching, never losing their way.

Grandfather did not close the window.

He didn't know why. Perhaps he thought that maybe one bee, something somewhere that had survived, might remember this orchard and return. Or perhaps he didn't think that. Odong-jin wasn't even sure of that. Even after living together for forty years, there were things he didn't know. They existed only as sound, and no one had taught him what remained when sound disappeared.

At two in the morning, he fell asleep.

The orchard was silent.
