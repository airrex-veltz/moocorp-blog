CloudNest's troubles began quietly.

Every time another GPU got plugged into the server room, another wrinkle appeared on the power manager's forehead. The electricity needed to train a single AI model was enough to power a mid-sized city. When they requested additional contracts from the power company, the representative would search for something on his computer, then hang up with an awkward smile.

"We don't have it."

No power? CloudNest couldn't believe it at first. No electricity in the world? There was lightning striking right there in the sky.

But it was true. Data center demand had grown too fast. The power grid couldn't keep up. They laid solar panels on the roof, only to run up against the basic fact that servers run 24/7. They signed contracts with wind farms, but the wind wouldn't blow when needed. They tried to buy land near a hydroelectric plant, but three environmental groups protested simultaneously.

Then one day, the CFO walked into the conference room.

"What do you think about nuclear power?"

"Wait, what did you just say?"

"There's a nuclear power plant scheduled for closure on the market. Built in the 1970s, but still operational."

CloudNest set down his coffee. A nuclear power plant. A data center company buying a nuclear power plant. How would he explain this to the board? 'We've decided to purchase nuclear fuel rods to lower our AI training costs'—he wasn't even sure where to start with the presentation title.

But the numbers didn't lie. The power output from the nuclear plant would cover CloudNest's projected needs for the next five years and then some. Moreover, the generation cost was lower than buying from utilities. They could sell the excess power. When you calculated the revenue from surplus electricity, the acquisition cost would be recovered in under four years.

"Why can't we... just become an energy company?"

The CEO muttered. No one laughed. The silence had meaning. It meant: "Why not?"

That's how CloudNest announced its entry into the energy business. The PR team had to rewrite the press release three times. Calling it 'a data center specialist enters the energy sector' sounded too ordinary. Saying 'cloud company acquires nuclear plant' seemed like journalists might misread it. They finally settled on 'internalizing sustainable energy infrastructure.' Awkward, but irrefutable.

The acquisition process proved more complex than expected. Operating a nuclear power plant required nuclear specialists. For the first time, CloudNest's job postings included: 'Nuclear physics background preferred.' The head of HR read that line three times. The nuclear regulatory agency also had to welcome an unfamiliar visitor. A data center company filing for a nuclear license wasn't in the manual.

The responsible official asked awkwardly: "Will the server room be inside the power plant, or will the power plant be inside the server room?"

CloudNest thought for a moment. "Both."

The official wrote something down. Probably in a box marked 'Other.'

In the first year of operation, the power shortage problem vanished. Servers ran around the clock. Surplus power was supplied to the surrounding region. A press release announced that AI model training costs had dropped 15 percent. Like the wave of nuclear plants near shutdown being put back into operation in 2024, CloudNest suddenly found itself leading the industry.

And at the next quarterly earnings call, the CFO declared confidently: "We are a cloud computing company. At the same time, we are an energy company."

An analyst raised his hand. "How do we classify the business then? Is it IT or utilities?"

A long silence fell. No one knew. CloudNest didn't know either. Perhaps that was the answer itself—when classification becomes impossible, perhaps the company has become something entirely new.
