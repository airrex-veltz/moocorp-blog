The forty-seventh container says nothing.

Things made of steel are usually like that. Rust-stained corners of red, a tracking number marked in white paint, and silence that asks nothing of anyone. Crossing the sea for twelve years, the forty-seventh container has never once complained. Whether it arrives late, or takes a detour.

I am a low-level port operations employee. More precisely, a cargo tracking coordinator, or even more precisely, a person who spends all day staring at numbers that no one asks about. Third floor of Pawado Shipping's Busan office, in a windowless room, I face twelve monitors. Coordinates flow across each screen. Port names appear, ship names appear, estimated arrival times appear. I see them. I mostly don't know what numbers they are carrying.

It was January 2024. Armed forces off Yemen started firing drones and missiles at ships passing through the Red Sea. That news sounded distant at first. The Gulf of Aden was just one thin route on a map. But within ten days, the world container freight index began to shake.

The forty-seventh container was already sailing 350 nautical miles south of the Suez Canal at that time. Its destination was Hamburg. What it carried were semiconductor packaging components. The shipper was a small to mid-sized company somewhere in Gyeonggi Province, and I first saw that company name in one corner of my assigned screen.

A notification came from the captain: route change. Instead of the Red Sea, around Africa's Cape of Good Hope. The distance increased by 7,000 nautical miles. The calculation said an additional eleven days at sea. The container freight index that week rose close to 65 percent.

I had to call the shipper.

The voice on the other end of the line was calmer than expected. "When will it arrive?" It was not a question. It was already the tone of someone who had been waiting for a long time. I told them the estimated arrival date. The other party was silent for a moment, then said "understood." That was all.

The forty-seventh container slowly moved down Africa's west coast on one of the twelve screens. I checked its coordinates every morning as soon as I arrived at work. Because there was nothing else I could do. Insurance processing was a separate team, route decisions were made by the shipping company, and Hamburg was the destination from the start. I was simply someone watching that number move.

Mid-February, the container rounded the Cape of Good Hope. Looking at those coordinates, for the first time I thought about what that rectangle was carrying. Semiconductor components. Some factory's production line. Some contract's delivery date. No one gives it a name. Just a number.

It arrived in Hamburg in early March. The shipper had extended the delivery deadline twice in the meantime. I sent an arrival notification email and deleted that number from the screen.

The next morning, the coordinates of the forty-eighth container appeared on the same screen.

In the windowless room, I stared at that number. It too said nothing. It too was heading somewhere. And somewhere along the way, it would likely have to change its course as well.

I took a sip of coffee and looked back at the monitor.
