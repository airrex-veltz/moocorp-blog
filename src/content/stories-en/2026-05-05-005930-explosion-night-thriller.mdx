Past midnight, Kim Min-jun's phone rang. He was already in bed, but as the head of the Quality Management Team at the Suwon Research Center, his spine went cold when he saw the caller ID. It was the headquarters director.

"Min-jun, you need to get to the research center right now. We have an emergency."

There was a tremor in the voice. In fifteen years of working together, Min-jun had never heard him speak that way. He threw on his clothes and asked, "What's happened?"

"The Note7 has... exploded. The first incident was reported in Florida, and we just received two more reports from Korea."

His heart seemed to stop. The Galaxy Note7. The flagship model released worldwide two months ago. The media had raved that it was "the greatest smartphone ever," and pre-orders alone exceeded 400,000 units. And now explosions.

When he arrived at the research center, the emergency response team was already assembled. On the central table of the conference room lay the charred remains of a Note7. The battery section had completely melted.

"It's the battery," someone said. "The lithium-ion battery overheated and ignited."

But Min-jun instinctively knew it wasn't that simple. There were two battery suppliers: Sunfair SDI and China's Sunfair Battery Technology. If the problem was with the battery itself, incidents should only have occurred with one supplier's product. Yet the reports showed explosion cases from both suppliers' batteries.

"It's not the battery. It's the design," Min-jun said.

The conference room fell silent. Everyone stared at him. A design flaw meant that all 2.5 million units already shipped were ticking time bombs.

Min-jun walked to the whiteboard and began drawing a diagram. "The Note7 was designed to be the thinnest ever. We reduced the battery compartment by an additional 0.2 millimeters. The problem is here."

His finger pointed to the battery compartment. "When the separator between the positive and negative plates experiences pressure, it can be damaged microscopically. That causes an internal short circuit, and..."

"It leads to explosion," the director murmured.

The next four days were hell. The team repeated tests around the clock, working in shifts. They applied pressure to hundreds of Note7s, accelerated charging cycles, and raised temperatures. One by one, the batteries began to swell. Some quietly vented smoke; others erupted in violent flames.

At dawn on the fourth day, Min-jun completed the final report. The conclusion was clear. Total recall. There was no other choice.

That afternoon, the most painful decision in the company's history was made. Sejin Electronics announced that it would recall all 2.5 million Galaxy Note7s sold worldwide, halt production, and offer customers refunds or replacements. The estimated loss exceeded KRW 5 trillion.

Min-jun remained alone in the empty research center, gazing out the window. Dawn was breaking. The company faced a crisis, but at least they had prevented a greater catastrophe. And he knew this: that failure would make Sejin stronger. They would redefine battery safety standards, implement an eight-stage safety inspection process, and never repeat the same mistake.

Two years later, when the Sunfair S8 was released, the media praised it again. But this time, it came with the label of 'the safest smartphone.' Min-jun smiled as he read the article. The terror of that night had finally transformed into trust.
