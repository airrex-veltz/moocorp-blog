I am Dongrim Power.

I have supplied electricity to three cities on the outskirts of the capital region for twenty-two years now. I became the morning light in someone's window, the beating heart of a hospital operating room. When blackouts occurred, I was the first to rush. That was me, and that was my reason for existing.

That pride began to waver last winter.

The power wholesale market settlement system changed. It was framed as a comprehensive overhaul of the fuel cost adjustment system. The wholesale price per kilowatt-hour that I paid to buy electricity from the upper-level network jumped from KRW 98 to KRW 142. A 45 percent increase in three months. When I first saw that figure, I paused for a moment. The number felt like a mistake.

The problem was that the price I could sell to consumers could not keep pace with this speed. The regulated tariff system had a ceiling, and raising that ceiling required government approval. The government had no intention of raising electricity rates before next year's election.

I began to slowly sink into that gap.

---

In March, the quarterly settlement ended in deficit for the first time.

When Chief Financial Officer Park Sung-hyun entered CEO Yun Jae-ho's office with the report, Yun Jae-ho flipped the document facedown. So the numbers wouldn't be visible.

"How far has this gone?"

"It's an internal draft. Before the audit."

"Then bury it."

I watched that scene. Something other than relief or agreement crossed Park Sung-hyun's face. Just fatigue.

Burying it wasn't difficult. Reclassify as one-time equipment costs, or leave it as accounts receivable. Numbers could go anywhere. The problem was that bills didn't wait to see where they went.

April. May. The hole grew larger.

---

By mid-June, Park Sung-hyun began arriving at dawn. At six in the morning, when no one else was there, he opened the spreadsheet alone. Without coffee. I watched his eyes move down line by line. I knew too—he wasn't calculating profit, but survival.

Where could he hold on. How much more could he hide.

In early July, an external audit firm announced their annual review. It seemed like nothing. That afternoon, Yun Jae-ho called Park Sung-hyun in.

"I want to see the audit response materials first."

"Which part?"

"Q2 distribution cost items. What you processed as fuel cost reversal."

Park Sung-hyun was silent for a moment.

"That's not the correct treatment for reversal. The substance is actually a loss—"

"I'm not asking about the substance. I'm asking about the treatment method."

In that silence, I felt something tighten. Park Sung-hyun was making a decision.

Without looking up, he spoke. "I signed off on the treatment method. The responsibility is on me."

Yun Jae-ho looked at him for a moment. Then he left the room.

---

That night I thought again about what I was.

I am an electricity bill. I am a transformer, an underground cable, a streetlight illuminating a dark alley. I am also a contract promising a day without blackouts. But I am also the document that Park Sung-hyun signed, the report that Yun Jae-ho flipped facedown, and the ledger that began to develop holes.

No company knows in advance how far it can hold on. Neither did I.

---

In August, the audit began.

The senior partner flagged the distribution cost items. Park Sung-hyun explained calmly. He provided numbers and evidence. The partner nodded while jotting down something. I didn't know where that note would go.

On the evening of the third day, Park Sung-hyun remained alone in the office after work. He opened a file. Q2 Results Amendment. Cancellation of fuel cost reversal. Restatement of losses. It was a draft he had prepared alone months before.

He affixed his signature to it.

And sent an email to the audit firm partner.

The moment the sending confirmation appeared on the screen, his hand stopped above the keyboard. Just briefly.

The next morning, Yun Jae-ho already knew. He called Park Sung-hyun and hung up in fifteen seconds. I never heard what filled those fifteen seconds. After that, I didn't see the two of them in the same space again.

---

In September, my first-half earnings were announced.

Loss reversal due to rising distribution costs. Wholesale settlement unit price increase noted. Restated Q2 financial statements attached.

Stock price fell 17 percent in two days. It was expected. I knew it, and so did Park Sung-hyun.

But the disclosure went out.

---

I still send electricity today.

Today too, lights came on in three cities. In someone's room, knowing nothing. In hospital corridors. On traffic lights. I cannot imagine it stopping. So I continue.

The deficit has not been resolved. There are rumors that the settlement system will change again, but no one knows when. No word on rate increase approval. What consequences the amended financial statements Park Sung-hyun submitted will bring to him—I still don't know.

I know only this: what I believed was solid turned out not to be solid, but simply not yet broken. The difference is greater than one might think.
