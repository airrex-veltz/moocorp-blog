Spring 2021. I stood before the board with the confidence that I was designing our company's future. One slide showed a five-year cloud migration roadmap, and the last slide bore the bold words: "On-premises server room demolition." I was applauded. CFO Lee Sang-ho said to me, "At last, we're embracing digital transformation." I felt proud.

Now it is March 2026. I stand before the same boardroom again. The last slide of this presentation reads: "Three-year on-premises return plan."

Manager Park Hye-young caught me in the hallway yesterday afternoon.

"Jae-hyun, are these numbers right? Is this really how much we spent on cloud over five years?"

I nodded. The numbers were accurate.

"We've been exceeding 240 million won monthly since Q3 last year. That's when AMZN raised reserved instance rates a second time. EC2 On-Demand had already gone up 18 percent before that."

Manager Park fell silent for a while. Then she asked quietly, "When you first designed this, did you not see it coming?"

If I said no, it would be a lie. If I said yes, I'd be judging my past self too harshly. So I said, "The projections were different."

In 2021, everything seemed right. Cloud was elastic. Scaling was one button. We didn't need to hire more server administrators. We saved on data center lease costs. Enterprises around the world were moving in the same direction. There was comfort in riding that wave.

The first three years were actually good. Servers never crashed during peak traffic. The dev team shipped features without worrying about infrastructure. I gave two conference talks: "Successful all-cloud transition."

The cracks started small. Late 2023, the billing team sent an odd alert. That month's bill was 40 percent higher than expected. Investigation revealed it was data egress costs. After adopting multi-region architecture, inter-region traffic exceeded projections by a factor of three. I reworked the architecture. Costs came down. Then I forgot about it.

Summer 2024, things changed. Three quarters in a row, On-Demand rates climbed. CoreSoft Azure raised rates for enterprise contract renewals, even for existing customers. I raised the proportion of reserved instances, mixed in spot instances, and barely kept my head above water. I called it cost optimization. It was actually patching holes.

CFO Lee called me directly for the first time that November.

"Jae-hyun, let's redo the TCO comparison. I hear on-premises rack costs have dropped quite a bit lately."

I sent him analysis a few days later. And as I confirmed those numbers again, I stared at the screen for a long time. Looking only at stable baseline workloads—batch processing and internal ERP systems that need to run 24/7—on a three-year TCO basis, on-premises was 38 percent cheaper. Seventy percent of our company's workload fell into that stable baseline category.

I wrote the number down in my notebook and looked out the window for a moment. The office on the 13th floor overlooked the Han River in Seoul.

The two days writing the report were long. It wasn't simply organizing data. It was going back through five years of decisions one by one. The 2021 version of me wasn't wrong. At that pricing, cloud was rational. But the 2026 version of me had to clean up the consequences of that decision.

Manager Park spoke again.

"Honestly, Jae-hyun. When you report this, won't there be accountability issues?"

I paused for a moment.

"There could be."

"But you'll do it anyway?"

"If I don't, it gets worse next year."

That was the most honest answer I could give.

The board presentation was quieter than expected. Lee Sang-ho scanned the numbers, then asked, "How long will the return take?" I said, "36 months. CapEx will be concentrated in the first 18 months." He nodded and spoke briefly with someone beside him. There was no rebuke. That made it even more uncomfortable.

After the meeting, I paused in the restroom. There was a tired-looking man in the mirror. The same person who, five years ago in this same spot, believed in all of this without question.

I still don't think cloud was a bad choice. The world just moved faster than we did. Or we tried to keep up but the structure was already set in stone. Either way, the outcome was the same.

The return plan was approved. Starting next week, I have to begin meetings with hardware vendors. That server room I thought five years ago I would never enter again—I had to redesign it. The blueprints are still blank.

This story is not unique to our company. Since 2025, global enterprises have been pulling out the same calculations again. I know this. Somewhere, another Jae-hyun is staring at the same screen. And for a long time, looking out the window.
