Jung Ha-ram stood before the full-length window of her office, gazing down at the lobby of the new building below. Four months earlier, a banner had hung in the center of that lobby: "Bloom Sound, Asia's Number One Music Platform." Now, there was nothing. Just an empty wall.

Bloom Sound Entertainment had been South Korea's third-largest music streaming platform. Ha-ram had founded it nine years ago and brought it this far. Then, early this year, the company announced an acquisition of Southeast Asia's largest music distributor. The deal was worth KRW 240 billion. Industry headlines proclaimed: "Bloom Sound finally expands into Asian markets."

The acquisition fell apart in four months.

During due diligence, it emerged that many of the target company's copyright licensing agreements were nearing expiration. One-third of the core artist catalog was essentially in limbo. The board withdrew from the deal. Of the KRW 18 billion already paid, not a single won would be recovered. In truth, such due diligence risks were not unusual in the domestic music industry. According to data released by the IFPI, the global music streaming market had surged in recent years, exceeding USD 20 billion in annual revenue. Yet beneath that growth lay structural risks: the complexity of copyright contracts and regional licensing mismatches that never disappeared. Bloom Sound had crashed directly into that structural risk.

But money was not the real problem. Money could be managed. The real problem came after.

"Director, we've scheduled an all-hands town hall for three o'clock this afternoon."

HR manager Yun So-jin tapped on the door as she spoke. Ha-ram nodded, but unease gripped her. Over the past three weeks, anonymous posts had accumulated on the internal bulletin board. "When we announced the acquisition, people were talking about promotions. Now nobody takes responsibility." "Our team's budget was already cut in preparation for the acquisition, so what are we supposed to do now?" The most-viewed posts all carried the same emotion: betrayal.

The most painful blow came from the Content Planning Division's exodus. Seven of the twelve people reassigned to a new business task force after the acquisition announcement had resigned in the past month. Three of them, rumors said, had joined Rhythm Forest, their biggest competitor. These were people Ha-ram had personally hired.

Ten minutes before the town hall began, Ha-ram opened her presentation deck, then closed it. This was not a problem that could be explained with numbers.

Nearly four hundred employees packed the main auditorium. With those joining by video, attendance exceeded seven hundred. When Ha-ram stepped onto the stage, the hall fell silent. There was no applause.

"I'm going to start with an apology."

These were Ha-ram's first words. The prepared script did not contain this sentence.

"When I announced the acquisition, I gave you certainty. I told you this deal would take us to the next level. That certainty was mine alone, not backed by due diligence data. I saw only what I wanted to see. And you paid the price for that."

From the back of the hall, someone drew a sharp breath that the microphone picked up. Ha-ram did not pause.

"Seven people from the new business task force left the company. I remember all their names. Some asked for exit interviews before they left. Others simply submitted their resignation letters in silence. Both scenarios are my fault."

Ha-ram advanced the slide. Instead of financial projections, a blank screen appeared.

"Today is not a planning presentation. This is a time for questions. Ask me anything you want."

Silence stretched. Finally, a third-year developer raised his hand.

"Director, why should we trust you again?"

It was a sharp question. The entire hall waited for her answer.

"Right now, there's no reason to."

Ha-ram replied.

"What I can offer you is not promises but process. For the next three months, every decision I make will have its rationale shared on the company intranet. To avoid repeating the oversights from the acquisition due diligence, from this quarter forward, every strategic decision will require a verification stage involving our operational teams. I'm going to change the structure where I decide alone and you clean up afterward."

Questions continued. Budget concerns, organizational restructuring, what had happened to colleagues who had left. Nearly two hours passed. Ha-ram remained on stage.

That night, Ha-ram stayed alone in her office and pulled up the list of people who had quit the new business task force. She called Oh Ji-hyeok, a member of the content curation team.

"Director?"

"I apologize for calling so late. I'm curious about something. What was the hardest part of the task force?"

Ji-hyeok chose his words carefully.

"The direction kept changing. Every time the acquisition came up, our roadmap got rewritten. Eventually, we didn't even know what we were working for."

Ha-ram wrote down his words. It hurt more than any question asked at the town hall.

"Is there any chance you'd come back?"

"Not right now."

It was an honest answer. Ha-ram was grateful for that honesty.

"That's fine. The door stays open."

Starting the next day, Bloom Sound moved quietly, without any notable announcements. Ha-ram began holding small roundtable discussions every Friday afternoon, rotating through departments. No agenda, no recording, no moderator. The first few weeks, employees held back. They seemed afraid of facing repercussions for being honest in front of their director.

During the sixth roundtable, a crack finally appeared. The marketing team's most junior employee raised her hand.

"Our team worked the most overtime during the acquisition announcement. But after it fell through, no one said a single word of thanks to us."

Ha-ram heard this and sent an email to all employees the next day. Not an apology—gratitude. She listed the names of every department that had worked overtime during the acquisition push, every person who had prepared materials. She didn't offer formulaic thanks. She wrote specifically who had done what. Responses flooded in. For the first time, the mood inside the company began to shift.

Three months passed. Bloom Sound's monthly active users remained flat. Their competitor Rhythm Forest seized the opportunity, launching aggressive marketing while the market share gap didn't narrow. By the numbers alone, nothing had improved.

But another number changed: the voluntary resignation rate. Compared to the quarter right after the acquisition collapse, resignations in the next quarter fell below half. Around that time, Ji-hyeok reached out about returning. Ha-ram did not call him back to his old position. Instead, she asked him to help design the decision-making structure for a new project.

"The roadmap won't change this time?"

Ji-hyeok asked.

"It might. But this time, I'll ask you first why it needs to change."

Ha-ram answered like this.

At an investor meeting, someone asked: What was greater—what Bloom Sound lost or what it gained from the acquisition failure? Ha-ram did not answer immediately.

"I'm still calculating."

She said.

"The financial statements show a confirmed loss. But how the organization has changed—I still can't see the whole picture. I think I might not know until years from now."

The empty wall in the lobby remained empty. Ha-ram had no intention of hanging another banner there. Instead, a small bulletin board had been placed in that space. A place where employees could freely share their thoughts each week. The first note pinned there read:

"Let's ask each other what we're working for this time."

No one ever revealed who had written it.
