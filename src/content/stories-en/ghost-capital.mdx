I set foot in that city for the first time in twelve years.

The train station was still in the same place, but I was the only person inside it. Above the ticket window hung a timetable covered in dust. The date when the last train had stopped was written there, three years after I had left the city. I stared at those numbers for a long time. It meant that this city had endured for three more years even in my absence.

When the government offices relocated to the south, people began packing. First the civil servants left, then the restaurant owners who fed them, and finally only those with nowhere else to go remained before they too disappeared one by one. There was a record that half the city's population had fled within five years. But walking it myself, the numbers said nothing. It felt as though not half but everything had disappeared.

I walked down Jungangno Road. Weeds pushed through the cracks in the stones, and half the streetlights were dark. There was a time when walking this street meant light pouring from shops lined on both sides. People brushed shoulders as they walked, and steam from the food stands clouded the winter air. I remembered the day Seo-jin and I first walked down this street. That person put their left hand in my right pocket and said, 'It's warm here even in winter.' I learned then that my pocket was wide enough to hold that hand.

Seo-jin remained in the city while it was still alive. I found an opportunity and left, and Seo-jin said they would stay. We first fought, and last fought, over what city we would live in. Seo-jin said, 'Even if this city crumbles, I will live here.' I could not tell if those words were a confession of love or a declaration of farewell. Perhaps they were both.

The night before I left, we sat on Seo-jin's rooftop and looked down at the lights of the city. The lights were still numerous then. We sat for a long time without speaking, then Seo-jin went down the stairs first. I remained alone looking at those lights for a while longer before returning. I do not remember whether I wept on the way back. It was the last time.

After that, there were a few exchanges of contact. At first frequent, then occasional, and finally nothing. I do not know when Seo-jin left this city or where they live now. I had tried to find out, but at some point asking that question itself became touching an old wound.

I did not come back to find them. At least that is what I told myself. I simply wanted to see once what had become of this city.

When I turned into the alley of the old central market, I stopped. Light was spilling from one window between the shuttered storefronts. Words written by hand on paper were pasted inside the window. 'Open today too.' I could see a person inside, sitting with their back turned, writing something. I knew that person was not Seo-jin. The build was different, and they looked older. But I gazed at that light for a long time.

There is always someone who stays until the end of a city. And those who leave always long for that light. That is what I thought.

It began to rain on my way back to the station. First fine, then heavy. I sheltered under the eaves for a moment, then walked again. Walking in the rain. There was a farewell I had not managed that day twelve years ago. For Seo-jin, who had been the first to descend those stairs, and for this city once full of light. That farewell was not in words but in steps.

I waited for the first morning train in the station waiting room. The wooden chair was cold, but I sat for a long time with my bag on my lap. Outside the window the rain was wetting the city. They say ghosts do not get wet in the rain. But I was soaking wet. That was enough.
