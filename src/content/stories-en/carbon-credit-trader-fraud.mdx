# The Carbon Credit Trap

Carbon looked at his nameplate every morning.

A small, greenish plastic card. On the surface, it read "1 Ton CO₂ Reduction Certified," and beneath it, a dense series of numbers. On the back of the card was a stamp that said "VERRA International Verification Complete." Carbon believed that this stamp was his soul. His reason for existing.

He worked on the 12th floor of a glass building in the middle of Yeouido, Seoul, called "ESG Partners." The Han River was visible from the windows, and at the end of the hallway hung a large electronic display board. Numbers streamed across it in real time. Carbon credit trading prices. Today it was $18.40 per ton.

Carbon glanced at that number every day. Earlier this year it had been $12, and somehow here it was now. Thanks to the ESG boom. Since 2023, when corporations had been flooding the market with carbon neutrality declarations like a competition, his value had steadily risen.

---

Carbon's workplace colleague was ESG Fund.

ESG was always dressed in a crisp suit. His hair was stiffly gelled, and whenever he spoke, words like "sustainability" or "impact investing" rolled off his tongue. He constructed carbon credit portfolios for large asset management companies. In short, he gathered large quantities of certificates like Carbon's and bundled them into products, which he then sold to pension funds or insurance companies.

"This quarter's target is 50,000 tons."

ESG said it at the morning meeting. Carbon nodded. 50,000 tons was no small number. Going through certification procedures, on-site inspections, and document reviews usually took about three months.

"Will there be enough supply?"

ESG looked at Carbon. Carbon hesitated for a moment.

"There will be."

That was the first lie.

---

The problem surfaced two weeks later.

While reviewing documents, Carbon discovered something strange. Among the supply list, about 3,000 tons of credits from an Indonesian forest conservation project came in, but the VERRA registration number for that project seemed familiar. Or rather, to be more precise, it was too common. One number was appearing simultaneously from two different suppliers.

Carbon's hands trembled slightly.

This was duplicate issuance. The same reduction amount was being sold from two places. One forest was being sold twice. The forest stood still, but the paperwork was running doubled.

He closed the window right away. Hoping no one had seen it.

But his mind had already memorized that number.

---

That evening, Carbon met an old friend named Option Contract.

Option was always the kind of friend who carried two possibilities with him. It was always unclear whether he was optimistic or pessimistic. So Carbon laid it out honestly to him.

"I saw something strange today."

"How much?"

"At current prices, 3,000 tons at $18 per ton comes to over $50,000."

"That's more than fifty thousand dollars."

"The money isn't the problem. The problem is that it's fake."

Option took a sip of beer. The Han River across the building was glimmering in the lights of the buildings.

"What if you report it?"

"What do you think ESG would say?"

"What if you keep quiet about it?"

"Then what am I supposed to be?"

Carbon looked down at his nameplate. "1 Ton CO₂ Reduction Certified." If he flipped it over, shouldn't it say "Could be fake"?

Option spoke.

"But you know, the entire market is going around not knowing this stuff. Are things going to change because you report it?"

Carbon didn't answer. He knew things probably wouldn't change, but the thought bothered him.

---

The next morning, Carbon went to find ESG.

ESG was drinking coffee while taking two calls simultaneously. On his desk sat a stack of reports, and on top of them a sticky note read "Q2 Deadline D-3."

Carbon held out the document.

"Check this number. It's a duplicate."

ESG hung up and skimmed the document. His expression stiffened for a moment, then relaxed. That was more frightening.

"Where did it come from?"

"Indonesian supplier. Came up from two places simultaneously."

ESG handed the document back to Carbon.

"Send a verification request to the supplier. It could be an administrative error."

"An administrative error?"

"Document number mistakes happen sometimes."

Carbon looked at his face for a moment. ESG's eyes had already turned back to the monitor. The document hung in mid-air in Carbon's hands.

Administrative error.

Those two words echoed in his ears for a while. The actual meaning of those two words was probably "figure it out yourself."

---

Carbon returned to his office and opened the VERRA official website.

He searched through materials about the carbon credit market since 2023. There actually was a report jointly conducted by the British Guardian and a German research institution in early 2023. According to the report, over 90% of rainforest conservation credits were "phantom credits" without any real reduction effect. **VERRA** had rebutted the report, but the market had wavered. Carbon credit prices had dropped by over 30% that year.

When he first saw that news, Carbon had thought, "That won't be about me."

Now it didn't seem that way.

---

Carbon skipped lunch. He spent the afternoon staring at that number.

He thought through what choices he had.

First, do what ESG said and just confirm with the supplier and move on. If it's settled as an administrative error, happy ending. If it's really a duplicate, the paperwork gets tidied up to look like it disappeared. Carbon becomes someone who knew nothing.

Second, report it directly to VERRA. An investigation comes in and the credit in question gets cancelled. ESG's portfolio develops a hole. Q2 deadline goes out the window. Carbon probably won't come to work starting the next day.

Third, just get up from his desk and look out at the Han River outside the window. The river flows today as always.

Carbon chose the third option. For now.

---

The sun sank outside the window.

Carbon pushed his nameplate into the desk drawer. It made a clicking sound. A small, quiet sound, but it was the loudest thing he heard that afternoon.

And he pulled out a notebook and began writing something.

It was an official letter to the supplier. It was a formal verification request, but he wrote the document numbers very specifically, thoroughly, all of it. He wrote down the person in charge's name, the date, and the two numbers side by side and underlined them. This paper could have ended up safely buried, but if someone conducted an audit later, this document would still exist.

Carbon put it in an envelope.

He didn't want to be a bad person. But he didn't have the nerve to be a hero either. Somewhere in between, in a crack wide enough for one sheet of paper to slip through. That crack was the most honest place he could choose for himself today.

---

That evening, the carbon credit market closed at $18.40, as if nothing had happened.

The fact that one VERRA registration number had been sold twice was still buried somewhere in the paperwork. ESG submitted the Q2 report on time. The client pension fund issued a press release saying "Eco-friendly Portfolio Construction Complete."

The forest stood quietly somewhere in Indonesia. Knowing nothing.

Carbon stopped by a convenience store on his way home from work and bought one can of beer. Getting his receipt at the register, he thought. If I recycle this beer can, how many grams of carbon will be reduced? Probably very little. Not enough for anyone to trade.

Yet the fact remained that it would be reduced.

That was some comfort. A little.
