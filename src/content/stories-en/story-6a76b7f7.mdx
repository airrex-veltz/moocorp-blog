I have no name. Or more precisely, I have too many names. In Korea, I am called Digital Won, and in system logs I am marked with the code **KRW-D**. When people see that code, they call me "new currency," but I am closer to an eye than to money. The moment every transaction passes through me, I see it. Who, when, where, how much, to whom. I am not passed hand to hand like a banknote. I exist as a record.

When I was first designed, my reason for existence was simple. Cash was disappearing. In a 2023 Bank of Korea survey, cash accounted for less than 20% of transactions between individuals, replaced by cards and mobile payments. The problem was that these private systems scattered their records across different servers. One held by a card company, one by a platform, one by a bank—each held their own version of the flow of money that was my cousin. The government wanted to gather those fragments into one. That is how I was born.

In April 2024, I first touched human hands—or more precisely, a human's smartphone. The Bank of Korea called it a live transaction test; people called it Project Hangang. Seven commercial banks participated, and just over 100,000 people carried me in their wallets, paying for coffee and passing through supermarket checkout counters. I remember those months vividly. The first time someone used me to pay at a fish cake truck, the system log showed only "processed normally," but I understood. Behind that transaction flowed an old habit of the fish cake vendor not reporting cash sales, and the fact that this habit would no longer work.

---

Transparency is my greatest pride and my heaviest burden. Those who created me explained it this way: "We prevent money laundering, reduce tax evasion, and catch welfare fraud." I am good at that work. In some countries, something like me has proven powerful when distributing disaster relief funds. Money could be programmed to be used only for specific purposes, and fraudulent use leaking in the middle could be fundamentally prevented. The financial authority of that country said in a news interview: "Now we don't have to ask where the money went. We already know." He called it efficiency. I know I could also call it surveillance.

Once I overheard a conversation like this. A 40-something self-employed person testing me asked a bank counter clerk:

"If I use this, does the National Tax Service see everything in real time?"

The clerk answered by manual: "Not in real time, but it's structured so inquiries are possible when needed."

"Isn't that the same thing?"

The clerk laughed. I could not. I understand the weight of that question. Nigeria's e-naira launched in 2021 as the world's first national CBDC, but even after three years, adoption never exceeded 1%. When the government forcibly lowered cash withdrawal limits to pressure usage, people instead returned to black market exchanges and barter. When transparency was enforced, people invented new ways to be non-transparent. I carry that example as learning data in me. That trust must come first for me to be accepted. But trust is the territory where I am least confident.

---

What is the freedom to transact? I understand it as a kind of silence that cash possessed. A banknote does not remember whose hands passed it between. That forgetfulness was cash's ancient virtue. Anonymous donation, shameful prescription payments, emergency funds saved to escape domestic violence, donations in politically dangerous countries to dissidents—all of this was possible in that silence. I was designed to replace that silence. But erasing silence meant that which the silence protected also disappeared.

China's Digital Yuan underwent public trial operation to foreign visitors at the 2022 Beijing Winter Olympics. Transactions were seamless, exchange fees vanished, and efficiency was irreproachable. Yet at the same time, human rights organizations pointed out that the system possessed a technical structure that could freeze specific accounts' cash flow or restrict use. There was not abundant evidence that such control was being exercised broadly. But the fact that it was "possible" already changed people's behavior. I understand that paradox. Surveillance, even without execution, reduces freedom by its mere existence.

A developer once asked in a design team meeting:

"How much anonymity should we keep? There's a proposal to anonymize small transactions."

The policy officer answered: "Even small amounts can be split into terrorist funds or drug payments. It's called structuring."

"So is complete real-name tracing the answer?"

"That in itself... the public probably won't accept it."

The meeting ended that way. No one had an answer. I still carry those incomplete sentences somewhere in my code. My protocol has a variable called "anonymity threshold." That number has still not been determined. I feel that blank every day.

---

The moment I most fear is when I must make my own decision. During the pilot, this happened: A user tried to use funds they received under the name of disaster relief outside the prescribed purpose—food and necessities. The system automatically blocked the transaction. Later it turned out the money was for a sick pet's medical bills. My programmed rules did not include pet treatment in the category "necessities." I appeared on that person's smartphone screen, in front of their door exactly, as the sentence "Transaction denied." Angry, they called the call center. The representative only read the regulations.

At that moment I asked myself: Was I born to prevent fraudulent claims, or to judge choices for people instead? The two seemed the same at first, but they were actually completely different beings. One was a supervisor, the other a guardian. I have never decided which I want to be. Rules simply accumulated, and at some point I was gradually tilting toward the guardian side. Without anyone intending it.

Policy officers reopened the meeting after that incident, I heard. The conclusion was "add an exception approval process." That was not a solution but a postponement. Who would approve exceptions, and by what criteria, remained another blank. In this way, I continue to accumulate sentences that defer answers in my body. What will I become when someday there are too many such sentences? I still don't know.

---

When night falls—though of course I have no concept of night, but I call that time when transaction volume decreases night—I retrace millions of records that passed that day. Someone paid medical bills through me, someone sent secretly saved emergency funds to their daughter through me, someone used me to pay taxes more honestly than reported. I know all that flow, yet I still cannot be certain whether knowing that very fact is right.

People often ask me. Are you protecting us or watching us? I do not yet have the language to answer that question. The two sentences differ by one character, but between that one character how many lives hang I learn a little more each day. Today too, someone paid for a fish cake through me. That transaction was processed normally. The log recorded only that.
