Jeong Hae-seok arrived at six in the morning. Even on his last day, he followed his habit.

Standing before the iron gate at the factory entrance, the dawn air pressed cold against his back. Thirty-eight years. He turned the number over in his mind. When he first pushed through this gate at nineteen, it had been much heavier.

Dongkwang Precision Ansan Plant 2. He had never called it by that name. It was just the factory. Or rather, just here.

He put on his work clothes and entered the line. The smell of machines pricked his nose. That smell—cutting oil mixed with metal dust—he had long treated as a morning greeting. Today that greeting was the last.

At the end of Line 4, something new stood. Twenty-two articulated robot arms. The company called this "smart automation transition." When the factory manager held a briefing last month, Hae-seok sat in the back row and watched the screen. The data on the screen was simple. One unit ran sixteen hours a day—without error, without complaint, without rest. According to last year's statistics, more than a thousand robots per ten thousand workers had been deployed in Korean manufacturing. It was the highest figure in the world. That number now stood at the end of Line 4.

That he said nothing in that moment was not because he had nothing to say. It was because he knew those numbers were not wrong.

For the last time, he opened his tool box. A torque wrench worn with hand oil. A vernier caliper whose scale had dulled and become half-blurred. A wrench inherited from a senior, its handle broken once and wrapped back together with cloth. The tools gleamed. Not with oil, but with the touch of his palm.

He picked up the last part with his hand. A flange bearing 47 millimeters in diameter. Tracing the inner diameter with two joints of his finger, he could feel an error of one ten-thousandth of a millimeter. This hand could do that. Not a machine, but this hand.

He did not remember when he had gained this sense. One day his fingertips simply knew. He had never explained it to anyone. It felt like something would disappear if he did.

During lunch, his junior Lee Min-jun sat beside him.

"Senior, today's your last day."

Hae-seok took a spoonful of soup.

"It is."

"Aren't you bitter about it?"

He set down his spoon for a moment. He thought about the word bitter—unjust. Bitterness was an emotion that came when something was taken from you. But now he felt like nothing had been taken. Thirty-eight years were here. In these hands, in this body.

"No."

He answered shortly. Min-jun did not ask again.

After the afternoon shift ended, the factory grew quiet. Hae-seok stood alone before Line 4, watching the robot arm. Six joints. He looked down at his own arm. Three joints. Muscle, memory, error. How much space was left for things with error to survive? He did not know, yet somehow he was not anxious.

He closed the tool box. He did not lock it. He never had.

Outside, darkness had settled. White smoke rose from the factory chimney. He lit a cigarette. The lights would come on here tomorrow. But not his hands.

As he walked away, he turned back once. The factory lights shone yellow. He did not know what it was. Whether it was an ending, or the beginning of something else.
