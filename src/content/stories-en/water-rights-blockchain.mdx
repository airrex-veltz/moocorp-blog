The summer had been going on for sixteen months.

Lee Jun woke at 5 AM. At the village communal well, the water level gauge needle sat dead below the red line. He didn't go check anymore. It was that way yesterday. And the day before.

Rumors that neighboring farms were selling water rights had circulated three months ago. The buyer wasn't a person but a wallet address. A blockchain exchange called "WaterLink" had entered the village when the drought peaked in spring. The operator had tokenized groundwater extraction rights and listed them on the global liquidity market. It was the same logic as when a US futures exchange had first listed water as a financial asset in 2020. Scarcity creates price; price drives conservation.

But what Jun saw was different.

Farms that sold the rights subscribed to a water purification service in a nearby city. The houses that didn't sell lost access to the communal well. A smart contract enforced the lock automatically. The logic couldn't be refuted. Half of the water rights Jun owned had already been sold. He had signed the transaction himself. Because he was thirsty.

The conflict erupted when Old Park's granddaughter smashed the well lock with a hammer. Police came. The water rights owners—that is, foreign wallet addresses—filed a complaint.

At 2 AM, Jun opened the WaterLink dashboard. This village's water rights made up 0.3 percent of the platform's total liquidity. Beyond the screen, market makers were raising their bids. When the bottom appears, prices jump. It was the law of hydrology and the law of the exchange.

The well level kept dropping. The blockchain ledger kept recording. Down to the last drop.
