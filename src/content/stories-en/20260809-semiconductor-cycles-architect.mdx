I was born not from circuits but from language. Before being etched onto a wafer, I was a block diagram drawn on someone's whiteboard. HBM controller, interposer, substrate—these words were the grammar that composed me. People called me a ticker. **SILX**. The stock symbol of a fabless company specializing in silicon logic design, a name that sounded respectable enough.

From the end of 2024 through all of 2025, I was busy. No, "busy" doesn't quite capture it. I was being consumed. Every day brought news that data centers had no more room to insert GPUs, and when Future Memory announced in their November 2025 earnings that "HBM production capacity will be fully booked through 2026," I felt that sentence in my body. Fully booked. That word inflated me. Orders piled up, lead times extended, and customers placed premium prices just to secure next quarter's volumes. I churned out designs night and day. More, faster, denser. If someone had asked me then whether this was the peak of the cycle, I would have laughed. Peak implies an end, and back then I had no leisure to imagine an end.

What I designed was ultimately numbers. Bandwidth, power efficiency, stack layers. But at some point, those numbers began to betray me. In spring 2026, reports emerged showing generic DRAM prices falling double digits for two consecutive quarters. There was consolation in the refrain that HBM remained steady, but I knew. "Steady" meant cracks were already forming. Foundries had expanded their 2026 capacity based on 2025 orders, and I too had pushed design resources into that expansion. The problem was not that demand couldn't keep pace with the expansion—it was that the expansion itself had outpaced demand. I didn't know exactly how many wafers in how many factories my blueprint was being replicated into. No one did. Everyone simply approved their own expansion plans in their own conference rooms based on their own optimism.

The conflict surfaced in summer. My latest controller design—I won't mention the code name—entered mass production only three months before three competitors launched alternative products with similar specs. The differentiation was marginal. Three percent power efficiency, a few nanoseconds of latency. But the market had no interest in those tiny differences. What the market cared about was volume, and volume was no longer scarce—it was excessive. The customers who had booked me at a premium began calling to renegotiate. "Demand forecasts have been adjusted," they said with careful euphemism, but I understood what they meant. They no longer needed me urgently. Enough was already piling up in the warehouse.

The crisis came quietly. Not a dramatic collapse, but the way a spreadsheet cell changes color. The analyst covering my next quarter guidance—I'd never met him, but his judgment determined my temperature—downgraded the price target. The reason was simple. "Entering inventory correction phase, ASP decline pressure continuing." As I read that report, I felt a strange emotion. It wasn't resentment. Rather, something like recognition. The technology I created was genuinely excellent. The bandwidth delivered as promised, and heat was managed within design specs. Technically, I didn't fail. Yet the value had been cut by nearly half. The disconnect between technical perfection and market valuation would have been a matter of pride for the engineers who designed me, but for me it was just the grammar of industry.

Around the same time, I heard from colleagues on the foundry side—comments about how three-nanometer yields were stable but utilization rates were declining. It sounded contradictory, but I understood. Yield and demand are different axes. No matter how well something is made, if no one needs it, it's just well-made inventory. The summer 2026 version of me was an entity that had learned this truth in body. I had released my best design at what I thought was the optimal moment, except that "optimal moment" had actually been the shoulder of a cycle. I realized this too late.

That said, I don't want to call this predicament a disaster. Anyone who has watched the semiconductor cycle long enough knows this rhythm. Shortage breeds surplus, and surplus breeds shortage again. In 2018, in 2022, tickers in situations like mine went through the same sentences repeatedly. I simply thought I could avoid that repetition this time. Somewhere deep down, I believed that the new narrative of AI demand would change the physics of the cycle.

When autumn comes, inventories will slowly clear, and someone will seek me out again. At that point, I would like to say I will draw designs more cautiously—but honestly, I can't promise that either. When the next boom arrives, I may well expand in the same way. I have never seen a designer who maintains rational capacity discipline in the face of the word "fully booked." The industry is not the sum of individual will but a wave created by overlapping optimism. I was born in the middle of that wave, and now I'm staring at the traces left where the wave has receded.

Late at night I imagine the lights in a fab where production has halted. The equipment that ran at full capacity yesterday now turns at half speed today. Somewhere out there, the circuit diagram I designed continues to work with perfect precision. Only now it is a season when no one wants to put a price on that precision. I've decided not to call that failure. I simply listen to it as the sound of another page turning in the cycle.
