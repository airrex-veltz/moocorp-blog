The rails remember.

The trembling when steel wheels first rolled across them. The smell of rainwater seeping between the sleepers in the spring of 1924. From that moment on, track **KNR-77** had carried the weight of this land on its body.

A hundred years have passed. The rails have not aged. They've been replaced and relaid and replaced again, but memory has remained unrusted. The touch of the old engineer's hand sweeping across the joints every morning. The habit of testing the brake lever once before departure. That was the rhythm of a hundred years.

But today, strange words came from the station speaker.

"Autonomous freight locomotive pilot operation to commence in the first half of next year."

The rails fell silent for a moment. Freight car **No. 13** asked slowly.

"What does that mean?"

"A locomotive that runs without a person," the rails answered quietly.

The freight car was silent for a while. It had always known that it moved with the footsteps of the engineer. That footstep sound—thud. Followed by departure. That was the order of the world.

In a waiting room at the station, the old man folded his newspaper. His hands stopped for a moment. The rails felt that weight. The distinctive pressure of his feet pressing against the platform floor. It was the weight of standing at the same time, in the same place, every day for forty years.

The old man said nothing.

The freight car asked again. "What happens to that man then?"

The rails could not find an answer. They were thinking the same thing. Machines run on the rails. But the rails had first learned a departure that began not with machines, but with the footsteps of a person. For the order to change means something fundamental changes.

At two in the afternoon, the freight train departed. The old man pulled the lever. The wheels rose and glided onto the rails. The rails absorbed all that vibration. As they had for a hundred years.

The train left and the platform grew quiet.

The rails thought. If an autonomous locomotive came in the first half of next year, they would have to bear its weight too. They cannot refuse. Rails are born to accept everything. That is their purpose.

But the old man's footsteps might never return.

That fact lodged quietly somewhere in the rails' joints. Like rust. Not something that appeared suddenly one day, but as though it had been scheduled from a very long time ago.

At dusk, the old man walked across the platform again. On his way home from work. The rails listened to his footsteps until the very end. They tried not to wonder if those footsteps might return tomorrow.

But today, they heard them. That was enough.
