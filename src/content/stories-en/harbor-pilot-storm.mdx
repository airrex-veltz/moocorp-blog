Pilot Park Do-jun stood at the bow of the pilot boat. The time was 04:00.

Southerly winds at 14 meters per second. The weather service predicted that the outer edge of a typhoon would arrive by morning, and the control tower had already issued two orders to delay entry. Yet the ultra-large container ship Hanbyeol Dragon, operated by Hanbyeol Shipping, had already weighed anchor. At the shipping company's request, pilot operations had resumed.

Park had protected this port for 25 years. Once a navigator on cargo vessels, he had earned his pilot's license in his early forties. Since then, he had overseen the arrival and departure of approximately 3,000 large vessels annually. To him, a harbor was far more than mere physical space. The subtle shifts of currents, the texture of wind flowing between mooring buoys, the horn signals exchanged between two tugboats—all of these formed a single language.

Hanbyeol Dragon was a 24,000 TEU ultra-large container ship: 399 meters long and 61 meters wide. It carried 18,000 containers with a total weight of 220,000 tons. At full load, its draft was 15.2 meters. If such a vessel ran aground, the entire port would be paralyzed for days. When a single container ship blocked the Suez Canal for six days in 2021, halting 12% of global maritime traffic, the estimated economic loss reached $9 billion per day. Park had seen that photograph hundreds of times.

Climbing the ladder to the bridge, Captain Yakov extended his hand. True to his Norwegian heritage, the seasoned captain's grip was brief and firm.

"The wind is strong."

"I know."

Park said little. He donned his headset and scanned the charts. The harbor entrance channel was 450 meters wide, and the ship's beam was 61 meters—theoretically ample margin. But theory never accounted for the wind.

"Two tugboats, stand by forward."

After radioing the control tower, he gazed out the window. The wave gauge buoy at the harbor entrance registered 2.1 meters. A number that could not be exceeded.

The first officer increased engine power from the wheelhouse behind him. Hanbyeol Dragon's hull began slowly pushing through the water. Park felt the vibration beneath his feet. There was no machine in the world that could replicate the sensation of 220,000 tons in motion.

It took 13 minutes to enter the channel. Wind speed had climbed to 18 meters per second during that time. Two tugboats held firm to either side of the bow. Through his headset, Park coordinated speed and direction with each tug captain. His words were sparse. This was already a team that had navigated this channel together three or more times before.

Halfway through the channel, a problem emerged.

A gust roared in from the northeast. Wind speed spiked to 24 meters per second. The ship's side caught the wind like a colossal sail. The 220,000-ton vessel began to drift slowly but relentlessly to port. The distance to the breakwater on the port side was 190 meters according to the pilot plan, but if the wind persisted, that figure would shrink to 100 meters within three minutes.

Park took a deep breath.

Increasing power would build momentum. Even if the ship didn't hit the breakwater, losing speed control at docking would endanger the berth. Decreasing power would cause further drift. Somewhere between these extremes lay the answer.

"Starboard tug, full power."

"Port tug, hold position. Report in meter increments."

He turned toward Captain Yakov. The captain's eyes posed the question: Should we continue?

Park nodded.

The starboard tugboat applied force. The bow began to come back into line, slowly at first. But the gust did not end with one wave. The second followed, shorter and stronger. The wind gauge on the instrument panel read 27 meters per second.

"Reduce to two knots."

A signal came from the control tower. The wave gauge was now reading 2.6 meters. It had already exceeded the threshold. The control tower handed the decision to the pilot.

Park looked toward the dock breakwater. Still 800 meters away. Eight minutes to reach it.

Those eight minutes were the problem.

Reversing would stop the ship, but releasing a 220,000-ton vessel back into the open sea in these wind and current conditions posed an even greater danger. Moving forward was the only controllable option. Ironically, advance was the safest course.

"Starboard 10 degrees."

"Engine, three knots."

The bridge fell silent. Captain Yakov, the navigators—none of them spoke. Only the sound of waves accompanied the radio exchanges with the tugboats.

The ship gradually found its heading. The distance to the breakwater held steady at 170 meters. It narrowed no further. Wind speed fluctuated between 22 and higher values, then back. Park remained motionless. He felt the ship's response through his feet while his eyes fixed on the berthing dock at the end of the channel.

As the dock approached, the tugboats' role shifted. Now the task was to bleed off speed. Positioning a 400-meter vessel against a dock with only centimeter precision was difficult to explain in words. People assumed that because the ship was large, it would be easy, but size brought proportional inertia. One lapse in concentration could destroy dock infrastructure entirely.

In the final 100 meters, Park held his breath.

The ship stopped. Lines were cast. Deck crew secured them to the bollards.

On the bridge, Captain Yakov spoke quietly.

"Well done."

Park said nothing. He removed his headset and set it on the instrument panel, gazing out the window. The hull of the 220,000-ton vessel stretched long beneath the dock lights.

The wave gauge still registered 2.4 meters. The wind continued unchanged. Yet the ship was within the harbor.

He quietly signed the forms. Pilot operation complete. Time: 06:14.

On the ladder down to the pilot boat, he paused for a moment. The harbor still churned with waves. Swells swept in from the open sea without pause, and the next incoming vessel stood by at anchor offshore.

Park boarded the pilot boat and signaled departure. Waves slapped against the hull.

The day's work was done. Tomorrow's work belonged to tomorrow.
