November 21, 1997, 4 a.m. Conference room at Raon Mobility headquarters in Yangjae-dong, Seoul.

"Default."

The CFO's voice cut through the frozen air. Red numbers lined the report on the table. Short-term debt: KRW 8 trillion. Days until maturity: 14.

Vice Chairman Jung Mong-gu looked out the window. Somewhere far away in the Ulsan plant, Sonatas were still rolling down the assembly line. Twenty-three thousand employees would still come to work tomorrow morning. But two weeks from now?

"Here's the restructuring plan."

The HR director pushed a thick file across the table. Ten thousand layoffs. Two of five factories closed. Withdrawal of overseas subsidiaries.

"No." Vice Chairman Jung's voice was low but resolute. "We're not laying off anyone."

"Then the company will collapse!"

"If it collapses, we'll rebuild it. But people don't come back."

The conference room stirred. It was a mad decision. Under IMF management, every major corporation was wielding the restructuring knife. Sejin, Mir, SK—all had let thousands go.

November 22, 9 a.m. Vice Chairman Jung headed to Ulsan.

Two thousand employees had gathered in the plaza outside the factory. Someone was holding a picket that read "No layoffs." Vice Chairman Jung stood before them without a microphone.

"We're not laying off anyone."

The murmur stopped.

"Instead, I ask something of you. For six months, take a 30% salary cut. Weekend overtime will be unpaid. And..." He took a deep breath. "Each of you must become a salesman."

"What about you, Vice Chairman?" someone called out.

"I'll take a 50% cut. And I'll sell my car." He pointed to the Grandeur in the parking lot. "From now on, I'll drive a Sonata. I'll show you with my own body that we're proud of our car."

November 23. Something remarkable happened.

Out of 23,000 employees, 22,000 signed the agreement. Most of those who refused left a note saying "If the company is struggling, you can cut even more."

But salary cuts alone weren't enough. Two weeks away, the KRW 8 trillion in corporate bonds coming due had to be addressed somehow.

December 1. Vice Chairman Jung boarded a flight to New York.

His destination: Goldman Sachs. The investment bankers spoke coldly. "Default probability for Raon Mobility: 85%. Investment not possible."

"Look at our employees." Vice Chairman Jung unfolded photos of the Ulsan plant. "They're giving up their salaries to build cars. There's no way a company like this can collapse."

"It's moving, but the numbers don't work."

"Then I'll make the numbers work."

December 3 night. On the flight back to Seoul, he calculated through dawn. Parts unit cost: 12% reduction. Logistics costs: 18% savings. Marketing budget: 50% cut. He wouldn't touch R&D. The moment the company gave up on the future, it was finished.

December 5. D-day.

The corporate bonds of KRW 8 trillion coming due. Without repayment, Raon Mobility would default that instant.

10 a.m. Creditors' meeting.

"I request a six-month maturity extension," Vice Chairman Jung proposed.

"What's the collateral?" asked the head of the Industrial Bank.

"The trust of every Raon Mobility employee and officer."

The conference room froze. It was nonsense.

Just then, the conference room door opened. It was the Ulsan plant union chairman. He set a thick stack of documents on the table.

"Bank statements from all employees. For the next six months, we'll automatically transfer 30% of salaries to corporate bond purchases. The total is KRW 420 billion."

The creditors stirred.

"And this..." The union chairman pulled out another envelope. "A signed agreement to waive interim severance payments. Signed by 22,000 people."

The head of the Industrial Bank looked at the documents for a long time. Then he looked up.

"Six-month grace period. Approved."

December 6. Raon Mobility survived.

But the real fight was just beginning. They had to produce results within six months. Vice Chairman Jung sent a message to all employees.

"From now on, we are not aiming to be Korea's best. We are aiming for the world's best."

Employees thought he was insane. A company on the verge of default aiming for the world's best.

But six months later, in June 1998.

Raon Mobility's exports increased 40% year-over-year. They didn't miss the opportunity that came from sudden exchange rate gains providing price competitiveness. Employees worked without weekends. They received 30% less salary, but productivity increased 50%.

And ten years later, in 2008.

Raon Mobility had become the world's fifth-largest automobile company. The 23,000 people who hadn't been laid off that crisis moment in 1997 had grown to 70,000.

Chairman Jung Mong-gu sometimes recalls those days. The 4 a.m. conference room. The CFO's voice saying "Default." And the decision he made.

When you protect people, people protect the company.

It was the most precious lesson Raon Mobility learned.
