People say the deep ocean is silent. That's wrong.

Kangmin Heo was listening to that sound in the instrument room of the repair ship Haelyong, headphones over his ears. When a fiber optic cable snaps, there is no sound of photons disappearing. Instead, the measuring device spits out a single tiny number. A reflection loss value. When that number appeared on his screen, he set down his coffee cup.

"Section K-7, signal lost."

It was a Bukseong Link cable. One of the fiber optic trunk lines connecting two nations. Kangmin Heo had been repairing undersea cables for eleven years, and in those eleven years he had never memorized a cable number. To memorize a number meant something was repeating itself.

The ship sailed north for four days. Water depth around seventy meters. The winter seabed is cold and visibility poor. It took half a day for the ROV (remotely operated vehicle) to descend and find the cable. Kangmin Heo watched the monitor, tracking the image feed from the ROV camera. Silt, silt, silt, and then the cable.

The cable was severed. The cross-section was clean.

Cables broken by wear or corrosion show ragged cross-sections. If caught in fishing nets, they leave scratches on the outer sheath. This was different. It had the form of something heavy pressing down and then dragging. An anchor. Kangmin Heo wrote in the first line of the accident report: "External impact, anchor suspected." It was a section overlapping shipping lanes, and winter brings thick fog. A common accident.

It was four days later.

This time it was a different cable. A Hanbyeol Undersea Communications trunk line. 190 kilometers northwest of the K-7 damage point. Water depth fifty-five meters. Same sea region, different location.

The ship had not yet finished repairs on K-7. Kangmin Heo opened the two incident reports side by side. The two cables were owned by different companies. The two cables ran through different countries. The time difference between the damages was seventeen hours.

"That's strange."

He muttered to himself. His senior colleague, Byungki Jo, looked up.

"What is?"

"For one anchor to cut both cables, it would need to travel 190 kilometers in seventeen hours."

Jo pulled out a calculator. "Eleven knots."

"There's no reason for a merchant ship to drop anchor while moving that slowly."

Silence.

Kangmin Heo opened the AIS vessel position data. AIS is a system that automatically transmits a ship's location. Most merchant vessels keep this signal on at all times, but some turn it off. Legally, that's a violation. In reality, it often goes unnoticed.

Twenty kilometers radius from the K-7 damage point, six hours before and after the estimated time of damage. He compiled a list of vessels in that range. Twelve ships total. Of those, eleven had kept their AIS signals on continuously. One ship had intermittent signal. A cargo vessel. Its port of origin and destination were listed, but that route did not match its position.

He performed the same analysis on the Hanbyeol Undersea Communications damage point.

The same vessel was within that range.

Kangmin Heo stared at the screen for a long time. He did not smoke, but at that moment he felt the urge to go out on deck and get some air. The air in the instrument room suddenly felt cramped.

When cables are cut, international data flows change. Bandwidth decreases, routes reroute. It is not merely a communication inconvenience. Financial transaction delays, shifts in military communication pathways, increased dependence on satellite bandwidth. There was something in a different category between one cable being cut and two cables being cut days apart by the same actor.

He opened the draft report file.

One field for describing the accident circumstances, another for the estimated cause. In the second field, he typed: "Anchor impact possible, but vessel AIS trajectory and damage pattern do not match a simple accident. Further investigation required." Then he paused at the cursor.

This report would go to both companies. It would go to the telecommunications regulators of both nations. It might go further than that.

He looked out the window. The northern sea was lead-colored. A single gull bobbed on the waves, then took flight.

Kangmin Heo turned back to his keyboard. He would write only facts. Measurements, coordinates, times, vessel list. Interpretation was not his responsibility. Or rather, it was difficult to judge whether it was his responsibility. He had been repairing cables for eleven years. There were others who traced why they broke.

But tonight, he opened the cable cross-section photo once more. The clean cross-section. He stared at it for a long time. The photons had disappeared. And he knew that something had existed before those photons vanished.
