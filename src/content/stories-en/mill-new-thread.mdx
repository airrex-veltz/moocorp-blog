Chung-sil Textile first drew thread in 1973.

For a little more than half a century since then, the looms had never stopped. Oh Min-jun was born within that vibration. At five in the morning, the sound of the machinery traveled up through the floor of the house, arriving before the alarm clock. His father would say, "When the machines run, we eat." That simple equation was this family's economics.

Oh Min-jun inherited the factory fifteen years ago. Even then, there were twelve employees. It was a simple flow: buy cotton yarn, weave cloth, and deliver the woven fabric to garment factories. Even if exchange rates fluctuated or cotton prices spiked in the middle, that flow never broke. When orders piled up, the machines ran faster. When orders decreased, they slowed down. That was all there was to it.

But in late summer 2024, word came that textile factories in Vietnam and Bangladesh had completed large-scale expansions. International cotton futures prices fell 18 percent in a single month. Consumers got cheaper clothes. Large apparel brands sent notices demanding even lower unit prices. For small domestic textile mills like Chung-sil, those numbers meant something different. They were already losing in price competition, and that distance widened once again.

By November, the looms had stopped.

Oh Min-jun sat in a worn chair in the corner of the factory for the third month, staring at the account books. The workforce had been reduced to three. Five looms: three had been sold to scrap dealers. The remaining two stood in the warehouse—the ones his father had bought directly from Osaka. He couldn't sell them. It was different somehow.

A buyer appeared around that time.

It was a woman named Choi Yu-jin who ran a materials development startup in Seoul. Her car was not expensive and her speech was fast and precise. What she wanted was not machinery or space.

"I need work that weaves carbon fiber and natural fiber together. It's a prototype composite material for aerospace, but existing automated equipment doesn't have the precision we need."

Oh Min-jun turned her words over in his mind three times. Aerospace. Composite materials. Could looms that had been producing cotton shirt fabric for half a century manage something like that?

"I've never tried it."

"That's why I'm here."

Oh Min-jun started to refuse, then stopped. He looked toward the looms in the warehouse. The smell of metal, the smell of oil. Somewhere in between, it felt like he could smell something like his father's sweat.

Two days later, he took out his work clothes.

The first prototype failed. Carbon fiber was heavier than cotton yarn and had completely different tension. Using traditional weft methods, uniform fabric structure wouldn't form. If a single thread broke, he had to start over from the beginning. Oh Min-jun changed the pattern through the night, and Choi Yu-jin came back the next day. And the day after that.

After a month had passed, Choi Yu-jin handed him a report sheet. Tensile strength at 94 percent of the target. A figure far exceeding what had been discussed at the beginning.

"How did you manage it?"

Oh Min-jun couldn't answer. He hadn't invented or developed anything. He simply knew, with half a century's worth of sensation, how thread moved. That sensation found its way bit by bit even before the new material.

After two months had passed, the looms were turning again. The sound was different from before. Slower and more careful. But something was being woven within it.

Oh Min-jun didn't know where this would lead. Whether the prototype would lead to a mass production contract, what form Chung-sil would take, or whether Choi Yu-jin's startup could attract investment on that single report—no one knew. Nothing had yet been settled in a contract.

But the looms were turning. At five in the morning, that sound traveled up through the floor.

Oh Min-jun opened his eyes. The sky was still dark.
