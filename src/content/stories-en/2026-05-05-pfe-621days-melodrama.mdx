Friday, January 31, 2020, evening.

The 42nd floor of Nano Vaccine's Boston headquarters. Dr. Park Ji-su stood gazing out the window. Beyond the Hudson River, the sun was setting, but her eyes saw nothing. Only the WHO report in her hand came into focus.

"International public health emergency."

At the same moment, in a small biotech company called Geno Bio in Berlin, Germany, Dr. Lee Jun-seok remained in the laboratory with his wife and colleague Kim Soo-hyun. Both scientists of Middle Eastern immigrant background had devoted their lives to cancer vaccine research.

"Our technology will be effective," Kim Soo-hyun said.

"Nano vaccine technology has never been approved before," Lee Jun-seok replied. "But if not now, when would we ever try?"

Two months later, Nano Vaccine and Geno Bio joined hands. A meeting between a pharmaceutical giant with 170 years of history and a 12-year-old startup. No one could guarantee success. Nano vaccine technology was theoretically possible, but had never been used on actual people even once.

"If we fail, the company could collapse," the CFO warned.

Nano Vaccine CEO Lee Jun-ho shook his head. "If we don't do this, who will?"

Research proceeded at a frantic pace. Phase 1 trials, Phase 2, then Phase 3. 44,000 volunteers participated. Half received the vaccine, half received a placebo. No one knew which group they were in.

Sunday morning, November 8, 2020.

Dr. Park Ji-su jolted awake at the sound of her phone ringing. It was the head of statistics.

"Doctor... it's 90 percent."

"Ninety percent of what?"

"The efficacy. Vaccine efficacy exceeded 90 percent."

Her hand holding the phone trembled. She had spent 30 years researching vaccines. She knew that flu vaccine efficacy was typically 40 to 60 percent. 90 percent was... a miracle.

But the joy was brief. Production was the problem. To supply 8 billion people worldwide with the vaccine, tens of billions of doses were needed. Nano Vaccine ran factories in the Netherlands, the United States, and Germany at full capacity 24 hours a day. Employees worked shifts and collapsed from exhaustion.

"Go home and rest," the factory manager said.

"Tomorrow, someone's parents might be saved by this vaccine," a worker replied. "There's no time to rest."

Minus 70 degrees. Nano Vaccine's vaccine could only be stored at ultra-low temperatures. The logistics team had to build an unprecedented cold chain. Special freezers, dry ice, GPS tracking systems. If even one thing went wrong, the vaccine would become useless liquid.

In the summer of 2021, the Delta variant swept across the world.

"Is our vaccine useless?" investors asked.

CEO Lee Jun-ho handed over the data. "It still prevents severe illness and death over 90 percent of the time. We continue."

621 days.

The time that elapsed from the first emergency authorization to full approval. During that period, the Nano Vaccine-Geno Bio vaccine supplied over 3 billion doses worldwide. It saved millions of lives.

On August 23, 2021, when Dr. Park Ji-su heard the news of official authorization from the health authority, she sat in a corner of the laboratory and wept. It was not joy. It was relief. And gratitude for the miracle that all the scientists, factory workers, logistics coordinators, and clinical trial volunteers had created together.

Lee Jun-seok and Kim Soo-hyun were still in their small laboratory in Berlin. Now they had gained worldwide recognition, but their work remained the same. Researching the next vaccine. Saving the next lives.

"We did it," Kim Soo-hyun said.

"No," Lee Jun-seok shook his head. "We all did."

Evening sunlight streamed through the window. The long night of 621 days had passed, and light was returning to the world once again.
