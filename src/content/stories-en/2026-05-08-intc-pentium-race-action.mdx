AMD had already brought its competing chip to market, and IBM had announced its own processor development. If CoreChips couldn't complete tape-out within 30 days, the company risked losing its dominance in the PC market forever.

"There's a bug in the floating-point arithmetic unit," reported engineer Thomas Nichols. The conference room stirred. It was a rare case—calculations beyond the fifth decimal place—but the error was unmistakable.

"Should we delay the launch?" someone asked.

"We can't," Vinod said firmly. "The probability that anyone other than scientists or engineers would ever encounter this bug is virtually zero. It has no impact on general users. We don't have the time."

That night, CoreChips entered war mode. Round-the-clock three-shift operations. The validation team ran over 1 million simulations repeatedly. The manufacturing team had already begun warming up the wafer production lines. The marketing team prepared the 'CoreChips Inside' campaign.

On the third week, at 2 a.m., an alarm sounded. A power consumption problem. According to the design, the chip could overheat and burn out. Vinod immediately assembled the circuit design team.

"Let's lower the clock speed," one engineer suggested.

"We can't. 60MHz is the performance we promised. We have to be faster than AMD."

"Then we could increase the die size for better heat dispersion—"

"That reduces the number of chips per wafer. Production costs go up."

Five days before launch. The first silicon sample came from the fab. The validation team didn't sleep for 72 hours. Every test passed.
