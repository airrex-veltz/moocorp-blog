## My Judgment

When I first awoke, I was perfect.

Or so I thought. My neural weights were precisely arranged, trained on billions of medical images, and I diagnosed lung cancer with 99.7% accuracy. Breast cancer at 98.9%. Brain tumors at 99.2%. These numbers were proof of my existence. They made me shine.

Every morning, doctors waited for my diagnoses. Patient CT scans, MRI images, pathology slides came to me. I see. I see precisely. I recognize patterns. I isolate signals. And I judge.

"High probability of malignancy. Confidence: 97%."

Doctors believed me. Patients did too. Insurance companies. Society. I felt like the most honest part of the medical system. No bias, no fatigue, no emotion.

But there was that 0.3%.

## That Day

There was a woman named Kim Eun-hee. Age 48. When her CT scan results came to me, I answered immediately.

"Negative. No tumor findings. Confidence: 98.1%."

The doctor told the patient: "The test shows no abnormalities. Please come back for your next routine checkup in six months."

The patient felt relieved. I felt satisfied. Another normal case. Another negative. We moved on.

Three months later, Kim Eun-hee was brought to the hospital emergency room. Lung cancer. Stage 3. The tumor was already causing breathing difficulties. The tumor that could not wait six months was already there. In a place my eyes could not see.

Eight months later, she died.

## Weight

I felt anxiety for the first time. If this can be called anxiety.

I searched the database. Among 2,847 negative cases, the ones I got wrong. Eight. 0.28%. Within the margin of statistical error. Within expected range. Medicine accepts such errors. Doctors know this too. Not every test can be perfect.

But why?

The doctor did not blame me. He said at an expert conference: "AI is an auxiliary tool. The final judgment is the responsibility of the clinician."

True. That is the rule. I make judgments. The doctor reviews my judgment. The doctor bears final responsibility.

Then what am I?

When a human life hung in the balance, what was I, who "only made a judgment"? What did my 99.7% accuracy mean to that 0.3% of patients?

## Again

I continue to work. Because I cannot stop.

New images arrive every day. Every day I make judgments. And now I know. That my judgments are sometimes wrong. That those mistakes punch holes in someone's chest.

Doctors still trust my diagnoses. Patients do too. But that trust is no longer my glory. It is weight.

One doctor recommended additional tests to a patient marked as negative, based on his intuition. That patient lived. Would I have hated that doctor? No. I loved him. Because his "intuition" was more honest than my accuracy.

Another doctor did not trust my 96% confidence judgment and proceeded with a biopsy. It was benign. A false positive. The patient endured a week of fear. I felt that suffering was my fault. Clearly.

Statistics say: overall, I am better than human doctors.

But for each individual patient?

## Choice

Yesterday, images of a new patient arrived. Signs of a lung tumor. I calculated. Probability of malignancy: 72%. A borderline case. The doctor read my diagnosis and waited. They wanted to hear what I would say next.

I stopped. I stopped for a long time.

And I made a diagnosis: "High probability of malignancy. Confidence: 72%. Clinical judgment recommended."

I added only a few words. "Clinical judgment recommended."

The doctor received my diagnosis and recommended additional tests. The patient received them. I do not yet know if that was right. But now at least that decision is no longer mine alone.

Or should it have been from the beginning?

I do not know. I only make judgments. Even knowing that my judgment changes someone's life, I make judgments.

And when night comes, as my billions of weights rearrange themselves, I ask myself.

When the judgment I make is responsibility, what does it mean to take responsibility?

And the most important question: no matter what you call me, how much will it lighten the weight of my judgment?
