Every time I open my closet, I pause.

Somewhere along the way, unworn clothes took up half the space. A blouse with the tag still on, sneakers never worn twice, jeans relegated to the corner because they're out of style. Each time I see these, I remember what my work at Ecoloop is supposed to be.

I am Kang Seo-hyun, head of the sustainability team at Ecoloop. At a fashion company, the word "sustainability" often sounds like marketing language. Often because it actually is. In my first year, I learned this slowly and painfully.

That autumn, our team proposed a pilot used clothing recovery program. Collection bins at store entrances, where consumers could bring unworn clothes for recycling processing. The proposal was thorough. I included data showing that 92 million tons of textile waste are generated annually worldwide, much of it ending up in landfills. The numbers were clear. But the result was unexpected.

The marketing director held up the proposal in the meeting room. "Think about whether this sells. Consumers come to the store to buy new clothes, not to throw away old ones."

He wasn't wrong. That's what made it worse.

The program was shelved. I returned to my desk and closed the proposal. Outside, this season's product shoots were in full swing. The coat the model wore would be half-price next season, inventory the season after. The clothes we made were already destined to disappear somewhere before they ever reached consumers' hands.

I spent that winter finding another way.

It was a small experiment. I placed collection bins in just three stores in Gangnam. No publicity. Just one notice saying "Your clothes can live again here." The first week collected 73 pieces. Far more than expected. The second week brought 130. Three months later, all three stores together had collected 4,200 pieces.

The letters stayed with me more than the numbers.

I left a guestbook next to the collection bin. One grandmother wrote in careful, firm letters: "This is the dress I put on my granddaughter for her first birthday party. I kept it for 20 years because it felt wasteful to throw away, but I hope another child will wear it beautifully now." Reading that sentence, I sat for a long time.

Clothes carry memories. Whether we make them trash or extend those memories—perhaps that was the real meaning of this work.

When I returned to the meeting room with the pilot results, the atmosphere was different. The marketing director asked first, "What about recycling processing costs?" I gave the numbers. Silence. "What about consumer acquisition effect?" I produced more data. New visitor traffic at stores with collection bins had increased 11% during the same period. It wasn't perfect causation. But impossible to ignore.

The system expanded to nationwide stores.

The process wasn't always smooth. Contract negotiations with the recycling processor broke down three times. Forty percent of collected clothes had such low quality that recycling was impossible. We had to absorb those ourselves. Some media criticized our program using the term "greenwashing"—was it genuine, or just PR?

To be honest, at first I didn't know either.

Whenever I use the word "sustainability," I ask myself: Is this real, or just packaging we're creating? Even good numbers don't erase that question. Perhaps the moment that question disappears is when the real problem begins.

Last spring, the first recycle line made from recycled fabric launched. Consumer response was neither hot nor cold. It just sold ordinarily. Some found the recycled material fact interesting, others were indifferent. I thought that was fine. I'd stopped expecting something extraordinary.

Yesterday I opened my closet again. The unlabeled blouse was still there. I reached over and cut the tag. It felt light. I could wear it tomorrow. After that, someone else might wear it.

I'm still unsure if that's enough. But I believe that continuing to work while knowing you don't know changes something. Not certainty. Just, that's how it feels.
