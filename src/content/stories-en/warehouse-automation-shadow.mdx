5 a.m. The first truck pulled into Hanbit Logistics' warehouse on the outskirts of Incheon.

Sung-hun Kim remembers that vibration. The heavy tremor traveling up through the concrete floor to the soles of his feet, forklift engine roar, brief greetings from coworkers. He'd worked in this warehouse for twenty-two years. Not a single day missed.

But this morning, the sound of the truck was followed by something different: the quiet hum of an electric motor.

---

Team leader Ji-su Park was already at the site. She stood by the entrance to Section A, clipboard in hand, but her expression was off. Her lip was trembling, and her grip on the clipboard was too tight.

"Cheongram Automation equipment starts trial operation today. All of Section A."

She said it first. Not to Sung-hun but into the air.

Sung-hun looked toward the entrance. Flat yellow machines were rolling in single file. About knee-high. Each carried a shelf bearing a QR code on its back, sliding across the floor without sound. No breath. No footsteps.

"How many?"

"Eight. Twenty-four next month."

Sung-hun said nothing.

---

At lunchtime, Team leader Park came to a corner table where Sung-hun was sitting alone, lunchbox in hand.

"Mind if I sit?"

Instead of answering, he turned his gaze out the window. Park sat down.

"Head office gave the orders." She paused. "If I had a choice, I wouldn't be doing this this fast."

"Is 'not having a choice' an excuse?" Sung-hun's voice was quiet. Closer to exhaustion than anger.

Park closed her lunchbox. She wouldn't eat.

According to industry surveys, logistics centers that install automated transport equipment see hourly throughput increase to two to three times the previous baseline. The gap widens during peak season before and after major holidays. The numbers were clear. It was a kind of fight humans couldn't win.

"Do you know how much volume those machines handle?" Sung-hun asked.

"Yes."

"Then how many of us are left?"

Park didn't answer. Couldn't answer. The numbers already existed, and the moment those numbers left her mouth, they became irreversible.

---

In the afternoon, Sung-hun worked next to a robot for the first time.

The device moved along a predetermined path. It stopped exactly where it was supposed to stop, started exactly when it was supposed to start. No errors. No fatigue. No aching shoulders or swollen ankles.

Sung-hun's hands grabbed boxes the way twenty-two years had taught him. Reading the weight distribution first, predicting the tilt, making space for the next worker to move in. These were things only someone who knew this space could do. The robot didn't do them. It didn't need to.

By evening, Team leader Park came to Sung-hun's line.

"Headquarters is opening an automation operations manager position. Preference given to workers with field experience."

Sung-hun kept sorting boxes without stopping.

"You want me to become someone who manages machines?"

"No one knows this site like you do."

It wasn't wrong. That was what made it heavy.

---

On the way home, Sung-hun sat on a bench outside the warehouse for a moment.

The direction the wind entered, the point where drops fell from the ceiling vents, the spots where you couldn't stand when a forklift turned a corner. He'd never explained these things in words. He knew them in his body and taught them to others the same way. These were things the warehouse remembered.

Team leader Park's offer circled in his head.

Robots build data. But some things disappear before they become data. Sung-hun knew that. Whether it could be a reason to stay, he still didn't know.
