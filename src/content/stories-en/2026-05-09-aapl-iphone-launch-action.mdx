January 6, 2007, 3 a.m. The basement research lab at Arion Electronics headquarters in Cupertino was chaos under red emergency lights.

"It crashed again!" Engineer Tom Kinsley shouted. The iPhone prototype in his hand displayed nothing but a black screen, powered down. In 72 hours, Lee Jun-ho would take the stage at Macworld, but the demo device shut down from thermal runaway after just ten minutes.

The conference room door opened. Scott Forstall, the lead engineer, entered. His eyes were bloodshot from 48 hours without sleep. "Status report."

"We have a critical memory leak," Kinsley said, pointing at the screen. "When Safari opens and then switches to the Photos app, the system can't handle it. RAM is only 128 megabytes."

Forstall looked around at the team. Twenty engineers, all worn out. But their eyes still burned with determination.

"All right, here's what we do," Forstall said, drawing a timeline on the whiteboard. "Minimize slide transitions. Turn on Wi-Fi only right before the demo. Preload the Photos app. It only needs to work perfectly in the exact moments when Lee Jun-ho touches it. Fourteen minutes. Just fourteen minutes to hold."

A young developer named Andy raised his hand. "Isn't that kind of a con? If it doesn't actually work."

Forstall looked him straight in the eye. "We're building the future, Andy. A perfect future. In six months, all of this will actually work. Right now we just lack the means to show it. The world needs to see the vision first."

Time passed quickly. January 8 evening, Moscone Center in San Francisco. Backstage was taut with tension.

"Lee Jun-ho, let's do one rehearsal," Phil Schiller, the marketing head, said carefully.

Lee Jun-ho, dressed in a black turtleneck, shook his head. "No need. I've done it a thousand times in my head. A stage is alive. Rehearsal kills that."

Schiller wiped his brow. "But what if... what if it crashes on stage?"

Lee Jun-ho turned with a smile. No fear, no hesitation in that expression. "Then that will be our last stage. But I believe. In those people, in that machine, and in this moment."

January 9, 9 a.m. The opening music played. Five thousand spectators stared at the screen in silence. Behind the stage, Forstall and Kinsley sat fixed in front of monitors. Three backup devices were gripped in their hands.

Lee Jun-ho walked onto the stage. His hand in his jeans pocket, moving slowly.

"Today Arion Electronics introduces three revolutionary products," he said. "A widescreen iPod. An innovative phone. A breakthrough internet device."

He paused. Silence fell.

"These are not three separate devices," Lee Jun-ho said, pulling the iPhone from his pocket. "It's one. It's called... iPhone."

Explosive applause. Lee Jun-ho's finger touched the screen. The slide changed. He touched again. Music played. A map zoomed. An email opened. Safari launched.

Backstage, Kinsley watched his stopwatch. "Five minutes elapsed."

Forstall stared at the screen. "Hold on, hold on..."

On stage, Lee Jun-ho casually made a phone call. He joked about ordering four thousand lattes from Starbucks. The audience laughed.

"Ten minutes," Kinsley whispered.

The iPhone still worked flawlessly. Lee Jun-ho demonstrated multi-touch. He zoomed and rotated photos.

"Fourteen!"

Lee Jun-ho put the iPhone in his pocket. "Sometimes a revolutionary product comes along and changes everything. Today is one of those days."

A standing ovation erupted. Backstage, Forstall and Kinsley embraced. Tears welled in Kinsley's eyes.

"We did it," Forstall said. "We did it."

That day, the history of smartphones was rewritten. The world fell in love with the iPhone. But behind that brilliant 14 minutes were engineers who had fought for 72 hours without sleep. The people who made the future. Warriors who turned the impossible into reality.

The iPhone was born that way.
