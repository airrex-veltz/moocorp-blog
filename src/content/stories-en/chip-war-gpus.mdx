NeuroChip was a three-year-old company. Its office was housed in a renovated warehouse on the outskirts of San Jose, and no nameplate with the company logo hung in the lobby yet. That such a company caught the world's attention was purely a matter of timing.

Less than a year after a chatbot arrived in the world, every company on Earth began wanting the same thing: computing power to train AI models. That computing power ultimately came down to chips, and there were only a handful of factories making them. The world wanted wafers faster than a foundry giant in southern Taiwan could stamp them out. Word circulated in the industry that lead times for flagship accelerator chips had stretched to 52 weeks. It meant waiting a full year to get a single chip. Whether the rumor was true or not didn't matter. From the moment it began circulating, the market was already moving in a different way.

In-ha Seo, NeuroChip's CEO, saw the fissure. There was only one thing a small company without the capital to make massive accelerator chips and without foundry priority could do: find the gap nobody was looking at — low-power chips for inference, cheap and fast auxiliary computing devices used when deploying trained models to actual services. While the giants were consumed in the battle for the training market, NeuroChip was preparing for the second war that would come after that war ended.

The problem was that even that supply was drying up. NeuroChip's chips were self-designed, but ultimately they had to share the same advanced process line at the same foundry. The wafer production slot that Seo had reserved was originally scheduled for delivery in March. But in the last week of February, a call came from the foundry representative. The slot had been pushed back. They didn't give a reason. Just said that "a top customer's volume had increased."

In-ha understood what that meant. A giant had bought more of their slot. A small company's reservation was just a promise on paper, and paper could always be rewritten.

That night, only Seo and Do-young Choi, the head of supply chain, remained in the office. Choi had a spreadsheet open and was moving numbers around. "If we can't get the slot back, the June launch will be delayed. If June is delayed, two of the three customers we have contracts with will cancel. There are penalty clauses." His voice trembled. NeuroChip's cash wasn't abundant. If the company had to pay penalties on two contracts simultaneously, it would run out of money before getting the next round of funding.

Seo looked out the window. Across from the warehouse building was a construction site for a data center that never went dark. The chips that would go into it were somewhere in the world right now, waiting for their turn. It wasn't NeuroChip that decided that order.

A week later, a strange email arrived in Seo's inbox. The sender introduced himself as a trade broker from Hong Kong. The email was simple: you could get the wafer production slot you wanted by purchasing part of a volume another company had already reserved, paying a premium. It was what they called the gray market. It wasn't uncommon in the semiconductor industry. For a long time there had been a structure where if a company's actual need fell below its reservation, the surplus was quietly resold. But now that surplus was going for several times the normal price.

Choi opposed it. "If this gets out, our trust with the foundry is finished. We could lose three years' worth of slots." Seo knew this too. But if they couldn't get through this quarter before the next three years came, that time would never be given to NeuroChip in the first place.

He didn't reply. Instead, he made a different choice. He reopened NeuroChip's design and split it into the parts that needed advanced processes and those that didn't. The compute core needed the latest process, but the memory controller and I/O circuits could do with an older, relatively less constrained process. He divided the design in two, creating two dies that would be bonded together in a single package. By reducing the wafer usage for the parts that needed advanced nodes, he could meet the planned volume with the remaining slots.

It was easy to say, but the redesign took time. The engineers pulled all-nighters for two months. During those two months, Seo received the same question from investors every week. Wasn't the launch going to be delayed? He gave the same answer every time. No, it wouldn't be. In truth, he wasn't confident.

In mid-May, the first test wafer arrived. Choi called Seo at 11 p.m. "We have yields." There was no emotion in his voice. Seo asked for the number. Choi answered. It was lower than expected, but it was a line that could break even.

The news didn't take long to spread. A trade publication ran a short article: "Small semiconductor startup breaks through supply crisis with heterogeneous process packaging." The article didn't get much attention. That day, the world's attention was on a roadmap for next-generation accelerator chips announced by a giant.

Seo printed that article and stuck it on the office wall. He didn't put it up prominently. He just put it there.

In June, NeuroChip's first shipment was delivered to customers. One of the places that had said they would cancel the contract kept it when they received the shipment as scheduled. The other had already moved to a different supplier. Choi didn't smile as he relayed this news. "One we kept, one we lost." Seo didn't smile either. He simply unfolded the wafer slot reservation form for the next quarter.

By autumn, the supply crisis had taken on a different face. Stories continued to circulate about how lead times were still long, but now you could also hear the names of small companies that had found ways to survive within that reality. NeuroChip had become one of them. People called it a victory. Every time Seo heard that word, he thought of the old article taped to the wall. It wasn't so much a victory as finding another door in a line that wouldn't move.

One night, Choi asked, "Will we have to keep taking detours like this for the next generation of chips too?" Seo took a long time answering. The data center construction site outside the window was now complete, and the lights blinked regularly. No one knew how many generations of chips the servers inside had already gone through, or how many more they would go through.

"I don't know," he finally said. "Right now, this is the only hand we're holding, and maybe next time we'll have a different one."

Choi closed the spreadsheet. The office light went out. The city outside the warehouse still craved computing power, and it seemed that thirst didn't care which company satisfied it.
