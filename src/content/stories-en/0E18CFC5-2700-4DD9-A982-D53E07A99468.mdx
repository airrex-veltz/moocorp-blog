Park Mu-seok was already at the office by half past six in the morning.

It was less a habit than a ritual. It had been the same when he first stepped into this building 35 years ago, and it remained so now, with fifty years nearly complete. The moment he crossed the threshold, the scent that reached his nose—a distinctive blend of aged paper and server room cooling air—woke him more effectively than any coffee. And the thought that soon he would no longer smell it made it feel somehow more vivid than usual.

He sat at his desk and opened the morning market report. He loved the feel of paper under his fingers. These days, his younger colleagues each carried tablets, and dual monitors crowded desks everywhere, but Park Mu-seok's first act upon arriving was to unfold the printed list of previous day's closing prices on A4 paper. When it touched his fingertips, he felt as though he were touching something real. Numbers should have texture, not just be data—that was something he had believed for a long time.

This morning, a single A4 envelope sat on one corner of his desk. It bore the seal of the Human Resources Department. He didn't need to open it to know what was inside. He had already been notified a month ago. An early retirement application. One line of signature in his own hand, and fifty years would be concluded within that document.

Park Mu-seok slid the envelope into a drawer. Not today.

Through the window, the Han River at Yeouido was visible. The sun had not yet risen, so the water was steel-gray. He had never counted how many orders he had processed while that river flowed like that. Tens of thousands? Hundreds of thousands? It wouldn't have mattered to count. Numbers were not memory. Memory was always people.

He made coffee. Instant coffee from a packet. His younger colleagues used capsule machines or went to nearby cafes, but Park Mu-seok had used the same method for decades. The steam that rose when he poured hot water and stirred slowly was the signal that marked the beginning of his day. It wasn't a matter of preference—it was simply what he was accustomed to. Familiarity, he had long believed, was a form of strength.

---

At nine o'clock sharp, the phone rang.

"Is this Manager Park? I'm Oh Jung-min."

Park Mu-seok checked the caller ID before picking up the receiver. He already knew. A Seoul area code number, a name he had saved for three years. Oh Jung-min. But before him appeared not a code in a customer management file, but a concrete voice and face. Sixty-two years old. Retired from the paper industry for three years. His wife had passed away two years ago, after which he had begun taking an interest in investment. He had tried learning on his own before being connected to Park Mu-seok through an acquaintance.

"Mr. Oh, it's been a while."

"I've been thinking since yesterday whether to call. I'm talking about Sean & Hold Insurance. It fell again yesterday. I'm wondering if I made a mistake getting into it."

Sean & Hold Insurance, **SNHL**. A small regional insurance company. Oh Jung-min had first mentioned this stock four months ago. Back then, Park Mu-seok had been cautious as well. Liquidity was thin, and until profit improvement could be concretely confirmed, the stock's price movement was irregular. On days with low trading volume, even just a few sell orders could move the closing price significantly.

But Oh Jung-min had already made up his mind.

"When I was young, my father was often ill. He spent a lot of time at the hospital, and that company's insurance covered those costs. Of course, I'm not sure if it was called Sean & Hold Insurance back then—it seems to have carried on through a merger. These days, no one even looks at this company. I don't understand why."

When he first heard those words, Park Mu-seok did not try to persuade him. He did not rattle off numbers. Instead, he asked one question.

"How long can you wait?"

"Until I die."

Oh Jung-min said it with a laugh, but Park Mu-seok could sense the expression through the phone. Beneath the laughter, sincerity. Park Mu-seok did not miss that nuance. It was what fifty years of experience had given him. Something that no report could teach.

From that day forward, he reviewed SNHL earnings reports once a week. Looking at the Q2 report this year, the loss ratio had improved slightly for three consecutive quarters. The business ratio was also 1.2 percentage points lower than the same period last year. The market remained indifferent, but the numbers were quietly, steadily changing.

"Yesterday's decline came from foreign selling. There was no news. The earnings trajectory is still holding."

"Should I buy more?"

"That's a decision for you to make, Mr. Oh."

"Why do you always do that?" Oh Jung-min laughed. "You never give your opinion. Everywhere else they tell me whether to buy or sell."

"I gave my opinion. In numbers."

There was a brief silence on the other end of the line. Park Mu-seok waited. Interrupting when a customer is silent is not the work of a good broker. He knew that the decision is made within that silence.

"Please buy another KRW 5 million worth. Match the unit price."

"Understood. I'll process it by the end of today and contact you."

"Thank you, Manager Park."

After hanging up, Park Mu-seok paused before entering the order. What had he said when Oh Jung-min first mentioned SNHL? He had explained the liquidity risk. He had discussed the volatility of small-cap stocks. He had even warned of the possibility of losses. And after hearing all of that, Oh Jung-min had said the same thing. That he would wait until he died.

Park Mu-seok knew the weight of that waiting. He had waited for fifty years too. He didn't know what he had been waiting for, but he knew.

---

After entering the order, Park Mu-seok turned his chair toward the window.

The stock exchange had been fully automated long ago. When he first started in this business, it was different. There were people at the trading windows. There were paper order forms. The signal that an order was executed was not a machine sound but the voice of an employee calling on the phone. When the morning session ended, execution confirmations came down from the floor. Papers scattered on the ground and people shouted. That commotion was the market.

Now there are no people on the exchange floor. Prices are made by algorithms, orders are processed in milliseconds. If a customer enters an order directly through a smartphone app, there's no need for a broker. The place for someone like Park Mu-seok still exists officially, but its meaning had changed quietly, without anyone noticing.

When automation first accelerated, he felt a sense of loss. Then came anger. What was the point of this? He had devoted fifty years to this work, only to learn that machines were doing in 0.001 seconds what he spent all day doing.

But now, well.

Now he thought something else remained.

When he opened a customer file, there were numbers. But behind those numbers was a person. Like in Oh Jung-min's case. His father's medical bills. A wife who had passed away. The solitary hours after retirement. A day in childhood changed by a single insurance policy. The algorithm didn't know such things. It had no reason to. There were no columns for such things on an order form.

Park Mu-seok took another sip of coffee. It was already cold. It didn't matter.

---

Just after two o'clock in the afternoon, a text came from Oh Jung-min.

"Confirmed the execution. Thank you. It went well, right?"

Park Mu-seok paused to think. Did it go well? The morning execution price was 2.1% lower than yesterday's close. That wasn't bad. It had used the timing when selling pressure briefly strengthened at the start of the session. But what would happen to SNHL going forward—no one knew that.

He sent a reply.

"Executed at 2.1% below yesterday's close. We'll continue monitoring the earnings trajectory."

Shortly after, a text came back from Oh Jung-min.

"Manager Park, you always say exactly what's needed. I really like that. Everywhere else they tell me this is going to be a huge win, it's sure to go up, but here my mind feels at ease."

Park Mu-seok rolled those words over in his mind. He had simply stated the facts. But to Oh Jung-min, it seemed to mean something different. It seemed to register not as numbers but as reassurance.

He didn't send a reply. He didn't know what to write. Saying thank you would sound awkward, and saying of course would sound arrogant. So he left it as it was. Perhaps that was the right answer.

---

As evening approached, lights in the office began going out one by one.

His younger colleagues grabbed their bags and left. Some offered greetings; others simply exchanged glances as they passed. Park Mu-seok nodded in response. Long ago, they all used to go out for dinner together when leaving. Now there was no such culture. It wasn't bad. It was just different.

He took one last look at SNHL's status. The close was 1.4% higher than the morning execution price. By daily standards, it was a meaningless number. Oh Jung-min's waiting was measured not in days. But today had ended not badly.

He closed the file. And opened the drawer.

The HR envelope.

Park Mu-seok took it out and placed it on his desk. He had already memorized what was inside. There was a field above the signature line to record the expected retirement date, and below that a space for the department head's stamp. At the very bottom, the applicant's signature. All he needed to do was write his name—three characters in fountain pen.

He looked at the signature line for a moment, then put the envelope back in the drawer.

He did not uncap his fountain pen.

Tomorrow morning at half past six, he would come back here, make coffee, and open the market report. SNHL's earnings announcement was still to come, and Oh Jung-min's investment story was not yet finished. While that story remained unfinished, his own story felt as if it too remained open.

Of course, he wasn't sure if that was a rational reason.

But when Oh Jung-min said he would wait until the end for his father's long-standing insurance company, whether it was a rational reason hadn't mattered either. What mattered was the fact that he waited. And that his waiting was not alone.

The algorithm didn't know that.

Not yet.

The Han River outside the window still flowed in the darkness. The water that had been steel-gray in the morning was now ink-black. Only the color had changed. The direction was the same.

Park Mu-seok turned off the light.
