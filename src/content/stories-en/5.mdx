Choi Eun-ja couldn't remember exactly when she last sat at the window counter. That day had no customers the whole morning.

Hansol Credit Union had been at the Haguri intersection for thirty-seven years. The building was rooted to the earth like an old, familiar landmark that had grown weathered with time. On the front glass door was a sticker reading "Fixed Deposits: 3.5% Annual Rate"—no one knew when it had been put there. Perhaps since the year Choi Eun-ja first began working there.

In 2023, financial regulator statistics revealed that the number of credit unions nationwide had fallen to half what it had been twenty years before. This was the result of closures and mergers. Haguri's Hansol Credit Union was one of the few that had barely survived within that halved population.

At 2:13 that afternoon, an alert sounded from the computer terminal.

KRW 500 million.

Choi Eun-ja looked at the screen once, then looked again. The sender was 'Park Jung-soo.' A man in his mid-50s who had been farming potatoes in Haguri before moving to the city five years ago. He used to visit the credit union in person every Tuesday of the first week of the month to organize his account. Now, somewhere in an apartment in Seoul, he had completed the transaction with a tap on his smartphone screen.

A message came with it: "This is the proceeds from land sale. Please hold it for me."

Choi Eun-ja read the message, processed it in the system, and printed a receipt. There was no one to receive the receipt. She put it in a drawer.

Beyond the counter, in the waiting area, was a machine that dispensed waiting numbers. Today's number of tickets printed: zero. Yesterday had been the same, and the day before. The credit union still had roughly 500 members, but only twelve to fifteen people visited the counter directly each month on average. Account loss reports, loan interest payments, and occasionally elderly members coming to withdraw cash on behalf of their children.

The head of the credit union had said last month that a decision on merger would be made by year-end. The village credit fund and another credit union at the county office nearby had both been integrated into their central branches. Hansol Credit Union had held out a little longer. That was all.

Choi Eun-ja checked Park Jung-soo's balance again. KRW 520 million. According to the records, he had first opened an account at this credit union in 1989. His initial deposit then had been KRW 30,000. Of course, those records no longer existed. When the system was digitized, the old ledgers had been loaded onto trucks and taken away.

What appeared on the screen was only numbers.

Choi Eun-ja looked out the window. At the Haguri intersection was one convenience store, one dry cleaner, and the closed building of a pharmacy. The pharmacy had moved to the county town last year. The village was slowly, quietly, falling apart on its own.

The fact that Park Jung-soo had sold his land meant he would not be coming back. And Choi Eun-ja knew too that the KRW 500 million would not stay long in this credit union. Around next month, contact would come. A request to transfer the funds to a securities company account in the Seoul metropolitan area, or perhaps to leave it be because he would withdraw it online himself.

The afternoon sunlight passed diagonally through the counter glass. A speck of dust drifted down through the rays of light.

Choi Eun-ja took the receipt from the drawer and examined it once more. Then she put it back in the drawer.

The computer terminal had entered screensaver mode. A blue bird, the credit union's mascot, was flying slowly across the screen. No one knew who had created it or when it first appeared.
