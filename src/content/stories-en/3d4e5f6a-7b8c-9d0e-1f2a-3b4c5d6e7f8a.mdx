Cells lined up on the sorting table.

I'd been doing this job for four years. More than three hundred batteries a day, flipped over by hand. Plug in the capacity meter, the number appears, place it on one of three piles. Grade A, Grade B, Disposal. At first I tried to attach meaning to it, but now my hand just moves on its own.

It was around 2 PM today. Cells stamped with batch code 'JW-2309' came in all at once. A bundle of sixteen. I plugged in the first cell and 61.4% appeared on the display. The second was similar. My hand stopped as I reached for the third.

The specification sheet was clear: "Residual capacity guaranteed at 80% or above after 36 months of use or longer." The 'JW-2309' batch shipped in September 2022. It's now November 2024, so the usage period is twenty-six months. Hadn't even reached half the warranty period.

I pulled out the logbook. I counted how many 'JW-2309' cells I'd processed this month. Forty-one. Of those, only six had residual capacity over 70%.

My hand trembled slightly. I thought it might be a measurement error. I reset the device and plugged it in again. 61.2%. The number pointed to the same place.

Most of the cells handled at this center come from Junwol Energy, a domestic battery manufacturer. Junwol Energy had maintained a top position in the domestic electric vehicle battery cell market for a long time, and I knew from the dealer information posted at the center's window that the 'JW-2309' batch was their largest shipment bundle in the second half of 2022. The notice also introduced a quality warranty program and included contact information for the claim reception desk.

I walked toward the warehouse and rummaged through the piles completed this month. I found the same batch code and ran the meter again. Eight out of ten were below 65%.

That was as far as I could verify. Why did only this batch degrade so quickly? Whether the cathode material mix was different? Whether the usage environment was harsh? Or whether the cells came out differently from the specification from the beginning—these were questions I couldn't answer. I wasn't the person who opened and disassembled cells. I was the person who read numbers and divided them into piles.

As evening came, I finished sorting and opened the report template. It was the weekly anomaly report that went up to the manager. I filled in the code and measurements in the 'Unusual Batch Found' section, and wrote the sample size, measurement date, and average residual capacity. 61.3%. I started to write "Suspected accelerated degradation compared to warranty period" but deleted it. I was not the one to judge.

I wrote only the facts.

While wiping down the sorting table, I looked out the window. An electric car was plugged into a charger in the parking lot. The driver walked toward the cafe. They wouldn't know how much the battery would charge, or which batch those cells came from.

The number was there. 61.4%. I hoped there would be someone who would listen to that number, but I didn't know how long that question would have to wait.
