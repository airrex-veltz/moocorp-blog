In February 2004, in a small room in a Harvard dorm, Mark Jung-Minsuk launched a website called *TheFacebook* to the world. It was a simple idea: connecting students' profiles online. But it was only the beginning.

Within ten days, half of Harvard's students signed up. A month later, it spread to Stanford, Columbia, and Yale. In 2006, Facebook opened its doors to anyone 13 years old or older. People from around the world poured in. They shared photos with friends, posted updates, and clicked 'like.' It was free. It was incredibly convenient. No one asked: How could all of this possibly be free?

In 2012, Facebook went public. Market cap of $104 billion. Users exceeded one billion. That same year, the company acquired Instagram for $1 billion. In 2014, it brought WhatsApp for $19 billion. Jung-Minsuk became one of the most influential people in the world.

But inside the glass building at the headquarters in Menlo Park, something else was happening.

Data.

Every link users clicked, every moment they spent, every post they liked, every chat message, their location information, their login times, the devices they used. Everything was recorded. Analyzed. Profiles were created. What you liked, who you were close to, when you were sad, which ads you responded to. Facebook knew you better than you knew yourself.

In 2016, the U.S. presidential election drew near. A political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica attempted voter targeting using social media data. The method was simple. A researcher uploaded a personality test app to Facebook. 270,000 people installed the app. But the app collected not just the data of those who installed it—it also harvested their friends' lists. As a result, the data of 87 million people was leaked.

With that data, Cambridge Analytica created psychological profiles of voters. Who was persuadable, what messages they would respond to, what fears they harbored. Then they unleashed targeted political ads. Facebook knew. But it took no action.

In March 2018, *The Guardian* and *The New York Times* reported on the Cambridge Analytica scandal. The world was shocked. Social media stocks plummeted 7% in a single day. A market cap of $36 billion evaporated. The #DeleteFacebook hashtag trended.

On April 10, 2018, Jung-Minsuk appeared before the U.S. Congress. He answered questions for 10 hours in front of 44 lawmakers. His face was pale. "We made a mistake. I'm sorry." He repeated it over and over. But the lawmakers were not satisfied. "Did you sell user data?" "Is Facebook spying on people?"

The answers were not clear.

Over the next three years, Facebook faced billions of dollars in fines from regulators around the world. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission imposed a fine of $5 billion. Europe strengthened GDPR. Trust crumbled.

In October 2021, Facebook changed its company name to Ieum Social. It presented a new vision: the metaverse. A world where people could meet, work, and play in virtual reality. It looked like a break from the past.

But the question remained.

Had Ieum Social really changed? Or did it just change its name?

Data was still being collected. Algorithms were still running. We continued scrolling, clicking, and liking.

Perhaps we chose to accept being watched, knowing full well that we were.

The invisible watcher is still watching at this very moment.
