The roof exhaled the August heat back onto the asphalt. O Sung-jin stood at the edge of the rooftop, holding a sheet of paper printed with inverter logs over his sweat-dampened gloves. His installation team had mounted 340 panels on this building's roof twelve months ago. Now those panels were producing only 73 percent of their design capacity.

Kim Su-hyeok, a quality assurance manager at Haeran Energy Tech, climbed the stairs at eleven in the morning, white polo shirt and clipboard in hand. The moment he stepped onto the roof, he scanned the panel array and sucked his teeth. It was a silent click of disapproval, but O Sung-jin caught it.

"We should start by checking the grounding resistance," Kim Su-hyeok's first words.

Instead of answering, O Sung-jin handed over the infrared camera results. Microcracking had been detected on 23 panels. Microscopic cracks crossed the cell surfaces, some overlapping in two or three layers. The pattern showed a mixture of what specialists called finger cracks and bus bar cracks.

"This is impact damage during installation," Kim Su-hyeok said immediately after scanning the results. "Microcracks can occur from improper stepping or material drops."

"Our team worked with walkway boards," O Sung-jin's voice was low. "We did EL imaging before installation, and we have the files."

EL imaging—electroluminescence photography—was a procedure to verify the initial state of the panels. If the pre-installation images showed no cracks, then the cracks had appeared at some point after shipping. Kim Su-hyeok's eyes lowered to the checklist on his clipboard. His pen paused briefly, then resumed.

The two men walked along the panel array. Heat rose through their feet, and in the distance, cooling towers vented steam. The roof was a world unto itself. The noise of the city rose from below, and only wind existed above.

Haeran Energy Tech was a mid-sized manufacturer in the domestic solar panel market. The model used for this building was a 400-watt monocrystalline unit, a product that had been on the market for three years. After 2023, global new solar installation capacity exceeded 400 gigawatts annually for the first time, causing panel demand to outpace supply. Industry magazines reported that some manufacturers had extended their quality inspection cycles. Whether Haeran Energy Tech fell into that category, O Sung-jin couldn't say. But as long as the EL files existed, that was the legal team's job to determine.

"This pattern," O Sung-jin said, stopping in front of Panel 23, "is a linear crack running along the bus bar. This is the kind that forms from lamination pressure before shipment. Impact cracks radiate outward from the center of the cell."

Kim Su-hyeok placed his hand on the panel surface. He made a gesture of pressing the frame edge with his index finger. "Depending on the direction of impact and load distribution, linear patterns can appear too."

"Then reproduce it yourself," O Sung-jin's voice rose for the first time. "Create the same impact on one panel with the same configuration, and if you get this pattern, I'll admit I'm wrong."

Wind swept across the roof. Beyond the rooftop boundary, the city blurred. Kim Su-hyeok didn't answer. O Sung-jin said nothing more. Between them lay something thicker than the cause of the microcracks. Contracts, warranties, and reports each would have to send to their superiors.

From Haeran Energy Tech's perspective, 23 panels represented 6.7 percent of the total installation. It narrowly exceeded the 5 percent threshold for free replacement. From the perspective of O Sung-jin's installation team, the cost of reworking 23 panels was significant in labor alone. While the two companies pointed fingers at each other, the building owner was preparing a lawsuit for compensation over lost generation.

"Send me the EL files," Kim Su-hyeok finally said. "I'll hand them over to our headquarters technical team for pattern analysis."

"Copies already went to our lawyer," O Sung-jin said only that, then walked on along the panel array.

The two men walked across the same roof in the same direction, but they were on different paths. The panels, indifferent to this conversation, received sunlight and generated electricity. Cracked or uncracked, it made no difference. The sun made no judgment.

O Sung-jin stood at the edge again and looked down. People moved in the shadows of the street below. He wrote numbers on the memo sheet clipped to his clipboard. 23 panels. 6.7 percent. 73 percent output.

The numbers were clear. The boundary of responsibility was not.

The war on the roof would not end until a verdict was reached. Perhaps even after a verdict, the two sides would meet again on another rooftop, each armed with their own numbers.
