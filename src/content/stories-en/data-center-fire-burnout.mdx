2 AM, Building B of a data center on the outskirts of Seoul.

Lee Han-sol stood before an array of monitors and read the numbers. 41.3 degrees. 42.1 degrees. And Rack 12 in Column A, 42.9 degrees.

The alarm hadn't sounded yet. The threshold was 45 degrees. But after ten years in this building, he knew how the numbers ran. At this rate, they would hit the threshold before 4 AM.

He opened the cooling control console. CRAC unit 7 was already running at maximum output. So was unit 8. And unit 9. The sound was different—a half-tone higher than usual, the sound of a machine under strain.

He already knew the cause.

Four days ago, Client P Company had filled all of Column A with a new GPU cluster. A 72-hour foundation model training job. Total power consumption: 3.2 megawatts. Seventy-eight percent of this building's design capacity was concentrated in a single column. Completely different from the load distribution the designer had anticipated.

He had sent an email to the engineer in charge at P Company last week, requesting a distribution of the workload. A reply had come back: "Not possible due to scheduling. Training is scheduled to complete Wednesday morning."

Wednesday morning. Twenty-two hours from now.

**NVDA**—the company that made the chips embedded in many of this building's servers—their latest H100 consumed up to 700 watts per single GPU. Column A contained 1,024 of them. The math was simple. But simple math was rarely actually accepted in this industry. Performance was needed, performance consumed electricity, and electricity generated heat. No one stepped forward to stop this cycle.

Lee Han-sol opened the emergency load limiting protocol screen. Two clicks and he could send a command to throttle Column A's power to 80 percent. The training job would slow or stop. A call would come from P Company's contact. Probably shouting.

But if he didn't.

He had read a report once about a data center fire in London in 2022. Thermal runaway after a cooling failure. Hundreds of servers burned to the ground. Recovery took three months. That was before the GPU cluster era. Now the heat generated in a single rack was twice what it was then.

42.6 degrees. 42.8 degrees.

In the lower left corner of the monitor was this building's PUE score. Power Usage Effectiveness. 1.4. A decent number. Lower than the industry average. He had redesigned the entire cooling piping three years ago to lower this score. He had even received an award from the Ministry of Energy.

Now that piping was running at its limit.

He didn't sit down. He knew that sitting down would delay the decision.

The window outside was invisible. This building had no windows. Servers didn't need light. Whether it was 2 AM or noon, here only numbers spoke.

43.1 degrees.

He found P Company's emergency contact number. And he left the load limiting command window open. With two screens spread out side by side, he stood there for a moment.

He hadn't decided yet which one to press first.

The heat kept rising.
