Port night supervisor Han Seo-jin switched on the lights at Gate 7 at 2:40 a.m.

The harbor at night is not quiet. The screech of cranes on steel, the idle rumble of trucks, the distant wail of ship horns—all blending into a low hum. Seo-jin had heard that sound for eleven years. By now, silence felt strange instead.

"Supervisor, could you review this?"

The young employee from the scale station handed over a tablet. On the screen were a container number and two figures.

Declared weight: 11,400 kilograms. Measured weight: 18,720 kilograms.

Seo-jin read the numbers twice. A difference of 7,320 kilograms. Too large to call an error.

"Has it been remeasured?"

"Three times. Same result every time."

Seo-jin walked to the scale station without a word. The container sat on a gray trailer, innocuously. The paperwork said "household plastic containers." Eleven thousand kilograms of plastic containers would have filled the entire box. Yet this one felt heavy, as if sinking from something far denser.

What was inside, Seo-jin couldn't know. All that was certain was that the paperwork and the scale were telling different stories.

The regulations were simple: if weight discrepancy exceeded 5 percent, hold the shipment and notify customs. After that, Seo-jin's work ended. The rest belonged to others.

Simple regulations didn't always make for simple nights.

Back at the office, the phone rang. The screen showed "Operations Manager O." A 3 a.m. call rarely brought good news.

"Supervisor Han, I hear there's a container stuck at Gate 7."

He already knew. Seo-jin sat down before answering.

"The weight doesn't match the paperwork. I'll place a hold per regulation."

"The ship departs at 4:30 a.m. If you hold it now, the next vessel is five days out. The shipper is in an uproar."

"The paperwork and the goods don't match."

"It's just a weight discrepancy. The packaging might have been heavier. This happens all the time. We had something similar last month and just let it through."

Seo-jin said nothing. What had been overlooked last month was something Seo-jin knew well. That it hadn't been Seo-jin's shift that night offered strangely little comfort.

"Manager, 7,320 kilograms cannot be packaging."

Silence on the other end. That silence was longer than any words could have been.

"Supervisor Han, you know there's a reshuffle at year-end. Your name is on the list. The director position, I mean."

Seo-jin breathed slowly. Too gentle to call a threat, too precise to call a favor.

"Give me one minute."

Seo-jin hung up and looked out the window. Under the night lights, containers stacked like colored bricks. Thousands of boxes, thousands of documents. All moving on the assumption that they trust one another. Shippers trust shipping lines, shipping lines trust ports, ports trust people like Seo-jin. If someone closes their eyes just once, that trust gets a small hole. And holes usually rupture at the worst times.

The young employee appeared in the doorway.

"Supervisor, the shipping company keeps pushing. What should we do?"

His words asked a question, but his eyes asked something else. What kind of adult is this person? I'll face nights like this soon too. What should I remember?

Seo-jin remembered the first day in this chair. Something the predecessor had said: The scale never lies. It's always the person standing in front of the scale who does.

"Container 7, place it on hold."

The voice was more composed than expected.

"Prepare the customs notice, attach all three scale readings. Take photos too. Write only what we saw. No speculation about what's inside."

"Understood."

The employee rushed out. Seo-jin picked up the phone again and spoke briefly to Manager O.

"Placed on hold. Reason: weight discrepancy. All records documented."

There was no response. The call ended.

The hands trembled slightly. Seo-jin took a sip of cold coffee to hide it. The director position might be out of reach now. Or perhaps it had been bait hanging there all along, waiting for this hour. It couldn't be known.

At 4:30 a.m., the ship departed without the container. The horn sounded long and low.

Seo-jin remained in the office, lights still on. Customs would arrive soon. When the box opened, the lie in the paperwork might be exposed. Or perhaps it would turn out to have been nothing at all. It remained to be seen.

But that was fine. What had been protected wasn't the outcome. It was the number the scale had spoken.

Dawn began to break. The low hum of the port settled in again. For the first time in a long while, Seo-jin breathed easily within that sound.
