## 1. Introduction — The Panic Begins

October 22, 1907. New York.

The office door of John Pierpont Morgan swung open roughly. A subordinate gasping for breath shouted.

"Mr. Morgan! The Knickerbocker Trust Company has collapsed!"

The 70-year-old Morgan stared out the window, cigar in mouth. The street below was already filling with panicked people lined up outside the banks. It was a wave of fear—depositors desperate to withdraw their money.

"How much time do we have?"

"Two days... no, one and a half days. If we don't secure $25 million by tomorrow noon, every bank in New York will go under in a chain reaction."

Morgan turned slowly. His eyes were cold.

"The war has begun."

## 2. Development — The First Domino

That same night at 11 p.m., Morgan's study.

The wealthiest bankers in America were gathered together. James Stillman of City Bank, George Baker of First National, Jacob Schiff of Kuhn Loeb... Men who normally regarded each other as competitors.

"Morgan, why should we save Knickerbocker? There's no reason to salvage a failed firm," Schiff said sardonically.

Morgan unfolded a map on his desk. Every financial institution in New York was connected by red lines.

"This isn't one company's problem. If Knickerbocker collapses, twelve trust companies face simultaneous bank runs. Then the commercial banks. Then... all of us."

Baker drew a sharp breath. "You mean dominoes?"

"Exactly. And the first one has already fallen."

## 3. Climax — The Library Lockdown

October 24, 3 a.m., Morgan's library.

After 48 hours of negotiation, Morgan finally made his decision. He locked the library doors and put the key in his pocket.

"Gentlemen, no one leaves. Not until you sign the agreement."

Fifty bankers trapped in the room stirred anxiously. Before them lay the rescue package worth $25 million.

"This is... this is coercion!" someone shouted.

Morgan pointed out the window. Dawn was breaking. Thousands were already gathering on Wall Street below.

"You see down there? The exchange opens in six hours. If we don't produce a solution by then, those people will bring down the American financial system. In just one day."

Silence fell.

"Sign. Right now."

Morgan's voice was low, but carried irresistible weight. One by one, the bankers picked up their pens.

## 4. Conclusion — The Cost of Victory

October 24, 9:30 a.m.

The bell of the New York Stock Exchange rang for opening. But the crash they had expected did not happen. Instead, the rescue statement led by Morgan's banking consortium was transmitted.

"Total emergency liquidity provision of $25 million confirmed."

The crowd held its breath. And then... they cheered.

Morgan watched the scene from his office. After 72 hours without sleep, his face bore not the joy of victory, but only deep exhaustion.

"We've won, Mr. Morgan," his secretary said.

"No," Morgan shook his head. "We've only... bought time. One day this will happen again. And when it does, no one man will be able to stop it."

His prophecy proved accurate. The panic of 1907 ultimately led to the birth of the Federal Reserve in 1913. And JP Morgan became both a hero who had averted crisis and a symbol of the danger of letting a single man wield such power over the entire system.

A hundred years later, the bank bearing his name still stands on Wall Street. But now, it is institutions—not individuals—that stand guard against such crises.
