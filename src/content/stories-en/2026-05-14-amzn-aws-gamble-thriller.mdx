Seattle, 2006. Conference room on the seventh floor of Skylight Corp headquarters.

"Have you lost your mind?"

CTO Allan Burmingham slammed the table. The seven executives in the room held their breath.

"You want to sell our server infrastructure to competitors? What if Walmart, Target, and Best Buy use our cloud? They could see all our secrets!"

Baesungho turned his head. His gaze was cold.

"Andy, explain."

Andy Jessi (who would later become CEO) stood up. He turned on the projector. A single graph appeared on the screen—a curve climbing sharply.

"Our data center utilization averages 28 percent. On Black Friday, we hit 100 percent, but the rest of the year—350 days—we're idle. If we sell that excess capacity..."

"Mad."

The VP of Finance muttered.

"No, it's not." Andy advanced to the next slide. "If we don't do this, someone will. NewSearch? Microsoft? They're already moving. We have to set the standard first."

The room stirred. Baesungho remained silent.

Burmingham spoke again.

"Still, what about security? The risk of customer data leaks? What if a terrorist rents our servers?"

"We isolate them physically. Three layers of virtualization."

"Will that be enough?"

"If it's not, we build it."

A long silence.

Baesungho rose. He approached the window and looked down at the Seattle night skyline. Raindrops traced down the glass.

"When the dot-com bubble burst in 2000," he said, "our stock fell from $107 to $6. Everyone mocked us. 'Skylight Corp.bomb,' they called us."

He turned around.

"What did we do then? We invested in distribution centers. Everyone said it was crazy. But today our logistics network is our weapon."

He held up the *Project Red Shutter* cover.

"This is not madness. This is survival."

"Hello?"
