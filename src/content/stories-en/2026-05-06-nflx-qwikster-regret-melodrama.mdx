July 2011, Streamhouse headquarters in Los Gatos, California. Reed Hastings gazed out the window at the summer sun and let out a deep sigh. In his hands was a statement he had just finished writing.

"We will separate the DVD business from the streaming business."

It was just one sentence, but within it lay a gamble with everything he had built over fifteen years. It was a sense of humiliation—paying late fees at a Blockbuster video store in 1997—that had sparked the birth of Streamhouse. Starting as an innovative service that rented DVDs by mail, he had taken the first step toward the future of streaming in 2007.

But now, he stood at a crossroads.

"The customers will understand," CFO David Wells said carefully. "That DVD is the past and streaming is the future."

Reed shook his head. Fatigue was evident in his eyes. "It's not about understanding... it's about choice. We're forcing customers to make a choice."

The announcement created far greater waves than expected. Prices rose 60 percent, and the DVD service was separated under the unfamiliar name 'Quikster.' Customer anger spread across social media in an instant.

"Betrayal."
"Arrogant CEO."
"Streamhouse is finished."

What Reed faced every morning was hundreds of thousands of cancellation requests. The stock price plummeted. 800,000 customers left. Anxiety flickered in the eyes of employees, and the board pressured him to resign.

One night in September, Reed stayed alone in his office and called co-founder Mark Randolph.

"Mark, I... I've ruined everything." His voice trembled. "I've destroyed the trust built over fifteen years in just a few weeks."

A long silence came through the phone.

"Reed, you've always looked five years ahead, ten years ahead. But customers live today. You forgot that."

Mark's words struck Reed's chest. He was a strategist, but he couldn't calculate human emotion. He spoke of innovation, but he had let go of the hands of those who had walked with him.

In October, he finally made a decision. The plan to separate Quikster was completely abandoned. And he posted a long apology on his blog.

"I was arrogant. I did not listen to the voices of our customers."

In February 2013, "House of Cards" was released. The world cheered. Streamhouse was no longer a DVD company. It was a company that made stories.

Reed stood by the window and gazed at the sunset over Los Gatos. The nightmare of Quikster still lingered in a corner of his heart. But that pain had made him a better leader.

"Failure is not an end, but a beginning."
