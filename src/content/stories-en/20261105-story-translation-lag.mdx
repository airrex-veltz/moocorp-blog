Ji-soo was burying her face in the monitor light. Seoul time 11:17 PM. Outside, the buildings of Yeouido were turning off one by one.

A connection request appeared on the waiting screen. Customer code KF-0094. Her third call today.

Ji-soo plugged in her earphones and pressed accept. The screen split into two.

Dhaka opened on the right side. Morning sunlight diffused through white curtains. Rahim sat down, buttoning his shirt neatly. The time there was 8:17 AM. There was a three-hour distance between Korea and Bangladesh.

"Hello, interpreter," Rahim's Korean was clearly pronounced. He'd been doing this for over three years.

"Hello," Ji-soo said. "I'll wait for the client."

The left window opened. A factory management room somewhere in Gyeonggi Province. A man in his 50s in a brightly lit room with fluorescent lights. He set down his safety helmet and adjusted the camera angle.

"Okay, can you hear me?" he asked.

"Yes, we're connected," Ji-soo replied.

The three-way call started. Ji-soo was the midpoint, converting Korean to Bengali and Bengali to Korean. The Hanbyul Interpretation platform had assigned her this role for off-hours rates. It could operate 24 hours at less than half the salary of a domestic interpreter.

The manager said they needed to change the night shift this week. The order volume suddenly increased, and they had to adjust the work hours of twelve workers dispatched from Bangladesh. Rahim was their field contact.

"Ask them if they can work additional shifts Saturday night to Sunday morning."

Ji-soo translated into Bengali.

Rahim paused. "The current contract specifies 48 hours per week. This week is already at 46 hours."

Ji-soo conveyed it in Korean.

The manager didn't frown. He simply said, "Overtime is calculated at 1.5 times. It's per the contract."

She translated again.

Rahim nodded and wrote something down. Morning sunlight fell on his notepad.

The conversation continued a bit longer. Work uniform delivery schedule, next month's visa extension documents, cafeteria menu changes. Ji-soo translated without pausing. One missed word could become a dispute later. She knew this from experience.

When the call ended, the manager closed his screen first.

Only Ji-soo and Rahim remained. The screen showed both cities' times side by side. Seoul 23:41, Dhaka 08:41.

"Have you been doing this for a long time?" Ji-soo asked. There was no need to make conversation. She just did.

"Three and a half years," Rahim said. "I used to teach English at school."

"Why did you switch?"

"It's three times the pay." He said it briefly. No apology or pride. Just a fact.

Ji-soo looked at the night of Seoul beyond the window. Dark office windows, distant taxi lights, one convenience store. And beyond the screen, Rahim's morning. He brought a cup of coffee and set it beside the monitor.

"I have the next call at 9," Rahim said. "You should get off work now, right?"

"I have one more. It'll probably be after midnight."

Rahim laughed quietly. It showed even through the screen. "Then I'll start my day."

The screen went dark.

Ji-soo watched the waiting screen, expecting the next call. The Hanbyul Interpretation login showed six countries and fourteen interpreters currently connected. Vietnam, Cambodia, Sri Lanka. Morning was breaking beyond each of those screens too. It was always someone's work start, which meant this waiting screen never went dark.

Ji-soo took a sip of water. The waiting tone sounded.
