The day the aroma of coffee fades — a story of the last barista that SBUX held.

---

5 a.m., and Park Min-jun was already standing in front of the grinder before the store lights came on.

He had been doing this for twelve years. At first it was a part-time job to pay for university tuition; at some point it became a profession; at another point it became his life. The movements repeated hundreds of times a day — locking the portafilter, steaming the milk, capturing the contours of latte art — were already etched into his muscles.

But today was different.

The machine placed next to the counter looked closer than yesterday. There was no particular reason. The machine had been in that same spot yesterday and the day before. 150 centimeters tall, 80 centimeters wide, stainless steel body. The company called it "Barista 3.0." Min-jun just called it "that thing."

---

**SBUX** headquarters began introducing automation machines several years ago. But it became reality only after last year's Q3 earnings report. At that time, the company officially announced its goal of recovering operating margins through labor cost savings, and unveiled plans to sequentially introduce semi-automatic systems to 40 percent of all stores. The stock rose 6 percent that day alone.

The shareholders cheered.

That evening, Min-jun stared at that number on his smartphone screen for a long time. 6 percent. When one company announced it would shed thousands of workers, the market embraced that signal as something to celebrate. He understood what that number meant. But he hadn't yet felt what it meant for the point where it intersected with his own life.

It took half a year for that feeling to sink in.

---

Store Manager Lee Su-yeon called a staff meeting early that month. There wasn't much talk. Barista 3.0 would be responsible for making all standard drinks starting next month. The baristas could focus on customer service and store management. Employment would be maintained. Only the role would change.

"The role will change."

After the meeting ended, Min-jun kept rolling that phrase over in his mind. What difference was there between change and disappearance? Everything he had accumulated over the past twelve years — the sense of adjusting espresso extraction time down to the second, the ability to read a customer's mood just by looking at their expression, knowing what expression the regular customer would make when you draw a little cat instead of a heart in latte art — all of that was too much time to be condensed into the word "role."

He didn't tell anyone this. Most of his coworkers thought the same way, but they also didn't say anything. No one believed that expressing discontent could change anything.

---

The morning Barista 3.0 first started operating, Min-jun came in thirty minutes earlier than usual.

The machine was already warming up. Today's order forecast was displayed on the screen. 143 Americanos, 87 lattes, 56 other drinks. The number was calculated by the algorithm analyzing three years of order data.

Min-jun started cleaning the grinder. It was now one of his main duties — machine cleaning and assistance.

The first customer came in at 6:10 a.m. A man in his late 40s, a regular who ordered an iced Americano every morning. Min-jun didn't know his name, but he secretly called him "Tuesday Without a Tie" because he was the only one who came in casual on Tuesdays.

The man hesitated at the counter. The screen greeted him first.

"Hello. Would you like to place an order?"

The synthetic voice was natural. The man looked back and forth between the screen and Min-jun for a moment, then fixed his gaze on the screen and placed his order. Min-jun took out a cup from the side. That was all he could do now.

The iced Americano was ready in 47 seconds.

The man took the cup and left without saying a word. The sound of the store door closing seemed unusually loud.

---

Around lunchtime, when he had a brief moment, Min-jun looked out the window.

The street flowed as usual. Office workers passed by, someone pushed a stroller, someone else walked by gesturing to explain something with their earphones in. The world seemed unchanged.

But something had changed.

The small conversations that used to happen in the store had diminished. There was no time to say, "You look tired today." The flow of customer ordering, screen receiving, machine making, cup being handed over was efficient. Precise. Fast and consistent.

But there was no accidental eye contact anywhere in that flow. There were no short remarks about what the weather was like today. There was no sense that the customer was remembered.

Min-jun thought efficiency had erased it. Or perhaps efficiency had revealed it — that all those conversations had only ever been byproducts generated in the process of drinking coffee.

He couldn't tell which was true.

---

Near the end of the shift, Store Manager Lee Su-yeon came and stood next to Min-jun.

"How was it?"

"No problems."

"That's all?"

Min-jun thought for a moment and answered. "That customer came in wearing a tie today. On a Tuesday."

Lee Su-yeon nodded with an expression that showed whether or not she understood what that meant. And she said nothing more.

Min-jun took off his apron and looked at the machine once. Barista 3.0's screen was dark as it powered down. It would turn back on at 5 a.m. tomorrow. Thirty minutes before that, Min-jun would also clock in.

He didn't know how much longer this would continue.

Whether he could do the first motion he'd repeated every morning for twelve years — filling the grinder with beans — tomorrow or not.

But today, for now, he had done it.

Whether that was enough, even he didn't know.
