The approval email arrived on Thursday morning.

The subject line contained only one sentence: "Your personal data deletion request has been fully processed." Jun-hyeok Han stared at the screen for a long time. His fingertips trembled slightly. He couldn't tell if it was joy or anxiety.

The right to be forgotten. When he first heard that phrase, Jun-hyeok had laughed it off. He had believed that digital traces were inherently impossible to erase, that no button had an undo. But the world had quietly changed. In May 2018, a data protection regulation enacted in Europe gave people, for the first time, a legal eraser. Within just a few years after that, similar rights spread across continents and reached the place where Jun-hyeok lived. Large platforms opened deletion request centers. People lined up quietly. Soon statistics emerged. A search engine had received deletion requests numbering in the millions. Jun-hyeok wondered how many stories lay within those numbers. People who wanted to say goodbye, those trying to bury their past, those who simply wanted to feel lighter.

Jun-hyeok had been preparing for eleven months. He was systematic about it. Starting with old photo archives, social media accounts, shopping histories, location records, files scattered across the cloud, old blog posts. He sent deletion request forms to each service and received confirmation before moving to the next item. He checked them off one by one on a spreadsheet. The process had a strange pleasure to it. The sensation of his own existence becoming lighter, moment by moment. Some days it felt like liberation.

If asked whether there was a special reason, he probably would have shaken his head. There had been no major incident. He had simply realized one day how scattered he was across so many places, and that dispersion had become unbearable. The things that appeared when people searched his name. Communities he had joined in college and forgotten about. Old usernames he had abandoned. Traces of emotions that had long since faded. He wanted to erase it all. He wanted to exist just once, from the beginning.

On the night his last platform approved his request, a message came from his sister-in-law.

"Jun-hyeok, something strange is happening. I looked at a photo from Mom's birthday, and your face is blurry. At first I thought it was an image quality issue, but... I can't think of any other explanation."

Jun-hyeok turned off the screen and sat in silence for a while.

The photo belonged to his sister-in-law. It was stored in her cloud, taken by her. But his face in it had disappeared. The platform where Jun-hyeok had registered had sent a signal to linked services when deleting his facial recognition data, and that signal had spread in ways he hadn't anticipated. Technically, the processing was correct. His face was his biometric data, after all. It was Jun-hyeok himself who had requested its deletion.

But from his sister-in-law's perspective, that photo was hers.

Jun-hyeok searched his inbox. He found old conversations. Messages with someone he once loved, brief updates exchanged with his mother, long words of comfort sent by a college friend in the early morning. Those messages were still there. What had been deleted was only Jun-hyeok's account on the sending end, but the inbox of the receiver survived. Yet half of it was empty. In the flow of back-and-forth conversation, one voice had vanished. Their words remained, like a solitary monologue.

Jun-hyeok slowly thought about what that meant.

Memory does not exist alone. A person lives on inside another person. His face becoming blurred in his sister-in-law's cloud, his words disappearing from the conversation, the unnamed space left in the shared album. Technically, those were his data, but emotionally, they were someone else's history. When his face faded in his sister-in-law's photo from their mother's birthday, did his sister-in-law's memory fade too? Did his mother's memory fade as well?

The act of erasing does not complete in solitude.

The next morning, Jun-hyeok called his sister-in-law. He thought for a long time about what to say, but when the connection came through, his words failed him. His sister-in-law asked in the same voice as yesterday: "Did you see what I said about that photo?" Jun-hyeok was silent for a moment. Morning sunlight was pouring through the window.

"Yeah," he answered.

That was all. But his sister-in-law waited a long time before speaking again. As if she understood something. "I just... I want you to be there."

It was then that Jun-hyeok realized there was one thing that hadn't completely disappeared yet. He also learned that the window to send a restoration request would close in ten days. Once the window closed, the choice would be permanent.

He hung up the phone and opened nothing. The screen remained dark. Between 0 and 1, he had yet to press either one.
