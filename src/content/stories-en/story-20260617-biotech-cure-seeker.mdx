The Cure Seeker

---

Three in the morning. In the hallway of the clinical trial data center, a single fluorescent light flickered in a steady rhythm. Park Min-jun counted the beats like numbers as he walked. In his hand, a single sheet of paper. The interim analysis results from the Phase 3 clinical trial. His eyes read the same line twice, three times.

Median survival: control group 11.2 months, treatment group 18.7 months.

He held his breath. He knew what these numbers meant. 7.5 months. For someone, that 7.5 months was a daughter's graduation, a son's first steps, a cup of coffee by the window on a spring morning.

And it was also his mother.

---

Min-jun had joined SGNE's research laboratory twelve years ago. At that time, SeulGene was transforming the treatment paradigm for multiple myeloma with lenalidomide, and Min-jun worked at the periphery of that wave as an immunomodulation specialist. He was not a brilliant name. His paper citations were middling, and he preferred the lab bench to conference presentations. But he had patience. In science, that matters more than many people realize.

The first time he touched the molecule SG-40-L was almost by accident. A colleague handed it to him when taking sick leave. At the time, the substance had shown sub-par response rates in Phase 2 and was stuck near the bottom of the internal priority list. Min-jun saw a flaw in the dosage design. They had underestimated the protein degradation rate in the bone marrow microenvironment. Over six months he re-analyzed alone, explaining the same thing to the new clinical trial director five times.

The sixth time they listened.

---

The year Phase 3 testing began, his mother was diagnosed. Relapsed and refractory multiple myeloma. She had already exhausted six lines of treatment. Her doctor said there weren't many options left.

Min-jun could not intervene in his mother's trial enrollment because of conflict-of-interest regulations. His mother was not enrolled. He deliberately did not explain this fact to her. He had no language for it.

Instead he came in at six every morning and opened the data files. Anonymized patient numbers lined the screen. His mother was nowhere among them, but he worked as though she were.

---

The board first applied pressure two months before the interim analysis came out.

In January 2019, Myung-Bristol was finishing its acquisition of SeulGene for USD 74 billion. As the merger integration kicked into high gear, pipeline restructuring began. Min-jun's program was suddenly asked to deliver fast results.

CFO-turned-strategy VP Larry Telford spoke directly in the conference room.

"The FDA negotiation window is open after fast-track designation. It's realistic to apply for accelerated approval using response rate data alone. If we wait for overall survival data, we lose another two years."

Min-jun rested his hands on the table. Quietly, in a way that suppressed something.

"The correlation between response rate and survival has not been established for this drug yet. If we get accelerated approval and then confirmation fails later, we won't have saved that two years—we will have disappointed patients twice."

Telford moved to the next slide. The discussion continued.

---

Over the next three months, Min-jun lived three kinds of time at once. Time on calls with the data monitoring committee, time sitting next to his mother in a hospital waiting room, and time in email exchanges with Telford.

His mother spoke less and less. One day she asked him.

"When will your drug come out?"

Min-jun stared out the window for a long time.

"I could release it now if we hurried. But then it might not be complete."

His mother nodded. "Don't rush."

Those words rang clearer than any conference room.

---

The day the interim analysis came back, the data monitoring committee unanimously recommended early termination. The efficacy crossed the statistical boundary with unmistakable clarity. Early termination meant success, not failure. The results were so significant that keeping the placebo group in the trial any longer would be unethical.

Median survival 18.7 months versus 11.2 months. Hazard ratio 0.61.

Min-jun printed the report and placed it in his desk drawer. Then he called his mother. He didn't mention the drug. He just asked her what she wanted for dinner tonight.

---

The day the FDA new drug application was submitted, champagne was opened in the office. Min-jun took a glass and looked out the window. The Georgia sky in September was high and clear.

Telford came up beside him. "You were right. If we didn't have the overall survival data, the reviewers would have been far more difficult."

Min-jun said nothing. What he was thinking about were the anonymized patient numbers, and somewhere the people waiting to be held within those figures.

His mother passed away that November. Forty-seven days after the application was filed.

---

The following spring, the FDA approved SG-40-L. Min-jun saw the news on a TV screen in a conference room. The news also mentioned that patients who had failed to meet the inclusion criteria in the trial now had access to the prescription thanks to the survival data.

Someone asked him how he felt.

He thought for a moment and spoke.

"The data was right."

It was the most precise thing he could say.

The hallway fluorescent light flickered that day in the same rhythm.
