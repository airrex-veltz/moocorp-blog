In the woodshop where the sound of the plane had ceased, Suyeon stood for a long time.

The planes that Grandfather had used his entire life hung neatly on the shelf. The blade portions were polished like mirrors, and Suyeon could no longer tell whether this was Father's care or Grandfather's. Both were gone now.

One wall was occupied by the new machine. A CNC router. Father had brought it in used three months before he passed away. It appeared he had simply removed it from the box and left it there, for some of the vinyl wrapping still clung to it. On the day Suyeon first opened the woodshop, that vinyl was already thick with dust.

---

Grandfather had made furniture by hand. He read the grain of the wood, watched the humidity, considered the seasons. When Suyeon was young and would squat beside the workbench, Grandfather would sometimes say:

"Wood cannot lie."

Suyeon didn't know exactly what that meant then. She only half understands it now.

Father had added drawings to this. He noted dimensions in millimeter increments, then measured the same dimensions again. His work was slower than Grandfather's, but without error. Suyeon had gathered those drawings into a three-drawer wooden case. The case itself was Father's work.

---

The first order came sooner than expected. A café wanted twelve tables. Same dimensions, same finish, within a month. Suyeon sent the quote and only then did the math.

By hand alone, the maximum was one and a half per day. Twelve tables meant eight days of work. Adding finishing and drying, one month was tight. One mistake and she'd miss the deadline.

The CNC was different. Input the same drawing and it repeated the same form. While the machine ran all afternoon, Suyeon could work on other tasks. The time to shape a tabletop was one-fifth that of hand work.

For ten days, Suyeon tried both methods in turn. She had the CNC create the form, then refined the edges by hand. At first it felt strange. The lines left by the machine and the lines left by human hands were different. The machine's lines were uniform; the hand's lines were subtly alive.

Would the customer notice the difference?

---

In the furniture industry, the phrase "handcraft premium" had circulated for several years. Survey results showed a growing number of consumers willing to pay thirty to fifty percent more for handmade pieces than factory-made ones. But there was something interesting about this. In those surveys, what consumers valued most was not "traditional methods." It was "honest materials" and "durability for long-term use."

Suyeon had taped that article to the wall beside her workbench, then took it down one day. It seemed like it might be an answer, but the more she read it, the more questions it raised.

---

The twelve tables met the deadline. The café owner came to the woodshop herself to pick them up and spent a long time examining one of the tabletops.

"This line here."

She was pointing to the edge molding. The part Suyeon had refined by hand.

"It's something different from factory pieces."

Suyeon didn't know what to say, so she simply nodded.

The machine had moved according to dimensions. The hand had moved while reading that day's wood. If the difference remained in a single edge, was it a flaw or a character?

Grandfather's words returned to her.

Wood cannot lie.

The machine could not lie either. Only the machine had never asked the wood anything.

Suyeon did not take the planes down from the shelf. Whether that was her answer, or whether she was still deferring judgment, she herself could not say.
