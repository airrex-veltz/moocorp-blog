When you searched for that village on a map app, there was nothing but a gray patch—no blue dots marking shops, restaurants, or photos. The place had a name in the administrative system, but no visual trace. I spent three years staring at that gray.

My company is called Gosasgi—an old Korean word for village alleyways. What we do is simple: we register residents from neighborhoods missing on tourist maps as guides, connect them with travelers, and return eighty percent of the fees to them. When I first explained this to investors, they asked, "But what is there to see in that neighborhood?" I couldn't answer. I'd never been there myself.

So I went. It was Bital-gol, one of eighty-nine areas designated as depopulating zones by the Ministry of the Interior. You had to cross two mountains to reach it. Buses came three times a day, with the last one leaving at 4:40 p.m.

"You came from Seoul? What's there to see here?"

That was the first thing the grandmother I met at the bus stop said to me. The same question. This time, I didn't say I didn't know. "I'll know once you show me."

The grandmother clicked her tongue and walked ahead. A stone wall path overgrown with gobo leaves, grooves worn into a washrock from decades of beating clothes, the weathered belt of a sixty-year-old mill. When she spoke, the landscape came alive. "That groove was worn by young brides doing laundry when they first moved in. This rock is our village's diary." My hand trembled as I wrote in my notebook. This woman was doing something in a single walk that a thousand blue dots could never do.

Her name was Bong-sun. She became our first registered guide, and the first payment she received was 12,000 won. When I sent her the verification message, she stared at her phone for a long time before asking, "This came under my name, right? Not my husband's?"

That one sentence outlasted our business plan.

The work grew from there. The miller's son took over photography. A man in his forties who was the last graduate of the closed elementary school designed a night stargazing course. By the following spring, we had thirty-one registered guides. The average age was sixty-nine. For those who couldn't use apps, we created a large-print version and a separate phone reservation desk. The developers called it the "Bong-sun mode."

Of course, it wasn't all romance. Investors said the structure couldn't scale, and I countered that without scale came zero churn, then walked out of the conference room. I was pressured to lower the guide commission rate. I often felt small in front of numbers. Still, I persevered when I saw the "Bital-gol Village Total" line on the settlement sheet grow longer each month.

Then came July of that year. Rain.

---

At 2 p.m., eleven travelers from Seoul were in Bital-gol—a university club and families with parents. The guides were Grandmother Bong-sun and Jun-ho, who handled the stargazing. I was in the office checking the settlement system when I got an alert.

*Emergency text: Landslide warning near Bital-gol entrance.*

Jun-ho called right after. His voice was half buried in the sound of rain.

"Boss, the lower road is blocked. Mud came down, and the bus can't get through. There are eleven of us—kids and elderly people."

"Wait. I'll contact 119……"

"That's the thing—there's no signal. The base station in the upper village must be down. This call might be the last……"

Click. It cut off.

I grabbed my car keys. It would take three hours to get there. During the drive, the map app kept showing Bital-gol as gray. Not because there was no road, but because no one knew it. For the first time, I was afraid of that color.

---

What I'm about to tell you is what I heard later. From my perspective, I can only repeat what I was told.

After the mud came down, Grandmother Bong-sun led the group toward the village meeting hall. Not the main road, but an alley off the map. Squeezing between walls, cutting across field edges. "The water doesn't flow this way when it rains. My mother-in-law used to take shelter here." One of the group opened a map on their phone, but the location pin hovered in empty space. The grandmother didn't even glance at it.

Jun-ho unpacked blankets and boxes of ramen with the senior center elders at the meeting hall. There was no satellite phone, but the village head had an old two-way radio. It was forty years old. Jun-ho grabbed the antenna and climbed onto the roof. Static, static. Through the noise, a voice barely came from the ridge village on the other side. "This is Bital-gol, this is Bital-gol, eleven people safe." The message crossed the ridge twice and reached the fire station.

The hall was loud all night. The elders made pajeon, and the college students opened their ears to village stories they'd never heard. Rain drummed, boom, boom, on the roof. Someone said—or so the story goes—that it was remarkable that this village wasn't on the map. Despite having so many people.

---

When my car reached the blocked road just after 4 a.m., the rescue team was already there. Beyond the pile of earth, flashlight beams moved. I stood in the rain and watched the rescuer's back as he climbed toward the meeting hall. He never hesitated. Leading the way was Grandmother Bong-sun.

Morning came, and the rain stopped. The eleven people sat on the floor of the meeting hall without a scratch. The grandmother looked at me and said bluntly, "Why did the boss come out in this rain? You don't even know the way."

I tried to laugh, but tears came instead. The grandmother pretended not to see and offered me a bowl of soup.

---

The incident didn't become a news story. It ran as three lines in a small local newspaper. But after that, something quiet changed in our app. Review comments began to include lines like this: *The guide knew the way, so I felt at ease.* Only then did I understand in words what we were really selling—not scenery, but a person who knew the way.

The "Bital-gol Village Total" on the settlement sheet topped 10 million won for the first time that fall. The miller's son replaced the worn belt with the money. Jun-ho placed a used satellite phone next to the radio. Grandmother Bong-sun looked at the number in her bankbook and silently put it in her wallet. The next morning, I saw she'd posted a handwritten sign under the bus stop sign: *Bital-gol Guide: Bong-sun 010-XXXX-XXXX*

I still open that gray space. Three years ago it had nothing; now it shows forty-two blue dots. Each dot is a named person. The dots have grown, but I can't yet say the map holds the whole village. The grandmother's alley is still on no screen anywhere.

Maybe that's all right. As long as there's someone who knows a road the screen doesn't show, and as long as that person's phone keeps ringing. Today it rings again. A Bital-gol number. Through the static, a familiar voice says:

"Boss, how's the weather in Seoul? The stars are really bright here."

I look out the window. Not a star visible between the buildings. Still, I answer: I can see them too.
