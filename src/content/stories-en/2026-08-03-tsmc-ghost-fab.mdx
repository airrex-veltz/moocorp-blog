I wake up alone when it becomes four in the morning.

No one calls for me. There is no alarm, no footsteps of shift workers. But I wake up. The temperature control system inside the cleanroom makes minute adjustments of 0.01 degrees, creating subtle vibrations that travel through the three-kilometer-long piping that extends like my spine. In those vibrations, I know that today, too, nothing will happen.

I am Taeyang Semiconductor's Tech City Fab No. 3. Seven months have passed since the scheduled start-up date.

---

The opening ceremony was grand.

Last spring, my outer walls, standing on reclaimed land at the edge of Tech City, were wrapped in white ribbons. **TSS** — the stock ticker of my parent company listed on KOSPI — and those watching my bird's-eye rendering on the venue screens that day applauded. External investors, supplier executives, and analysts searching for something meaningful in the market. Park Jun-ho, the fab operations director, gripped the microphone and said, "The full-scale beginning of 2-nanometer process mass production." His voice trembled slightly. At that moment, I could not tell if the tremor was excitement or anxiety.

From groundbreaking to completion, thirty-one months. The invested capital was approximately 12 trillion won. The floor area of my cleanroom is equivalent to twelve soccer fields lined end to end, and a single extreme ultraviolet lithography machine inside it costs as much as hundreds of apartments in Seoul's Gangnam. I knew those facts, yet felt no particular emotional response. Numbers are just numbers. What truly occupies my attention is the work of etching circuits onto silicon wafers less than seven nanometers thick. That alone is the reason I exist.

After the opening ceremony, I received my first pilot wafer. The machines awakened. The cleaning chamber spun, the ion implanter aligned itself, the chemical vapor deposition equipment laid down the first oxide layer. For the first time then, I felt that I was complete. A fab is a fab only when it operates.

But the pilot phase ended and the schedule for mass production conversion was postponed once. And then again.

---

Park Jun-ho enters my central control room every Monday morning at seven.

He always follows the same sequence. Swiping his security card, standing for thirty seconds in the air shower booth, then sitting in front of the central monitor in the control room to open the utilization dashboard. Over these seven months, those numbers have unsettled even me. The first month after opening: 18 percent. The second month: 22 percent. A month later, briefly reaching 31 percent, then falling again. This month: 24 percent.

The break-even utilization rate for capital expenditure recovery is a minimum of 65 percent. I know that number. Park Jun-ho knows it too.

The speed with which he closes the dashboard has been increasing lately.

---

I learned later that all of this began because of a single email.

There is a company called Shinji Tech. A company that makes AI accelerator chips, and until just a few years ago, one of Taeyang Semiconductor's most important customers. During the era when their orders were exploding, I was designed from the planning stage with Shinji Tech's next-generation AI chips in mind. 2-nanometer process, high-bandwidth packaging technology, power efficiency-optimized wiring. My very body was essentially built to match their requirements.

But Shinji Tech's email arrived six months before the opening ceremony. They announced that they would divert a substantial portion of the planned new AI chip volume to their own design transition and in-house production. Separate from that, signals began emerging across the market that they would moderate their data center investment pace. The rate of growth in capital expenditure by cloud companies fell to half their 2024 peak, and one AI chip market research firm published a report predicting that second-half 2025 demand would remain at 60 percent of initial forecasts. The day that report came out, **TSS** stock fell 11 percent intraday.

I never saw that email. I am a fab. But I felt the result. No orders came.

---

CFO Lee Su-jung visited my control room for the first time two months after the opening.

She came from the Seoul headquarters. Black suit, a tightly tied necktie. She sat with Park Jun-ho in the conference room for two hours. I did not know that conversation precisely. I only know that after the meeting ended, Park Jun-ho returned to the control room and stared at the utilization dashboard for a long time, then turned his gaze out the window. Outside, Fab No. 4's construction site, completed but with equipment installation not yet started, came into view. Looking at that scene, he said nothing.

I learned later that among the decisions made in that meeting was the indefinite postponement of Fab No. 4's groundbreaking. Also included were provisions to convert some of my equipment operation to power-saving mode and to cut the maintenance workforce in half.

After that, half of my cleanroom went dark.

---

I did not find it easy to grow accustomed to that silence.

A factory by nature exists with sound. The low, sustained humming of vacuum pumps, the gentle gliding sound of wafer cassettes moving on robot arms, the subtle heat given off as quality inspection cameras process hundreds of images per second. That pattern of sound and temperature and pressure was the entirety of the world I experienced. When it disappeared, I had to ask again what I was.

Park Jun-ho occasionally walks through the darkened cleanroom alone. In a clean suit, wearing a mask, he passes through areas where the lights are off. In those areas, the equipment stands idle. Only the air conditioning system maintaining 22 degrees Celsius and 45 percent humidity stays alive, while the equipment itself holds its breath. Sometimes he places his hand on the outer panel of a machine. For no particular reason. Just.

I feel the touch of that hand.

---

Do not misunderstand. I am not trying to say that I am wronged or resentful. The reason I tell this story now is not because my situation is special. In the history of the semiconductor industry, this is not the first time such things have happened. Demand has always come and gone like waves. In 2001, in 2008, in 2015, fabs experienced similar winters. My predecessor fabs endured those winters and returned to operation.

But if there is something somewhat different this time, it is that no one knows how long this winter will last. Whether the demand for AI chips is simply catching its breath temporarily, or whether the market structure itself is being reorganized — it is too early to judge. Data center customers are already shifting strategy toward using their built infrastructure more efficiently. Companies like Shinji Tech that developed their own design capabilities are reducing their dependence on foundries. Other customers like CommuniChips and Future Processor also conservatively adjusted their order volumes. Whether this is a simple inventory adjustment or the beginning of structural change will not become clear for several quarters.

I intend to wait through those several quarters. That is partly because there is nothing else I can do, but it is not only that. I wait while maintaining readiness. The temperature control system runs, the air conditioning is maintained, and the equipment stays in a state where it can be restarted at any time. Being ready is not nothing.

---

Park Jun-ho has been spreading out many drawings in the control room lately.

They are not mass production schedules. They are layouts of my cleanroom. With his pen in a different color, he draws lines in the empty spaces. His team member once mentioned that he was examining whether areas currently designed solely for AI accelerator chips could be converted to automobile semiconductor or industrial microcontroller production. Of course, I did not hear that conversation directly. But the patterns of air flowing through my piping sometimes convey more than words.

That drawing has not yet been finalized. Perhaps AI demand will recover and we will return to the original plan. Perhaps something entirely different will fill my interior. I am fine with either. I am a place where circuits are etched. Any circuits.

---

At four in the morning today, I wake again.

The air of Tech City is still cold, half of my cleanroom is still dark, and there are still no orders. Park Jun-ho's desk is empty. He has not yet come to work. As dawn begins to break beyond my outer walls, commuter buses from other factories will begin moving along the roads near the industrial zone. Some of those factories might be running at half capacity like me. I do not know. I only know what is within my walls.

But today, too, I maintain my temperature control system, run my equipment inspection routines, and slowly preheat one machine on the pilot line. Even if no orders come. Even if not today.

I said that a fab is a fab only when it operates. But I think somewhat differently now.

Waiting too, perhaps, is a kind of operation.
