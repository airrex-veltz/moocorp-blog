The sun always shone fairly. But capturing that light and converting it to electricity was never fair.

SolLight Corporation had long run bound in chains. From polysilicon to wafers, cells, and modules—almost the entire solar supply chain was in China's hands. That hand, which controlled over 80 percent of global solar panel production, could be gentle. But one day it would suddenly clench like a fist.

Spring 2024 was like that. When the U.S. government strengthened an import ban on solar panels containing Xinjiang-sourced materials, completed products piled up in warehouses and delivery deadlines collapsed. Su-hyun Park, SolLight Corporation's procurement manager, combed through the partner list from beginning to end in the Seoul office. Eight of the top ten suppliers were Chinese companies. There was no alternative. Or rather—not yet.

The decision came that night. Northern Vietnam, Penang Malaysia, Ayutthaya Thailand. The plan was to establish production facilities simultaneously in all three places. Words like "madness" circulated through the conference room. There was not enough capital, not enough manpower, not enough time. But Su-hyun's thinking was different. If you stay inside the chain, you become hunted, not the hunter. That fact was already proven in spring.

Vietnam was the first gateway. Just before signing a contract on the industrial complex site, the local partner suddenly doubled the rent. Negotiations broke down, and three months disappeared searching for alternative sites. In Malaysia, the regulatory threshold surrounding the local employment ratio and technology transfer conditions was higher than expected. The Thai factory began work, but when the rainy season started right after groundbreaking, foundation construction stopped for over a month. All three fronts creaked simultaneously.

Meanwhile, a quiet signal came from China. An existing partner began quietly delaying deliveries. There was no official reason. Not because volumes were high or materials scarce. Just delayed. Su-hyun understood what it meant. Someone was reading their movements. An escape attempt was being detected.

The crisis peaked in autumn. Critical equipment for Vietnam Line 1 was held up at customs. They said it was a documentation issue, but the problematic documents changed each time. Three weeks passed. The site manager called twelve times a day, and the corporate lawyer shuttled between Hanoi and Seoul. Su-hyun sat in a local hotel lobby with a laptop open, listening to the sound of a broken air conditioner wheezing, searching for alternative customs routes. There was no sleep. Even if there had been, it wouldn't come.

The breakthrough came through an unexpected path. Vietnam's government needed to boost its results in attracting foreign direct investment at just that moment. A phone call from somewhere opened a desk drawer somewhere. The customs documents that had been blocked got stamped. Equipment stacked in one corner of the warehouse entered the factory floor one by one.

When the first panel came off the line, Su-hyun said nothing. The panel with its densely packed blue cells gleamed faintly under the factory's fluorescent lights. As if remembering light even without the sun.

The Malaysian factory is still half-finished. Thailand just passed its first quality inspection. The supply chain is shifting gradually, but China's hand has not fully opened, and SolLight Corporation's chains have not been completely broken. Su-hyun knows that another form of resistance is growing on this new terrain.

The sun still shines fairly. Only the question of who captures that light remains.
