Father's ledger took three readings. The first time, I read it as numbers. The second, by his handwriting. The third, by his heart.

The inherited estate consisted of seven buildings, appraised at KRW 500 billion. The lawyer paused as he handed over the documents. There were two unfamiliar private lending companies, a project financing loan where Father had personally guaranteed, and a bridge loan whose maturity had been repeatedly extended. Combined, they totaled KRW 150 billion. Father had never told us about any of it.

"He didn't hide it—he just didn't show it," said Dohyun, my eldest brother. It sounded less like an excuse and more like self-persuasion.

That evening, we held our first family meeting on the second floor of the kalguksu restaurant where Father had eaten lunch his entire life. I'm Seo-yoon, the middle child, and I work with numbers. So I sat there as someone belonging to neither side.

Dohyun said, "Sell and be done with it." If we dumped the buildings at low prices, we'd pay the debt but have less left over. He proposed tearing down two old buildings in Seongsu-dong and building a mixed-use complex. He calculated that by taking out more loans and leveraging up, our assets would double in five years. His eyes held the same glint Father's had when he was young.

Ha-jun, the youngest, shook his head. At thirty, he'd never owned anything beyond a salary. So he was more resolute. "Liquidate everything, pay off the debt, and put what's left in an index fund. We need sleep." Ha-jun still remembered Father's nightly sound of tapping a calculator in the living room.

I unfolded the rental yield table. "Neither of you is entirely wrong. But the order matters."

We soon understood why that order mattered. Inheritance tax. In the bracket exceeding KRW 30 billion in taxable base, the rate was 50 percent. Subtracting the debt, our net assets were KRW 350 billion—meaning tens of billions in taxes alone. The buildings weren't cash, and the tax office wanted cash. We applied for installment deferment to spread payments over several years, but that was another loan bearing interest. Dohyun's development plan died halfway in that moment. Ha-jun's liquidation plan met the same fate. A hasty sale would trigger capital gains tax separately.

We reached what couldn't be called an agreement. We decided to divide the buildings into three portions and manage them each our own way. I put it this way: "Let's see in five years—not who was right, but what each of us learned."

The first year was peaceful. Dohyun finished the design for the Seongsu-dong site and signed a contract with a builder. Ha-jun sold two buildings and immediately paid off the highest-interest loans. I changed the tenant composition of the remaining three buildings. I brought a hospital and a tutoring school into the long-vacant floors, and instead of raising rent, I extended contract terms. Yields climbed quietly.

In autumn of the second year, the market froze. The base rate jumped from 0.5 percent to 3.5 percent in just a year, and the bond market dried up, halting construction financing. Dohyun's loan rate climbed from the 4 percent range past 9 percent. Construction costs rose, but sales inquiries stopped.

Dohyun called at dawn. His voice was hoarse. "Seo-yoon, the interest alone is KRW 200 million a month."

I had no answer. Ha-jun quietly said beside me, "I told you so." Those words cut like a blade through our brother, and the two didn't speak for nearly a year after.

The way money divides a family is undramatic. Replies in the group chat come an hour late. The order we sit in at the holiday table changes. For our nephew's first birthday, only envelopes pass between us. We drifted apart that way.

My own path wasn't easy either. The hospital building proved stable, but when one tutoring school closed, a deposit refund dispute erupted. "Revenue optimization," the phrase sounded dignified, but it was really just putting out small fires every month. Ha-jun invested most of the proceeds from the two sales into an index fund. In that year's market downturn, his valuation fell nearly 20 percent, and for the first time he fought the urge to sell. Even the choice he'd trusted wavered. He told me alone.

In the fourth year, Dohyun sold one Seongsu-dong plot below market price. The other plot where construction was underway barely completed, but filling tenancy took another year. By the numbers, he'd lost the most. Yet that winter, he called Ha-jun and me for the first time.

"I'm not calling to say I was wrong. I think I understand now why Father didn't tell us," Dohyun said. "He knew we'd stop him. And he knew he couldn't stop himself."

Father's KRW 150 billion wasn't a trace of greed. It was the trace of a man who'd mortgaged building after building to keep these buildings. Dohyun took after that way. Ha-jun grew up in fear of it. I sat between them reading the ledger. The three of us were different fragments of the same person.

By the fifth year, the numbers looked like this: Dohyun's share had shrunk to about a quarter of the original, but one of the completed buildings had begun to generate rental income. Ha-jun's share carried no debt and held its valuation steady. My share grew slowest, most quietly, most steadily. Yet I couldn't say I won. Had the interest rates moved the opposite way, the order would have flipped.

The final meeting was again at that same restaurant. The owner left Father's seat empty and ushered us upstairs. Ha-jun asked, "So what did we inherit? Buildings or debt?"

No one answered right away. Outside the window, the weathered sign of the first building Father ever bought was visible. Its lights were on.

I lifted a spoonful of broth and spoke. "Remember the memo next to the tax notice? Handwritten in Father's script—'the kids' meals.'"

Dohyun laughed. It was the first laugh I'd seen from him in five years.

The notebook held no numbers. What we have isn't buildings, but the fact that three people who can fail in different ways sat at the same table again. Whether that's an asset or a debt—there's still no column in the ledger for it.
