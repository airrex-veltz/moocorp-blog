When walking through the dark corridors of a server room, a person realizes how small they are.

Lea Huang knew this feeling well. Somewhere on the sprawling campus near Ashburn in northern Virginia—a building known officially only as "Building K-14"—she was spending her sixteenth month. Her title was Senior Infrastructure Auditor. What she actually did was walk between server racks, searching for anomalies in cooling efficiency, power distribution, and log patterns.

That day, she thought would be unremarkable.

The inspection routine was simple. Read the temperature sensors on each rack, scan through the power consumption graphs, and issue a ticket if there were any outliers. In sixteen months, the only anomaly she had found was a cooling fan running one degree warmer than it should. It was tedious work, but within that tedium, Lea found a strange sense of stability. Even if the world collapsed, these servers would keep running. Hanwoo Cloud was carrying an MRI diagnostic software for a hospital somewhere, an online class for a student somewhere, a video call between an elderly person and family somewhere.

In the afternoon of such thoughts, a strange number caught her eye on the monitor.

Zone 47-D. Power consumption was spiking slightly above the normal range. 3.2%. By audit standards, it wasn't even at the "observe" threshold. But Lea stared at the number for a long time. The problem wasn't the absolute value. It was the pattern.

The 3.2% excess had been sustained for seven days.

If it were a cooling issue, the temperature sensors should have risen as well. But the temperature was normal. If it were a traffic surge, there would be traces in the logs. But the logs were clean. Lea quietly pushed her chair back and walked toward Zone 47-D.

The servers blinked silently. Green LEDs flickered regularly, whispering that they were alive. Lea verified the rack numbers one by one and stopped in front of the twelfth rack. On the surface, nothing was different. But when she pulled up that rack in the management console, an unexpected metadata field appeared.

`archive_tier: glacier_deep_internal_v2`

Lea had never seen this tag before. She knew Hanwoo Cloud's Glacier. It was an archive storage service that kept data frozen for years to decades. But `internal_v2` was a name that didn't appear anywhere in public documentation.

She sent a message to her team lead, Daniel. "Found unusual storage tier tag in 47-D. Please check." The reply came three hours later. "Checking." That was all.

Two days passed.

Lea couldn't sleep at night. The sensation of walking through the data center corridors kept coming back to her. Somewhere in the world, there would be data that someone had entrusted to that server. It could be a backup medical record, corporate internal audit documents, satellite observation data from decades ago. Or it could be something no one remembered anymore, yet something that would be critically important to someone someday.

On the morning of the fourth day, Daniel walked to Lea's desk.

"I handled the 47-D thing," he said matter-of-factly. "It was an internal engineering project. A long-term storage layer operating without a public API. Not customer data. Operational logs of the infrastructure itself."

"Operational logs? What kind of logs require a separate storage tier like that?"

Daniel hesitated for a moment. "It's for failure prevention. The system collects decades of patterns to understand what signals it sends before dying. They're planning to use it someday to train more sophisticated predictive models. For now, it's unused data. We're just storing it."

Lea nodded. It was a reasonable explanation. It made technical sense. At the scale of large infrastructure, systems evolve in unexpected ways, and internal experiments accumulate in the process. Considering the scale of a global cloud business like Hanwoo Cloud—hundreds of data centers worldwide, millions of servers, hundreds of millions of API calls a day—it might be a system that humans could never fully understand.

Yet Lea stared blankly at her monitor screen for the rest of that afternoon.

Operational logs of the infrastructure meant, in some sense, that the infrastructure was recording its own life. Boots and shutdowns, minute fluctuations in temperature, moments when requests came in, were processed, and went out. If all of that accumulated into decades of data, that was history in a certain sense.

Who would read that history? And when it was read, what would be seen?

Lea couldn't be sure whether she had discovered something now, or whether she had discovered nothing at all. The green LEDs in Zone 47-D continued to flicker regularly. Alive. Remembering. Though no one asked.
