## The Invisible War

1958, Fresno, California. An executive at Bank of America launched a bold experiment. He mailed plastic cards to 60,000 people without any credit checks whatsoever. The name was BankAmericard. For the time, it was an unthinkable gamble.

"This is madness!" the board objected. But they didn't know. This was the beginning of a revolution that would reshape global finance forever.

The losses in the first year were staggering. Fraud and defaults were rampant. Yet consumers who received the cards tasted a new kind of freedom: the remarkable experience of being able to buy things even without cash in their wallets. Slowly, but surely, the tide began to turn.

## The Rival Emerges

In 1966, fourteen California banks struck back. The Interbank Card Association, which would become Mastercard. Bank of America was no longer alone. The war had begun.

The two factions raced across America, fighting to secure merchant partners. Restaurants, gas stations, department stores. It was a game where whoever secured more retailers won. Salespeople occupied cities day and night, relentlessly pitching their cards.

In the mid-1970s, BankAmericard made a decisive move. To compete in a larger arena, it needed a new name. In 1976, a new banner rose. Bija Card. Bija. A name easy to pronounce in any language, aimed at the entire world.

## Crossing Borders

In the 1980s, the war expanded into a global conflict. Europe, Asia, South America. There were markets where Mastercard entered first, and regions where Bija planted its flag first. Both sides refused to yield an inch.

Boardrooms in Tokyo department store chains deciding which card to accept. Contracts at London's Heathrow duty-free shops. Negotiation tables at São Paulo supermarket headquarters. Quietly, but fiercely, territorial wars played out in unseen places.

Bija secured sponsorships of the Olympics and World Cup. On a stage watched by the entire world, it displayed its blue and yellow logo. Mastercard countered with its "Priceless" campaign. It appealed directly to consumers' emotions.

## 2008: Worst Timing

March 2008. Bija decided to go public. But the timing was terrible. The subprime mortgage crisis had erupted, and financial markets were in panic. It was just before Lehman Brothers collapsed.

"Going public now would be insane," Wall Street experts warned. But Bija's executives pushed forward. "It's now or never."

March 19th, New York Stock Exchange. The opening bell rang. The first trading price was $44. Trading volume alone that day: 460 million shares. It was the largest IPO in history. In the midst of the financial crisis, Bija raised $17.7 billion.

The market was shocked. Banks were collapsing, yet Bija soared alone. The reason was simple. People used cards even in recessions. In fact, they used them even more when cash was scarce.

## Digital Battlefield

In the 2010s, new competitors emerged. Arion Electronic Pay, Alipay, PayPal. Rising powers armed with smartphones. They sought to make plastic cards themselves obsolete.

Bija reinvented itself once more. Chip cards, contactless payments, tokenization technology. Physical cards were now merely symbols. The real battlefield was inside smartphones.

In 2016, Bija launched Bija Direct: a system for direct transfers from bank accounts without needing a card, yet still using the Bija network. Even Arion Electronic Pay and other emerging services ultimately relied on Bija's infrastructure.
