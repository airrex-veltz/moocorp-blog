Eleven o'clock at night. The office lights on Teheran-ro in Seoul's Gangnam District never went dark.

Lee Jun-ho sat before a monitor. Or rather, he was collapsed into it. His body surrendered to the chair's backrest, eyes fixed on the screen. Numbers flowed before him. Red. And more red. An endless river of crimson.

**TFIN** — TechFin's stock price had plummeted 18.3% in a single day. By market capitalization, KRW 320 billion had vanished. But it was not the stock price that crushed Lee Jun-ho. The stock price was just a number. Numbers could rise again. What terrified him was something else.

"CEO, the press release draft is ready."

Yun Seo-yeon, the marketing team lead, held out a tablet. Lee Jun-ho took it but did not read it. His gaze remained fixed on the window. The streetlights of Teheran-ro were blurred by rain.

"What's the headline."

"'Algorithm Error, Immediate Correction Underway.'"

"Error."

Lee Jun-ho turned the word over in his mind like he was chewing it. Error. It sounded simple when you said it that way. A mistake. A bug. Something that could be fixed and forgotten. But he knew. This was not that kind of problem.

TechFin's credit rating algorithm, 'ClearScore v2,' had reviewed 38,000 loan applicants over the past 14 months. A significant portion of them had received credit grades lower than their actual creditworthiness, and their loans were rejected. Some lost the opportunity to buy a home. Some failed to secure business capital. They did not know why they had been rejected. The algorithm did not explain. That was the algorithm's way.

In 2019, a similar incident occurred in the United States. A major global credit card company's algorithm was found to assign lower credit limits to women despite identical financial situations. The uproar was significant at the time. But the industry quickly forgot. Algorithms became more sophisticated. They also became more opaque. Lee Jun-ho knew that era well. Yet, despite knowing, he believed his model was different.

He had believed.

"Our algorithm has no bias. It only looks at data."

That was what Lee Jun-ho said three years ago at the first funding announcement. Laughter had erupted in that room. There was applause. Journalists took notes. He felt like a hero. A mission to overturn the old banking system and create fairer finance. That mission had carried him this far.

"CEO."

Yun Seo-yeon called again.

Lee Jun-ho set the tablet on the table. Slowly, soundlessly.

"Have we finalized the count of those affected?"

"Yes. 38,412 people total. Of those, approximately 1,200 have filed appeals."

Around 1,200 people. They had sensed something was wrong. They had appealed to the system. But the system did not respond. Lee Jun-ho's algorithm had answered them with silence.

Footsteps echoed in the hallway. Heo Jae-won, senior developer from the engineering team, entered. His face carried a mixture of fatigue and something else. Too heavy for resolve, too hot to call resignation.

"CEO, I need to tell you something."

Lee Jun-ho looked at him.

"ClearScore v2 had bias in its dataset from the moment it was first deployed. The training data itself came from records between 2010 and 2018, and during that period the repayment patterns of communities with limited financial access — particularly gig workers and self-employed individuals — were significantly underrepresented. The bias was already inside the numbers we believed were unbiased."

A long silence settled in the room.

Lee Jun-ho turned his head toward the window. Raindrops drummed against the glass. Fine and relentless.

Innovation. How many times had he spoken that word? At every lecture, every interview, every investor meeting. He had said innovation was good, speed was virtue, data was fair. Those beliefs were now swelling in this room like water-logged paper.

"I asked the wrong question."

Lee Jun-ho murmured it to himself, yet Heo Jae-won heard.

"We kept asking 'how accurate?' We never asked 'accurate for whom?'"

Heo Jae-won said nothing. Neither did Yun Seo-yeon. That silence was agreement, and at the same time, it was consolation. They were all in the same boat. With the same beliefs, rowing in the same direction.

Lee Jun-ho stood from his chair.

From a drawer, he retrieved an old notebook. He had written in it when the company was founded. The cover was worn. He opened to the first page. In his own handwriting, young and rough, was written:

'Finance must be for people.'

That sentence was still true. He could be certain of that much. But the dozens of pages beneath it — plans, algorithms, models, revenue structures — all needed to be rewritten now. Not from the beginning, but from somewhere deeper.

Outside, the sound of rain eased slightly. Or perhaps he had simply grown accustomed to it.

Lee Jun-ho turned the monitor back on without closing the notebook.
