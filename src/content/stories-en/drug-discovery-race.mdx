Chaiyeon revised her presentation slides twice. Not because the molecular structure diagram on the left side of the third slide bothered her, but because of the number written in the right column. Seventeen years. That was how long Cheongmyung Pharmaceutical's Central Nervous System Research Institute had spent wrestling with this single protein.

The hotel conference hall in Seoul's Gangnam district was filled with a symposium on rare neurological disease treatment development. The presentation order was alphabetical. If Cheongmyung Pharmaceutical was last in the morning, GenSerabio was first in the afternoon.

Chaiyeon saw him for the first time in the corridor.

Seojin sat alone in a hallway chair, his laptop balanced on his lap. The fan was quietly running—his model was processing. He wore a hoodie instead of a suit. He looked like a graduate student who had wandered into the wrong venue.

"GenSerabio?" Chaiyeon spoke first. His name badge showed his company name.

Seojin looked up. "Cheongmyung Pharmaceutical."

Both of them already knew they were aiming at the same target. CDRK-14. The key protein in a childhood-onset rare neurodegenerative disease. Fewer than three thousand patients worldwide.

---

After the afternoon presentation ended, the two were assigned to the same round table.

Seojin spoke. "Our algorithm reverse-traced CDRK-14's misfolding pathway from a protein structure prediction database released in 2021. That was the first time protein structures could be predicted from amino acid sequences alone. Since then, the possibilities have changed completely."

Chaiyeon set down her coffee cup. "It's true that structure prediction became possible. But the database doesn't have data on how misfolded proteins actually behave inside cells. You have to spend seventeen years in front of a culture dish to understand that."

Seojin paused briefly. It looked like listening rather than rebuttal.

"At which residue does the misfolding begin?" he asked.

Chaiyeon hadn't expected the question. His face showed genuine curiosity, not attack. "At leucine 271."

Seojin opened his laptop. A molecular model unfolded on the screen. "I'm looking at the same location."

---

The two companies continued on their separate paths. Cheongmyung Pharmaceutical proceeded with cell cultures and animal experiments, while GenSerabio filtered tens of thousands of candidate compounds through algorithms. News of the competitor's patent applications came through industry newsletters, and Chaiyeon found herself scrolling more slowly each time she saw that name.

Four months later, GenSerabio announced they had narrowed down to three candidate compounds. Among them, JB-0192 showed meaningful signals in animal models.

Chaiyeon received the paper and called within two days.

"JB-0192—have you looked at the hepatotoxicity profile?"

A brief silence came through the phone.

"Which numbers?"

"The ALT level rises nonlinearly after 72 hours of administration. We saw the same pattern with a similar derivative three years ago. Your algorithm probably doesn't have that data. It's never been published—it's failed data."

Seojin spoke slowly. "…Can you send it?"

The data from Cheongmyung Pharmaceutical went to GenSerabio's model at no cost. The legal team opposed it. Chaiyeon compressed the file anyway, knowing this.

---

The collaboration didn't start with a formal contract but with an email chain. The subject line was "Re: Re: Re: Request for JB-0192 Toxicity Data Review."

After learning from the unpublished failure data Chaiyeon sent, Seojin's algorithm reordered the candidate compound list. JB-0347, which had been twelfth, moved up to second. The toxicity prediction score changed.

Chaiyeon placed the compound in a cell culture dish.

The results took three weeks.

During those three weeks, the two met via video every evening. At first it was because of the data. Later, they found themselves sitting in front of the screen for reasons other than data. Chaiyeon didn't try to define it.

---

The experimental results were inconclusive.

JB-0347 had no toxicity issues, but its efficacy was lower than predicted. It fell short of expectations. Yet it wasn't a complete failure either. An unexpected secondary pathway had opened up at the leucine 271 binding site. Seojin called it "noise," and Chaiyeon called it a "clue."

They used different words about the same data.

But neither was wrong.

"What's the next step?" Seojin asked.

Chaiyeon paused to think. Late autumn rain was falling outside the window. The research institute's lights were reflected in the rainwater.

"I'm not sure. But I don't think we should give up on this pathway."

Seojin answered quietly. "Neither do I."

His expression on the screen wasn't one of discussing data. Chaiyeon noticed that, but didn't confirm it. It was something seventeen years had taught her—certain hypotheses fall apart if you rush them.
