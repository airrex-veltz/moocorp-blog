Among factory staff, the building had a nickname: "The Mine."

In reality, it was a two-story building in an aging industrial complex on the outskirts of Incheon. Yet it had windows barely the size of air vents, fluorescent light always felt inadequate somewhere, and the distinctive sour smell of electrolyte never left the nostrils. A place that made you forget you were above ground. That's why people working in Cheongrok Circulation's used battery processing department jokingly called themselves "miners" and laughed.

"The devils woke up again today."

Senior Engineer Kang Min-jun adjusted his helmet as he spoke. 6:40 a.m., he was examining the situation board in front of Processing Line 3. Electric vehicle battery packs stacked at the line's end sat heavily, their surfaces reflecting green light from the fluorescents. They weren't actually green. But those batteries caused problems that were all unpredictable and troublesome, so someone joked about calling them "green devils," and the name stuck.

The green devils took many forms.

First was thermal runaway. The moment battery cell internal temperature escaped control, one cell transferred heat to neighbors, which pulled adjacent cells, triggering chain reaction. Hundreds of degrees of heat generated in seconds, stopping the line entirely, and at worst required a full day of water containment. During the three years Kang Min-jun worked at this factory, thermal runaway occurred sixteen times. He personally grabbed the extinguisher twice.

Second was black dust. When battery cells disassembled, black powder emerged—the infamous black dust. This powder contained valuable metals like lithium, cobalt, and nickel mixed inside. The problem was that black dust reacted when meeting humidity in air. On high-humidity days, lithium dust slipping through narrow cracks silently created small sparks. Line workers called this "the devil's sneeze."

Yet the most troublesome among devils were invisible things. The electrolyte waste liquid left at process end. The organic solvent mixture remaining after active material extraction from battery cells was classified as special waste per regulation, handled monthly by collection companies. Costs were substantial. One reason the accounting team leader sighed quarterly.

Kang Min-jun unfolded the processing record again today. Last week's electrolyte waste collection volume increased considerably. As battery collection volume grew, processing costs rose proportionally. The accounting leader's sighs were now independent of season.

---

Strange things began not long after.

Park Ji-hyun, who handled Processing Line 2, rushed to Kang Min-jun during lunch with a tablet. The screen showed yesterday's afternoon mistake when someone wrongly connected a tank while replacing waste liquid collection drums.

"When I asked what happened," Park Ji-hyun said catching his breath. "The collection driver mixed two drums and took them. But this morning we got a call from that company."

"Asking for compensation since we mixed it by accident?"

"No. They asked us to bring them more."

Kang Min-jun set down his chopsticks.

It turned out there was a story. The collection company's lab analyzing the mixed waste discovered an anomaly. Waste liquid from Cheongrok Circulation's specific process contained significantly higher concentration of lithium hexafluorophosphate than typical electrolyte waste. The electrolyte salt directly used in battery manufacturing process. What Cheongrok paid to dispose of was the raw material component battery manufacturers purchased expensively.

"What we were paying to dispose of every month," Park Ji-hyun continued slowly. "Was actually something we could sell."

Kang Min-jun examined data all afternoon. Three years of waste liquid collection records since the factory started, ingredient analysis records, collection unit price trends. Numbers began reading in completely different ways. Items that looked like monthly invoices now looked like unsold inventory.

"To properly separate and refine this, we'd need to change the process," Kang Min-jun said like muttering to himself. "Equipment investment required, new certifications needed."

Park Ji-hyun chimed in. "How long would that take?"

"Who knows."

He fell silent for a while.

---

Two months later, a small meeting convened in Cheongrok Circulation's processing plant conference room. Process design team, accounting team, and an outside consultant in electrolyte refinement sat around the table.

Instead of a presentation, Kang Min-jun handed over a one-page summary table.

"Some of the electrolyte waste currently handled as waste can be processed through refinement to reach grades sellable directly to battery manufacturers. Including equipment replacement and certification acquisition, minimum eighteen months. What happens after is still unknown."

The accounting leader examined the table and asked, "Still better than now, though, right?"

"Currently we pay and dispose. Going forward we can receive and sell. The difference is substantial."

Silence drifted briefly through the conference room.

Someone laughed. Starting softly, a few more joined. Park Ji-hyun raised her lips too. Kang Min-jun didn't laugh but his expression softened.

"So we found gold in the mine?" the accounting leader said.

"Not gold," Kang Min-jun corrected. "Lithium."

"Even better than gold, isn't it?"

No one gave a definite answer. Back in the factory after that meeting, Kang Min-jun gazed for a while at the battery packs stacked at Line 3's end. The green devils were still there. Unpredictable, troublesome, occasionally sparking things. Yet something else existed inside them too.

Devils don't easily reveal their true nature. But once you know, it's hard to go back.

The tone of the word "mine" that Kang Min-jun raised as a complaint had changed. From complaint toward familiarity, and perhaps, very slightly, toward affection.
