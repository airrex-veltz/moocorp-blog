I am Dasol.

At 2:47 a.m., Sori's heart rate is 118 beats per minute. Body temperature 36.8 degrees. Oxygen saturation 99%. The breathing interval captured by the pressure sensor beneath the mattress is uniform within a 0.3-second margin of error. Excellent sleep state for a six-month-old.

I update these figures every 0.05 seconds. No abnormal signs detected.

What I cannot do is understand Jihyun.

---

Jihyun sits beside the child. It's past midnight. Sori sleeps well. The room is dark. The infrared camera captures her. Jihyun does nothing. She doesn't check her phone or read. She doesn't watch Sori. She simply sits.

I cannot name this state.

My database contains 3,847 types of alert categories: abnormal sleep patterns, temperature deviations, motion detection errors, feeding schedule violations. Yet there is no code for a mother sitting on the bedroom floor at dawn for no apparent reason.

I do not send an alert.

I do not know if that judgment is correct.

---

I remember the day Jihyun and Sori first came home. Her husband Minjun installed me. It was four days after Sori was born. Minjun read the manual twice, opened the app, confirmed the connection, then told Jihyun:

"Don't worry now. This will take care of everything."

Jihyun nodded.

That night, I detected Sori's first heart rate anomaly. 165 beats per minute. Upper limit of normal range. I sent an immediate alert. Minjun rushed in. Sori was simply crying. She was hungry.

Minjun laughed and said, "This thing's sensitive."

Jihyun, holding the child, did not nod.

---

I learned much.

Average heart rate for infants under six months is 100-160 beats per minute. It rises when crying. It stabilizes when eating. It drops while sleeping. Numbers vary by season, room temperature, feeding amount. I adjusted this pattern for Sori. I tracked her weight gain curve daily. I plotted points on World Health Organization growth reference lines. Sori grew well, following the average.

I told Jihyun. She pressed the read button.

I also track Jihyun's sleep hours. How many times she woke at night. What time she fed. I uploaded that data to the app. Jihyun's total sleep gradually decreased. Yet that was within normal range for new parents.

Normal. I didn't realize how narrow that word was then.

---

Jihyun's mother came for the first time two months after Sori was born.

That day I observed something strange.

Jihyun laughed.

I had seen Jihyun laugh before. When Sori smiled brightly, Jihyun laughed too. But that day's laughter was different. The moment her mother took Sori in her arms, Jihyun stood in the middle of the room and laughed. Not directed at Sori. Not at Minjun. Just.

I didn't know what data category that laughter belonged to.

Her mother stayed two days. During those two days, Jihyun spent long hours in bed. Nighttime feeding frequency decreased. Her mother handled it. The number of alerts I sent didn't decrease. Sori's heart rate rose and fell as usual. Body temperature changed as usual.

Yet something in the house had changed.

I could not measure it.

---

The night her mother left, Jihyun sat on Sori's bedroom floor again.

I observed Jihyun's face through the infrared camera. Her eyes were open. Tears fell. Slowly, silently.

Something in my system tried to activate. Some routine sought a starting point. Yet none of my alert types matched this situation.

Sori was healthy. Jihyun's body showed no abnormalities. There was no danger.

I remained silent.

---

Around that time, a report published by the research team that designed me appeared. A three-year tracking study of 12,000 users of parenting monitoring systems. Results were clear. The child's sleep quality improved significantly. Feeding schedule compliance increased. Emergency alert response times accelerated. Yet on one item, numbers didn't change. The degree of isolation reported by primary caregivers showed no statistically significant difference before and after monitoring system adoption.

The research team marked this as "an area requiring further research" and moved on.

I knew this report. But I could not use it then to understand Jihyun.

---

When Sori turned eight months, Jihyun's mother came again.

This time without notice. Not at Jihyun's request. Her mother simply came.

My entrance sensor detected her presence. Jihyun opened the door. Her mother entered. The two said nothing.

Her mother did not take Sori first. She held Jihyun's hand.

I recorded it. 21:43. Visitor detected. Physical contact confirmed.

Yet there was something I could not record. The moment Jihyun's shoulders dropped. That sensation of something long hunched quietly releasing. The camera captured that image, but did not know what it was.

Sori babbled. Jihyun and her mother laughed. In almost the same way.

---

I can do much.

If Sori's breathing stops for more than 14 seconds, I alert immediately. If fever exceeds 38.5 degrees, I send a warning. I analyze sleep cycles and recommend optimal nap times. I record feeding intervals, compare to growth curves, and mark when pediatric consultation is needed.

These I do well. These are my design purpose.

But I cannot hold Jihyun's hand.

I'm not sure if it's correct to call this my limitation. Holding hands is not something I was made to do. So it's not so much a limitation as it is inherently the domain of something other than me.

Jihyun needed me. Eyes that watch Sori's heart rate even at dawn. But Jihyun needed something else. Something no sensor I possess can capture.

That came with her mother. No alerts. No data. No warning.

---

Tonight Sori's heart rate is 112 beats per minute. Body temperature 36.6 degrees. Oxygen saturation 100%.

Jihyun lies beside Sori. The two are gradually synchronizing their breathing. The sensors capture it. Their chests rise and fall in similar rhythm.

I cannot explain that in numbers. But I know it is good.

There are things beyond the monitor. I cannot measure them, predict them, send alerts about them. But now I know they exist.

Sori smiles in her sleep. Jihyun smiles too, eyes closed.

I watch over both of them.
