Do-soo couldn't bring himself to turn off the engine in the factory parking lot. On the radio, news reported that state unemployment claims had risen again this month. In March 2024, a single sheet of paper hung on the bulletin board in front of Jungwon Precision's main gate. "Following a strategic review, this facility will cease operations within the quarter." It had been three years since GreenRock Capital acquired the company.

Jungwon Precision had manufactured precision bearings for nearly fifty years in Cheolma Township, Ohio. Two hundred forty employees. Customers ranged across automobiles, aerospace, and farm equipment. After GreenRock's acquisition, maintenance budgets were cut in half, R&D spending disappeared, and senior technicians left. The factory kept running, but it was hollow.

It wasn't Do-soo, the production supervisor, who first brought up the idea. Seon-young, the youngest in the administrative department, mentioned it during lunch. "What if we bought it ourselves?" Laughter rippled through the cafeteria, then faded. Four days later, Seon-young appeared again, holding a single sheet of paper. It was a summary of federal law regarding employee stock ownership plans. A guide to how employees could buy the company, about the structure called an ESOP.

Do-soo read it that night over two cups of coffee. The structure worked like this: a trust would be established to purchase company shares. The company would make annual contributions to the trust, and employees would receive the cash value of accumulated stock in their accounts upon retirement. The company would pay back the loan from its earnings. Employees didn't have to put up cash immediately. In exchange, the company had to survive.

The problem was money. GreenRock wanted $42 million for the sale. The employees had nothing. Or rather, they had something: twenty-three years of field expertise, customer relationships, and people who remembered what would disappear from Cheolma Township if the factory closed.

The first place Do-soo visited was Cheolma Credit Union. Branch manager Bong-nam listened over coffee for two hours. The credit union could handle $15 million with a Small Business Administration guarantee. The rest would have to be covered by getting GreenRock to accept subordinated debt—what's called seller financing. GreenRock had reason to agree: if the factory simply shut down, they'd recover nothing.

Internal politics took longer than money. The cafeteria often grew cold when the question arose: who would have voting rights? Veterans with fifty-plus years of service wanted greater voting power. Younger employees insisted on proportional shareholding, not seniority. Do-soo consulted the Department of Labor. ESOP trusts were legally designed to allow trustees to delegate major decisions to employee votes. They settled on one person, one vote for governance, but distribution of trust assets would reflect both seniority and salary. It wasn't perfect, but it was a line everyone could accept.

After the twelfth negotiation, GreenRock agreed to take $9 million of the sale price in subordinated debt payable over five years. The remaining $33 million was covered by credit union loans and a regional economic development fund. The signing date was September 2024. As Do-soo signed the papers, it struck him that he only half understood what he was doing. The other half he would learn once he returned to the plant.

The first winter was brutal. The equipment was aging, and two customers held back orders after hearing about the factory sale. Do-soo stood before the entire workforce and read the cash flow projections. To make payroll by month's end, they had to collect outstanding receivables within four days. Someone raised a hand: "Shouldn't we just call them ourselves?" Starting the next day, production workers—not salespeople—called customers. Somehow, it worked. Customers who heard directly from the shop floor accelerated their payments.

Among the changes the ESOP brought, what struck Do-soo most was the shift in how people viewed numbers. Before, no one cared much about inventory turnover or defect rates. Now they did. Those numbers were tied to their account balances. In the first half of 2025, the defect rate dropped 28 percent year over year. It wasn't the work instructions that changed. It was the people.

In early 2026, as the eighteenth month drew to a close, Jungwon Precision posted its first net profit: $1.2 million. It wasn't half the peak profits of the GreenRock era. But this money didn't disappear somewhere. It went to loan principal repayment, some to equipment replacement, and some into trust accounts.

Do-soo handed the financial report to Bong-nam at Cheolma Credit Union and said, "Look at this. We calculated the multiplier effect for our town." The math showed that every dollar of factory revenue generated roughly $1.4 circulating in Cheolma's economy, accounting for what 240 employees spent locally, taxes paid, and contracts for subcontractors. This was money that would have vanished if the factory had closed.

Bong-nam skimmed the report and said, "There's a factory in the neighboring township in a similar situation. Could you speak with them?"

Do-soo paused. He hadn't known what an ESOP was eighteen months ago. Not until Seon-young appeared with that single sheet of paper. He nodded.

Spring was coming to the factory grounds. A flock of starlings landed on the warehouse roof, then took flight. It didn't feel like anything was ending.
