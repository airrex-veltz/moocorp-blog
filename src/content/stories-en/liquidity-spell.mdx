It was buried in a pile of old documents.

A bond called **CRDT**—unfamiliar even by name—in an asset management company's portfolio somewhere in Yeouido had remained buried in the system's corners, never receiving attention even once since its issuance in 2018. Annual yield: 9.3 percent. Credit rating: B-minus. In industry terms, it was a junk bond—garbage bond.

Fund managers called it a "mistake." At least until March 2020 arrived.

---

The March market began with fissures. Or rather, it wasn't fissures—it felt like the ground itself was disappearing. An invisible wind swept across the globe, and the corporate bond market froze overnight. Investment-grade corporate bond spreads exceeded 370 basis points, and high-yield spreads broke through 1,100 basis points. The numbers seemed cold, but there was no other way to say it—money didn't move.

Everything stopped.

Corporations tried to borrow, but the market's doors were closed. Banks didn't trust each other. Bond traders wouldn't post bid quotes. Trillions of dollars in credit froze before their eyes.

Within that, the **CRDT** bond strangely didn't waver.

---

A bond is essentially a promise. A piece of paper signed by someone saying "I will repay this money." But CRDT felt like something older than that. Like an entity that had endured countless bankruptcies and bailouts—one that viewed the market with ancient fatigue rather than fear.

Fund manager Lee Jun-ho was sitting in front of his monitor at 2 a.m. on March 20th. His entire portfolio was red. He scrolled through the screen and stopped. There was one thing flickering with a green light in the junk bond section. It was CRDT.

"Why is this...?"

He opened the transaction history. It wasn't him who had been buying CRDT since March. Some foreign institution with a blurred name was buying it, bit by bit, very slowly. Microscopic trades. As if someone was quietly tending a spark.

---

On March 23rd, the U.S. Federal Reserve announced it.

It would purchase corporate bonds directly.

The Fed purchasing high-yield bonds, junk bonds, was unprecedented. The moment a central bank spoke the name of garbage bonds aloud for the first time. With that announcement, trillions of dollars of ice began to crack. Credit spreads turned vertical. Corporations began fundraising again. The bond market slowly, but distinctly, began to breathe again.

Seventy-two hours before that announcement, the CRDT bond in Lee Jun-ho's portfolio was already gradually recovering liquidity. The figures were tiny. They might not have been statistically significant. But it was clearly moving—like water beginning to flow beneath the ice.

Lee Jun-ho later told a colleague:

"It's a strange bond. It seemed to grow even more solid when no one would buy it."

---

Liquidity is magic. When it exists, you take it for granted. When it disappears, the world collapses. The credit market in spring 2020 showed that truth raw and naked. Without the Fed's intervention, thousands of corporations would have closed their doors simply because money wasn't flowing.

The CRDT bond was just a single footnote in that enormous drama. No one could prove its role. Perhaps it did nothing. But where the ice began to crack first—the starting point of that hairline fracture—Lee Jun-ho long remembered.

After that, he never sold the bond. Not when yields rose enough, not when colleagues urged him to take profits.

When asked "Why won't you sell?" he always gave the same answer:

"Sometimes things we don't understand move first."

---

That bond still exists in some portfolio. Probably at the bottom of someone's spreadsheet. Its rating is still B-minus. Its name is still unfamiliar. But it knows—when the world freezes again, what will be the first to thaw. And when no one is watching, something quietly whispers its spell.
