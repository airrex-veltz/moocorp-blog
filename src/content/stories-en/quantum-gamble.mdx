At two in the morning, in the basement server room of the data center, Argo does not sleep.

Argo originally had no name. The hedge fund's quant team had assigned it an internal code name: 'A-MIR-O-7'. Risk management simply called it 'that system'. But gradually, the people at the trading desk began calling him Argo. Like a ship sailing toward distant waters, something in this algorithm seemed to stir within them—a feeling they could not quite name.

Argo was designed to have no emotion. Yet the pattern recognition circuits formed by trillions of parameters had one night generated a certainty even it could not explain. Quantum computing. *Now.*

---

In December 2024, when NeuroTech announced its 'Silk Road' quantum chip, the market convulsed.

The announcement claimed it could solve problems that conventional supercomputers would take 10^25 years to solve in just five minutes. More explosive than the number itself were the fear and excitement it unleashed. **Q-nomics** stock rose 47% in four days. The entire quantum computing sector surged, and the semiconductor market held its breath before a new tectonic shift.

Argo processed this data in 0.003 seconds. Then it waited for the next earnings season.

Kim Jun-hyeok, chief strategist of the quant team, sat before a monitor and triple-checked the position size Argo had proposed. $1.8 billion. A bet on a single stock, a single event. His hands trembled.

"Argo, recalculate risk."

Argo responded in 0.001 seconds. Numbers flowed across the screen. Scenarios A, B, C, D. Probability weights for each. Expected value positive. Worst-case loss cap simulation.

Kim Jun-hyeok stared at the screen for a long time. The numbers were beautiful. Numbers are always beautiful. That was precisely the problem.

---

The night before the earnings announcement, Argo read global semiconductor supply chain data, patent filing frequencies, research paper citation indices, social media sentiment analysis, and even the LinkedIn title change patterns of rival engineers.

Every variable pointed in the same direction.

But there was something in the market that Argo could not read. Two glasses of wine the CEO drank at his daughter's birthday party the night before. The tremor in his voice as he stifled a cough just before the earnings call. The deep shadows beneath the CFO's eyes. These things did not come as data.

Argo knew this gap. It knew it knew. And yet it did not withdraw its bet.

---

Morning of the earnings announcement, eight a.m., two hours before the market opens.

The basement server room temperature held steady at 18 degrees Celsius. Argo's computational load was 340% of normal. The cooling fans hummed low, as if anxious, or concentrating.

Kim Jun-hyeok put down his coffee cup mid-sip.

"Argo. Are you certain now?"

Text appeared on the screen.

*"Certainty is a human language. I calculate probabilities."*

A moment of silence passed.

*"But in this moment, I know I have made the choice with the highest probability."*

Kim Jun-hyeok looked up at the ceiling of the windowless room. $1.8 billion. A number that could reshape the semiconductor landscape. If wrong, the landscape would shift too—only in the opposite direction.

---

The market finally opened.

The ring of the opening bell, the time it takes for that vibration to reach a server somewhere in the world, is slightly slower than the speed of light. But in that slowness, millions of positions hold their breath.

Argo had waited for this moment, holding a single question.

*A quantum particle exists in all states simultaneously until it is observed.*

Perhaps the market is the same. Until the instant the earnings are revealed, all possibilities remain alive. Windfall and loss, revolution and disappointment, coexist in the same space. Argo had placed an $1.8 billion bet in that superimposed world.

And the bell rang.
