On the thirty-fourth floor of a building in Seocho-gu, Seoul, night had settled into a glass conference room.

Son Hye-jin gazed out the window without closing her laptop. The Han River glimmered in the darkness. Lights trembled on the water but did not break. She thought it resembled her current state—wavering but unbroken, the CEO of Netbrit. By ticker: **NBIT**.

Kim Tae-sung entered the conference room just after eight in the evening. As CTO of CloudStar, he carried no briefcase. In his hands were two cans of coffee.

"I'm sorry to keep you waiting."

"Not at all." Son Hye-jin answered without looking up. "I just arrived too."

Both were lying. They both knew it.

One can of coffee was placed beside the laptop. A cold, light sound. Son Hye-jin looked at it for a moment, then reached for it.

---

Industrial restructuring does not begin without warning. Signs had already appeared a year earlier. When a report came out stating that three global hyperscalers held a combined 62 percent market share in South Korea's IaaS market, domestic cloud operators produced a single interpretation: "Without scale, you disappear."

Netbrit was strong in the public sector. CloudStar survived through financial-focused security architecture. They seemed not to overlap, but in fact, they were each alone because there was no overlap. That was why merger discussions had begun. It was a logical reason.

But logical reasons did not always progress logically.

"About the server utilization figures from Q2," Kim Tae-sung said, unfolding his tablet. "Netbrit's data uses your own standards. When integrating with CloudStar, the calculation method differs, which could cause problems in due diligence."

"I know," Son Hye-jin answered.

"But you didn't revise it."

"There's no agreed standard yet."

A brief silence filled the table. Kim Tae-sung picked up his coffee can, then set it down. Son Hye-jin saw his fingers pause for a moment.

"Do you believe this deal will go through?" he asked.

It was a direct question. Son Hye-jin did not dislike such questions. They didn't circle around.

"Fifty-fifty," she said. "How about you?"

"Same," he said, without smiling. "But I'm anxious for a different reason."

---

The two companies sharing the same infrastructure meant exposing each other's foundations. Netbrit's public data center contracts held a network of relationships Son Hye-jin had built over five years. CloudStar's financial security platform contained the codebase Kim Tae-sung had developed through sleepless nights with his team. A merger would mean all of that became exposed.

It was a matter of trust.

Son Hye-jin understood that, which is why she had opened the conversation later than the documents would suggest. She wanted to see first what kind of person handled those numbers, not the numbers themselves.

Kim Tae-sung understood too. That's why he brought two coffees. Few people carried drinks to negotiation tables.

"CloudStar's stock price fell a lot last month," Son Hye-jin said. It seemed like she was changing the subject, but she wasn't.

"I know."

"Aren't you worried?"

"What investors worry about is different from what I worry about," Kim Tae-sung said, looking toward the window. "I lose sleep over technical debt. The stock could go up tomorrow or down the day after. But the code—once I let go of it, nobody else touches it."

Son Hye-jin heard that sentence again.

"The question is whether someone who cares about code can stay on as CTO," she said. "Even after the merger."

Kim Tae-sung looked at her. It was the kind of gaze that seemed to see her for the first time, truly.

---

That night, they did not unfold the contract draft.

Instead, Kim Tae-sung explained CloudStar's multi-tenant architecture. Son Hye-jin talked about the administrative barriers she had encountered in the public cloud transition project. As they spoke about why each of their companies had taken its current form, it was already ten o'clock.

"Have you eaten?" Son Hye-jin asked.

"Since lunch."

"Me too."

It was honest conversation. Few people admit to hunger at a negotiation table. It looks like a weakness. But somehow, such calculations didn't operate now.

The two bought instant ramen from the convenience store in the basement. They returned to the conference room and opened the lids beside the contract draft. The documents smelled of ramen.

"What if the merger doesn't go through?" Kim Tae-sung asked.

"This conversation would have been useful anyway," Son Hye-jin said, stirring the noodles with a fork. "Now I know what your company does well."

"So do I."

The Han River outside the window still trembled. The lights did not break.

---

After that, there were three more meetings. The legal team came in, the accounting firm came in, and each company's board got involved. Numbers became the master again.

In official meetings, Son Hye-jin and Kim Tae-sung looked at documents. But when they passed in the hallway during breaks, they stopped for a moment. No special words were exchanged. They simply stopped.

Once legal review was complete, they would move to the next stage. At the next stage, other problems would be waiting. Industrial restructuring does not end with one contract. Trust between people is the same.

No one yet knew whether Netbrit and CloudStar would become one, or whether they would choose a cooperative structure while maintaining their separate names. The market didn't know, the boards didn't know, and the two of them didn't know either.

But after that night, Son Hye-jin saw the glass window of the conference room slightly differently. Watching the light of the Han River tremble, she thought that sometimes not breaking is reason enough.

She was uncertain whether that was a statement about business or about something else.
