Appand's office was a three-minute walk from Gangnam Station Exit 4—or more precisely, a twelve-minute walk that the real estate agent had insisted was three minutes. For three years, Jung Seung-hyun walked those twelve minutes every day to meet the imaginary users in his head who loved his app.

The app was called "Rice Friend." A social matching app for people who hated eating alone. The concept was simple: connect someone who didn't want to have lunch alone today with another solo diner nearby. Jung Seung-hyun came up with the idea at a bar, and the fact that three out of five people there said "Hey, that's not bad" was enough to get him pitching for seed funding.

Three years ago, on that day, he created a formula on an Excel sheet calculating TAM (total addressable market): "Number of one-person households in Korea × three meals a day × 365 days = some huge number anyway." He took that formula to investors and raised KRW 500 million. The investors nodded at the number. Nobody asked what it actually meant. That's how the startup myth works. Big numbers make big questions disappear.

For the first six months, Jung Seung-hyun came in at 9 a.m. every day and rewrote the same sentence on Notion: "We are solving the real problem of loneliness." The sentence changed a little each time. From "solving loneliness" to "revolutionizing dining experience," then to "reducing the friction cost of relationships." Every time the sentence changed, his team applauded. Nobody asked the users. Asking users was for later. Now was the time to "align the vision."

In year one, the first prototype launched. He gathered 200 beta testers. Jung Seung-hyun believed these 200 were "early adopters." In reality, they were people who wrote their names on a survey form because someone mentioned free Starbucks gift cards. Of those who downloaded the app, only 14 actually requested a match. Of those 14, only 2 actually met. Those 2 didn't like each other and left after 20 minutes, leaving a review that said so. In the team meeting, Jung Seung-hyun summarized this feedback as "initial friction observed, but an early signal of product-market fit." On the whiteboard in the conference room, the letters "PMF" were underlined three times in highlighter.

In year two, when investors asked "Why is user retention so bad?" Jung Seung-hyun answered, "We're currently focusing on cohort quality rather than growth." The sentence meant nothing, but the room went quiet and they moved to the next item. In the startup world, sentences like that are like bulletproof vests. Nobody wants to take them off. Because if you do, there's nothing inside.

When he was preparing for Series A, Jung Seung-hyun conducted what he called real user interviews for the first time. He met 20 people in person. This happened only after a mentor at a startup accelerator nagged him for the third time: "Please just go out and meet real people." Jung Seung-hyun sat in a café with his laptop and read from his question sheet. "Was there anything inconvenient about eating alone?"

Seventeen out of 20 answered like this: "Not really. Eating alone is comfortable. I watch YouTube."

Jung Seung-hyun wrote down the answer in his notebook and felt his hands trembling. Three years of time, KRW 500 million in seed funding, KRW 1.2 billion in pre-Series A, eight team members' lives, and most of all, 47 Notion pages with the sentence "the real problem of loneliness"—in that moment, he heard them all scatter into the air. Or so he would recall later. Of course, no such sound exists. All that existed was the café's air conditioner and his own heartbeat.

What about the other 3 people? One said honestly: "Actually, I don't even have the app installed. I came to get the interview fee." One said they'd go to the bathroom and never came back. The last one stared at Jung Seung-hyun's face for a while, then said this: "But you're the CEO who made this, right? Could you have lunch with me instead? I signed up because I didn't want to eat alone."

When he heard that, Jung Seung-hyun laughed. Later, he'd be told this laughter was "an early symptom of burnout," but in that moment, it was just funny. Three years of work, a matching algorithm, eight team members, KRW 1.7 billion in funding, a TAM slide, bulletproof sentences about cohort quality—and the conclusion of it all was "Just have lunch with me."

Appand quietly shut down that winter. The official announcement said "due to changing market conditions and difficulties in raising capital." This sentence was also a bulletproof vest that hid the real reason. The real reason was simple: people were comfortable eating alone. And nobody had asked them that three years earlier.

What is Jung Seung-hyun doing now? He works as a product manager at a startup in Gangnam. Every Tuesday at the new feature planning meeting, he asks the same question: "Did you actually ask the users about this?" His teammates hate that question. Last week his boss told him, "Stop obsessing over details and think bigger about the vision."

And every lunch hour, Jung Seung-hyun goes alone to the kimbap restaurant in front of the office and eats while watching YouTube. Very peacefully. He's now successfully solving every day the problem he spent three years trying to solve. Only without the app.
