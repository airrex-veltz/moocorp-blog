Kang Jae-ho woke at five in the morning.

Three hours before the Global Metal Exchange opened. Outside the window, Seoul's Yeouido lights spread across the sky, and he sat at his desk and turned on his screen. A sip of coffee, a check on nickel futures prices. A motion repeated for twenty-three years.

His official title was a commodities trader, but what he actually did was sell spreads. The minute gaps between source prices and demand-site prices, those few hundred dollars carved into commissions. It was a profession of instinct. Trading done not with data but with intuition.

Copper futures hung on the screen. The three-month contract had plummeted 4.7% from the previous day.

He read the number three times. It wasn't unusual. Lately, this was the norm.

---

Since 2024, implicit volatility in commodity markets had risen to chronic levels. Recurring US-China trade disputes, demand structure disruptions from the green energy transition, and now high-frequency algorithmic trading combined to make daily price swings of 5% routine. Kang's hedging costs tripled compared to 2023. His margins had been halved.

He had known for some time. But knowing and feeling occupied different realms.

---

The problem was not one day. Like spring giving way to summer, it was a change impossible to pin to any single moment.

Last October, he had hedged his copper position. It was textbook judgment. Four days later, algorithmic players liquidated positions in the opposite direction all at once, and his hedge added loss to loss. Defense had become attack. The future rebounded 2.1% late that afternoon before London close, and Kang took hits from both sides.

It was difficult to call it strategy. It was no longer strategy but standing on the opposite side of gambling, which is to say, losing a bet with no odds at all.

---

He opened the company messenger. A message from the Singapore desk was waiting.

"Manager, did you recheck this month's copper margins? Once you subtract hedging costs, it's essentially zero."

Kang didn't reply. Instead, he looked toward the window. Yeouido's buildings were catching the light of first dawn, reflecting off the glass.

Twenty-three years he had endured on instinct. The shifting atmosphere as futures expiry approached, the widening bid-ask spreads when rumors of mine strikes circulated, the liquidity gaps that opened in that slack London afternoon hour after lunch. All of it was an asset built since he was twenty-five.

But algorithms didn't smell. They didn't read the room. They operated only on speed with no senses, and before speed, experience lost meaning.

---

It became six in the morning.

Kang didn't let the screen go dark. He slowly opened that chart again. His finger paused over the mouse.

He didn't open a position. Didn't close one either.

He just watched the numbers move. Dawn over Yeouido was quiet, and London was still two hours away.

Some endings are not loud. No documents, no retirement ceremony, just arriving one morning with the realization that you no longer speak this market's language.

The light outside the window faded gradually. Dawn was breaking.
