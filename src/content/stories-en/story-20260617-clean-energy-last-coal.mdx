That morning, I took the rental car off the highway exit. As I turned left where the GPS directed, the landscape shifted abruptly. At the edge of the fields stood three smokestacks. They rose against the gray sky—not with smoke, but emptiness. The smoke was already gone.

As the executive vice president of our company, **HNRG**—Han-Cheong Energy—today's purpose could be summed up on paper as "site inspection and handover review." But I left those documents on the back seat.

A man was waiting in front of the power plant's main gate. Lee Man-soo. His face was past sixty, his hair nearly all white. I later learned he had worked at this plant for thirty-three years. Even as he extended his hand in greeting, his eyes were fixed on the smokestacks behind me.

"I knew you would come," he said. His voice was neither welcoming nor resentful.

Inside, the turbine room was dark and cool. The machines were silent. Two years ago, the government had announced it would phase out seven aging coal plants by the end of 2025. This one was last on the list. Three months before the deadline, before the fuel ran out, they quietly turned off the lights.

Lee Man-soo guided me silently. A handwritten memo still clung to the control room wall. A shift handover note. The ink had faded, but the letters were clear: "6 a.m. Burner 3 inspection recommended." Looking at it, I felt the documents I had brought vanish from my mind.

"What about the people who worked here?" I asked.

"Eleven left last month," Lee Man-soo said matter-of-factly. "They called it 'voluntary retirement.' Five applied for the retraining program. They're preparing for solar installation technician certificates."

Instead of nodding, I paused. Our company's press releases mention the phrase "job transition support program" several times. The subsidy figures are specified. But nowhere does it say what it weighs on someone in their mid-fifties learning a trade for the first time.

Lee Man-soo opened a door to one side of the warehouse. Old tools were arranged neatly. Dust-free, as if someone had polished them one last time before leaving.

"What's the plan for these?" he asked, looking at the tools but not at me.

I knew the answer. Disposal. Or auction. The handover manual said so. But the words would not leave my mouth. I simply looked at the tools with him.

From the rooftop, I could see the ridge behind the power plant. Beyond it lay the old coal mines. Where the region's economy once concentrated, then dispersed. Now, plans were in place to develop a wind turbine farm there. It was our company's contract.

The wind was cold. Lee Man-soo pulled out a cigarette, then put it back in his pocket. For a few seconds, there was silence.

"It's not that renewable energy is bad," he said slowly, looking toward the smokestacks. "Just... I wish someone would remember that this place existed too."

I had no answer. I could not find one. I simply looked at the smokestacks, the wind, and the man. As Han-Cheong Energy's executive vice president, my purpose for being here today was clear. But it felt smaller than these smokestacks.

As the sun descended, the power plant's shadow stretched across the parking lot. Before getting back in the rental car, I turned to look at the building one more time. The smokestacks cut precisely through a patch of sky. Without smoke, without emission, they simply stood. Still.
