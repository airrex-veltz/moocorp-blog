Joo-young smelled dust before coffee when the elevator opened on the fifth floor.

The top-floor café of Hansung Department Store. Two decades ago, seats were impossible to find. By 2 p.m., shopping bags packed every table. Women lingered three hours over one cake slice. Now, of sixteen tables, only three held cups. Those belonged to no one yet.

He sat by the window and placed a manila envelope on the table. He didn't let go.

---

Inside were three documents.

First: a consulting report. Summary page numbers were crisp. Last year nationwide offline department store foot traffic: -19% YoY. Hansung wasn't alone. Same period online retail channels: +41% growth. Consumers age 30 and under visiting department stores "regularly": 11%.

Second: legal team closure procedure review.

Third: unsigned board resolution form.

---

Joo-young had occupied Hansung's CEO chair five years. Before that: CFO. Before that: strategic planning. His first day: age 27, his father Sung-jin still led operations. Sung-jin wasn't the founder—Joo-young's grandfather, Bong-yeol, was.

1955. Two years post-Korean War. Bong-yeol opened a 120-pyeong shop downtown. Hansung Trading. First: men's suiting and underwear. Next year, shoe department. Four years later: three-story building. The name "department store" came in 1963.

Joo-young had memorized that history. He'd heard it since childhood. He had no choice.

---

11 a.m. HR Director Dae-ho came up.

"Staff waiting in conference room."

The word "staff" caught in Joo-young's throat. Hansung's total workforce: 987. Regular sales floor employees: 218. The rest: brand consignees.

"How many?"

"Twelve with union rep."

Joo-young slipped the envelope into his bag. His hand shook slightly.

Down the escalator beside the first-floor cosmetics counter. A sales rep waved a scent sample at passing customers. Two walked past without looking. The rep's smile didn't drop. That expression lingered in his vision.

---

Across the table sat Park Sun-ja. Women's casual department. 32 years with the company. She was already seated when Joo-young first entered this building.

The union chair read prepared questions. Restructuring scale. Severance terms. Role assignments in online transition—where did sales floor staff fit?

Joo-young listened. He separated what he could answer from what he couldn't.

"Board resolution hasn't been finalized yet. Official statement comes after."

The union chair paused.

"Then why convene this meeting?"

Joo-young hesitated.

"To listen."

The room went quiet. Sun-ja opened her mouth.

"President, I've worked here 32 years. When my daughter said she wanted to work in the same building, I told her no. I knew retail was dying. But back then..." Her voice didn't steady. "...I loved it. The sound of customers entering. The register receipt machine sound. Christmas tree lights turning on. That love—it wasn't wrong, was it?"

Joo-young neither nodded nor shook his head.

---

After the meeting, he climbed back to the rooftop.

Seoul's 11 a.m. darkens fast. Crowds below were starting to thin. Two decades ago at this hour, lines stretched from the ground floor. Year-end sales. Ticketing systems. Department store loyalty points. Strategy meant creating reasons for people to come. Devices. Crowds. The room alive.

Now the reason had to be invented on screens. Apps. Notifications. Recommendation algorithms. The same strategy inside phones.

Father Sung-jin's parting words surfaced: *"As long as this building stands in Seoul's heart, people will come."*

He'd been right then. The world had changed since.

---

Past midnight, in his home study.

The third document unfolded. Board resolution. His name, three characters, printed.

The signature block was empty.

Within two months: four of five operated stores close. Company-wide transition to digital. Among 218 sales floor staff, most become severance candidates. No position in the online transition roadmap.

Refusing means continued quarterly losses. Default on bank covenants could trigger restructuring professionally appointed. They'd execute without feeling. Results identical or faster, same direction.

He reopened the first document, consulting report. On the last page: a footnote.

> *This analysis did not quantify the intangible value provision of offline space—brand experience, customer loyalty, community sense. Such elements' loss cost is measurably long-term but outside this report's scope.*

He read twice: *measurable but beyond scope.*

Outside the window: Seoul's night lights. Thousands of them. Which were opening windows, closing—indistinguishable from this distance.

He closed the papers. Stamp remained in the desk drawer untouched.

Tomorrow morning something would decide. Or tomorrow morning he'd sit in this spot again, lights unchanged.

He conceded for the first time: he didn't know. Not knowing, sitting there honestly, felt his most honest act now.

---

That night Park Sun-ja, before leaving, toured her corner. Regular habit. Tag spacing adjusted. Wrinkles smoothed. Thirty years making the same motion; hands moved without thinking.

Last she checked: lighting angle matched the merchandise perfectly. Small thing. Tomorrow nobody might notice.

She still adjusted it.
