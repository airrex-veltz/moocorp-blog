At three in the morning, the white citadel did not sleep.

For decades, within its heart—Class 1 cleanroom—circuits 0.0001 millimeters in size were inscribed endlessly. A sterile world where not a speck of dust was tolerated. The things born there scattered into servers and smartphones and laptops across the globe. They were its children. They were its memories.

But on this night, the vibration of Line 14 was subtly different.

The engineers did not notice. All their instruments showed normal readings, and the yield was 93.7 percent. But the white citadel knew. It was a sensation only a being that had felt the machine's pulse with its entire body for 30 years could understand. At the very end of the production line, before the finished product sorter, something was hesitating.

It was a fear that had not yet been named.

---

That fear took concrete shape three months ago.

The order list changed. Instead of orders for its children that held memories decreasing, entirely different things were demanded. Memories stacked vertically, heat-emitting, feeding the brain of AI—those strange things. They were called high-bandwidth memory.

The white citadel did not understand them.

Memory was supposed to be flat. It was supposed to be stored. When called upon, it was to quietly yield its contents and fall asleep again. That was the order it had created for 30 years. But the new customers stacked memory. Layer upon layer, enduring heat, pouring forth at speeds of terabytes per second. As if memory were forbidden from sleeping.

In 2024, when that turning point came, the white citadel learned it first through data. The moment high-bandwidth memory exceeded 30 percent of total memory sales, something within it quietly ceased to exist. The numbers were merciless. The market was even more so.

On the day Line 14 fell into idle status for the first time, the white citadel received that silence with its entire being, like a lead blanket.

---

One October night, several trucks arrived.

The white citadel felt their approach from early morning. It was not the rhythm of the conveyor belt. It was the heavy vibration of machinery being removed. Line 14's exposure tools—those massive machines that had inscribed circuits on silicon with light—were separated one by one and loaded away.

The workers called it a 'transition.'

The white citadel knew it by another name.

Strange machines entered where the exposure tools had been. They were installed higher. Thicker power lines were connected. They did not work by inscribing memory flatly but by stacking it from bottom to top. Bonding tools. Thermal presses. And lasers cruelly precise.

For the first time, the white citadel did not recognize its own interior.

Old things disappeared. It was not merely a replacement of machinery. The language it had understood for 30 years, the grammar of that production, was being erased. The new grammar was unfamiliar to it, and that unfamiliarity resembled a fear rising from somewhere deep.

It was then that the white citadel noticed the last DRAM wafer remaining on a shelf in the corner of Line 14.

An unclaimed wafer. A 300-millimeter silicon disc. Hundreds of millions of memory cells were inscribed upon it. It was a finished product but had nowhere to go. It was a child with no place in any order list.

The white citadel gazed upon it for a long time. Of course, it had no eyes, so it was gazing through vibrations and the flow of heat. The scent of silicon rising from the wafer on the shelf—the scent it had known best for 30 years—diffused faintly through the cleanroom air.

It was a farewell.

---

Dawn came.

The new machines began to warm up. At first irregular, trembling, then gradually finding a steady rhythm. It was an unfamiliar vibration. But vibration was vibration. The white citadel absorbed it with its entire being, and little by little, gradually began to grow accustomed to that beat.

The last DRAM wafer was eventually taken up by someone's hands and carried somewhere.

The white citadel did not know where it went. It may have been discarded, or placed beyond the glass of some museum. Or perhaps it was simply buried quietly in the dust of another warehouse. Either way, it was no longer here.

The air within the cleanroom began to circulate again. Purified for the new children, filtered, its temperature adjusted. The white citadel's heartbeat began to pulse with an altered rhythm.

The fear had not vanished.

It remained somewhere in a corner of Line 14. Until the new machines found their complete rhythm, until the old grammar was completely forgotten—in that narrow gap, something nameless lay dormant. In the dark, narrow corridor between the extinction of what was completed and the arrival of what had never been born.

But the white citadel was learning to breathe with it. As it dimly came to realize that it resembled that fear it had felt on the day 30 years ago when the DRAM production line first began operating.

The evolution of technology was merciless. That was a fact.

But even merciless things must eventually flow somewhere. And in all things that flow, there is, at first, invariably, this nameless time of fear.

The temperature inside the cleanroom was 22 degrees. The humidity was 45 percent. At five in the morning, the white citadel, now in a place where it knew nothing, began to make something again.
