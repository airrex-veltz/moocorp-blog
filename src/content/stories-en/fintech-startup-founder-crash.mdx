## Before the Flame Goes Out

The morning **Singeum Finance** stock halved for the first time, Han Jun-seo gazed down at Seoul's skyline of towering buildings from beyond the conference room window.

His reflection in the glass felt unfamiliar. At thirty-six, he was still young, yet his face bore the weight of thirty years of exhaustion.

He had founded the company in 2021. Its name was Singeum Finance, and its tagline was "Breaking the final barrier in payment." At first, investors laughed. By the second round, they lined up. Series C valued it at KRW 1.2 trillion. No one doubted it. At least not then.

---

The first signal of regulation came quietly.

In November 2025, the Financial Supervisory Service released a draft guideline on prepaid card protection. It mandated that electronic finance companies deposit customer funds in external trust accounts, segregated 100 percent. The industry initially responded formally, calling it "a natural measure."

Han Jun-seo saw it differently.

He read page seventeen of the guideline four times. Then he called the finance director.

"Pull up our working capital structure again."

The numbers came. They had been deploying customer deposits as short-term operating assets. It was legal. But once the new guideline took effect, that liquidity would freeze instantly. The amount was around KRW 87 billion.

The IPO roadshow was scheduled six months ahead.

---

Throughout winter, Han Jun-seo held meeting after meeting with financial regulators. Too courteous to call it lobbying, too futile to call it persuasion. Their faces were always the same: an expression of understanding, yet one that said the criteria could not change.

In March, the guideline was finalized. The grace period was six months.

A call came from the investment bank. They said the IPO timeline needed to be reconsidered. The language was euphemistic, but the meaning was clear: the current financial structure would struggle to pass listing review.

Han Jun-seo hung up and leaned back in his chair. He looked at the ceiling.

Four years.

Four years he had poured into this company. The livelihood of 340 employees, the data of approximately 1.9 million users, the expectations of investors — all of it was quietly crumbling in this room.

---

In mid-May, **Singeum Finance** stock plummeted again.

This time the market had figured it out itself. One analyst, having carefully read the financial disclosure, published a report. The title was cold: "Liquidity Risk Reassessment — Scenario Analysis Post-Regulation." It was a sell recommendation.

That afternoon, the stock fell 11.4%.

As Han Jun-seo watched the trading terminal, he felt a strange sensation. It was not sadness. It was not anger. It was a kind of clarity. The market had always worked this way. No emotion. No imagining the future. When numbers changed, prices changed. That was all.

Innovation was built by people who believed in possibility. But regulation saw the present state, not the future potential. At the point where those two perspectives collided, companies often collapsed quietly.

---

In late June, the board met.

There were two items on the agenda: indefinite postponement of the IPO, and restructuring.

Han Jun-seo did not ask for the floor. Instead, he put up one final slide: a customer testimonial.

*"For the first time, international transfers didn't feel scary. Thank you."*

No one spoke.

One director cleared his throat. The vote proceeded.

---

As Han Jun-seo left the conference room, he chose the stairs over the elevator. He walked down seventeen floors. With each step, he tried not to think. But thoughts kept seeping in.

It was hard to say regulation was wrong. Protecting customer funds was the right thing to do. He could not deny that principle. But the fact that a right principle had slowly suffocated another right attempt — that was the tragedy.

Evil had not triumphed over good. Two goods had simply passed each other.

As he pushed through the ground-floor door, the wind of early summer evening brushed his face. The city's noise still flowed in the same rhythm. The world had not changed at all.

That was the strangest part.
