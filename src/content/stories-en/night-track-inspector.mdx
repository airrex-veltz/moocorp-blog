1:47 AM.

All I hear is the sound of gravel crunching beneath my feet. The flashlight in my hand sweeps across the rails left and right. One bolt. One sleeper. I hold a ruler against the joint gaps and transfer the numbers to my notebook. The maintenance window runs from 1 AM to 4:30 AM—exactly three and a half hours. Within this window, when both the upbound and downbound tracks are closed simultaneously, I must inspect the entire section.

I am Hong Jun-ho. A track inspector with eleven years of experience.

When I first started this job, my senior told me three things. "On the tracks, remember only three things. Listen. Feel. Don't trust." I didn't understand what the third meant back then.

Tonight's section spans approximately 600 meters between the 7-kilometer upbound and 7-kilometer downbound markers. The gap between the two tracks is 4.7 meters. This width is my entire world. When a train passes, the airflow created between the cars is strong enough to stagger an adult male. Within the maintenance window, theoretically, I won't face that airflow. In theory.

A burst of static came from the radio.

"Jun-ho, this is control. An unscheduled test train formation may enter the maintenance window. Estimated passage time is 2:09. Please confirm your current section location."

I stopped and checked my GPS. I'm 340 meters inside the entry switch from my current position. I'd have to run to get back out.

"Currently at K-section, 340 meters in. 2:09 means I have 22 minutes. That's enough time."

"Confirmed. However, we've just been notified that the formation may depart earlier. The exact time is estimated between 2:02 and 2:09."

2:02. It's now 1:47. Fifteen minutes.

340 meters at a run. That's not difficult. I put my notebook in my pocket and adjusted my grip on the flashlight.

Then the radio crackled again.

"Jun-ho, additional notice. A depot return formation on the downbound track is scheduled to depart at 2:04. The formation will enter the section at an estimated 2:06."

I stood still for a moment.

The upbound could arrive as early as 2:02. The downbound at 2:06. Two formations entering this section simultaneously.

4.7 meters of space. I'm standing right in the middle of it.

A high-speed train at 300 km/h translates to 83 meters per second. Once a formation passes through the entry switch, it will pass this point in roughly four seconds. But speed isn't what I really need to worry about. It's pressure. The pressure wave a train creates reaches ahead of the car body itself. Powerful enough to knock a person standing on the tracks right off their feet.

If two trains pass simultaneously on either side of me, the pressure waves will squeeze in from opposite directions at the same time. In that moment, whether a human in 4.7 meters of space falls left or right becomes a physics problem.

"Control, is an emergency stop request possible at the entry switch?"

"Checking now. The test formation is on a different approval path. We estimate two minutes."

1:49.

I started running. Toward the upbound direction—the direction the upbound train will enter. I have to close that 340-meter gap. If I can reach that point before the upbound train enters, I can get outside the switch. The downbound enters from the opposite direction, so it will take longer to reach that point.

Gravel catches at my ankles. The flashlight illuminates ahead. Sleepers are spaced at regular intervals. The spacing between sleepers is 60 centimeters—shorter than an adult male's stride. If I break rhythm, my foot catches. Here's where the third thing my senior mentioned comes in. "Don't trust" means don't trust your body's rhythm. The stride you've mastered on flat ground betrays you on the tracks.

1:52. A voice came from the radio.

"Jun-ho, the test formation's departure time has been changed. 1:58."

Six minutes.

I didn't stop. I calculate the remaining distance in my head. If I increase speed, I'll trip. If I slow down, I won't have enough time.

When about 150 meters remain, my toe catches on the edge of a sleeper. My knee hits the gravel. The flashlight tumbles away. My palm burns.

As I get up, I place my hand on the rail.

That's when I felt it.

Vibration.

The second thing my senior mentioned. Feel.

The rail transmits micro-vibrations even when a train is more than 2 kilometers away. Metal carries waves faster than air. This vibration I feel through my palm isn't time—it's distance.

Close.

I grabbed the flashlight and ran again. The last 100 meters. I move by rhythm, not stride. I don't step on the sleepers—I step between them. Even intervals. Odd intervals. I abandon the pattern.

The switch signal light comes into view. Red.

I vault over the fence on the outside of the switch.

Wind rushes from behind me.

I press my back against the fence and catch my breath. My heartbeat rises to my ears. I hear a train being pulled into the section. Then another one from the opposite side.

Two formations pass through that 4.7 meters at the same time.

The controller's voice comes from the radio. "Jun-ho, confirmed you're clear of the section. Are you alright?"

I don't answer. I'm still out of breath.

Crouching on the gravel, I examine my palms. They're scraped shallow. No blood.

The maintenance window still has two hours and thirty-one minutes left. I've inspected less than half the section. My notebook is still in my pocket.

I stand up.

The track is still there.
