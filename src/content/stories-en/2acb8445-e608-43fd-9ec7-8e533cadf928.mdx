Min Seo-ha stared at the cloud band on the other side of the screen for a long time.

It was not an actual cloud. It was atmospheric pressure data processed by the company's servers, rendered in color. The red band was slowly descending along the east coast of the Korean peninsula. Four years had passed since he joined the company, but every time he saw this screen, he still felt something peculiar. There was something disappearing somewhere in the process where weather became an image, images became numbers, and numbers became money.

"This is the typhoon trajectory three hours from now."

He steadied his voice and looked at the video conference screen. Jo Min-chul, the risk management team lead at Han-byeol Insurance, sat on the other side. His tie was loosened, but his eyes were sharp.

Min Seo-ha worked at Ucheon Data, a climate information startup. By combining Korea Meteorological Administration satellite data, private IoT sensor networks, and raw radar data purchased from satellite companies, they had created a weather forecasting service for enterprises. When the founders first launched, people said no one would pay when the weather bureau provided data for free. It took the market three years to prove that statement wrong.

"The precision is different."

Min Seo-ha brought up two screens side by side. On the left was the public weather service's typhoon forecast; on the right was Ucheon Data's forecast. They were tracking the same typhoon, but the predicted landfall point differed by 120 kilometers.

"A difference of 120 kilometers means insurance claims can vary by more than double."

Jo Min-chul did not take his eyes off the screen. Min Seo-ha knew what that silence meant. He was calculating.

Earlier that year, according to data released by Hanpoong Reinsurance, the global economic loss from climate disasters in 2023 exceeded $380 billion. That number had shaken the insurance industry. The problem was not the loss itself, but the fact that the loss had been predictable and they had failed to predict it. Risk models were not keeping pace with reality, and only more precise data could bridge that gap.

The climate data market's gold rush began around that time.

Insurance companies rushed in first, followed by logistics companies, agricultural companies, and construction firms lining up. Companies like Cheong-poong Musan, which had supply chains across Southeast Asia, began using climate data to move inventory three days before floods. In truth, they were not buying precise climate data. They were buying decision time.

Competition grew rapidly. Subsidiaries of major overseas technology companies entered the domestic market and lowered prices. Long-established weather companies tried to shift toward consulting rather than data, but what clients wanted was not reports—it was API endpoints.

Min Seo-ha sent off the contract and sat back at his desk. He was alone in the office. The CEO and four developers were simultaneously running meetings at three different locations. The distinctive startup smell hung in the air—overheated laptops and delivery coffee mingling together.

On the screen, the red cloud band continued to move.

He paused to think. Weather had always belonged to everyone. You could not monopolize what happened in the sky. What Ucheon Data was selling was not weather, but the method of reading it. The method of filtering the signals millions of sensors poured out every second, of finding the current location in the accumulated patterns. It was closer to language than to weather.

And now, the distance between those who could read that language and those who could not was appearing as a 120-kilometer forecasting error.

The phone rang. It was Jo Min-chul's number. Min Seo-ha instinctively knew it was a call asking about contract terms.

Outside, the wind was gradually changing direction. The server knew that too. Three hours before people would feel it.
