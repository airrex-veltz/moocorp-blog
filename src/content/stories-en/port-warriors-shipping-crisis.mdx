At two in the morning, the wind at Berth 4 of Baekha Port smelled of iron before salt.

I had smelled this wind for 32 years. The container crane cab sits 40 meters above the water, and from there, the entire port looks like a circuit board. Yellow lights on the yard, yard tractors moving in formation, ship navigation lights waiting their turn beyond the breakwater. For 32 years, my fingertips knew where the current flowed through that circuit.

This is my final shift.

Hanseng Port Operations confirmed last spring the complete automation of berths 4 through 7. The remote control center is already installed on the third floor of the administrative building. Starting at six in the morning tomorrow, the three cranes at Berth 4 will move without people. The operator's chair will be ripped out, and eight cameras will be mounted in its place.

"Chief, coffee?"

Ji-hyun Oh climbed up the ladder. Twenty-nine years old, three years with the company. Two years younger than my son. He's been reassigned to the automation team. I haven't. The company didn't do anything wrong. I'm sixty-one, with four months until retirement, and putting someone with four months left on a new system is a waste of money. The math is sound. I know there's no room for emotion in sound math.

---

The problem started at 3:17 in the morning.

That week, Baekha Port was already struggling. After the Red Sea route closed, ships bound for Europe from Asia started going around the southern tip of Africa, adding more than ten days to round-trip voyages. When ten days slip, arrivals don't scatter—they clump. Two weeks of empty docks were suddenly hit with two days' worth of cargo yesterday. The yard occupancy rate was 94 percent, double the usual.

And then the gate died.

The Terminal Operating System—we just call it the TOP—stopped responding. No one knew if it was external intrusion or simple failure. One thing was certain. The brain that knew which container was in which block at which tier of the yard had simply gone blind.

An automated crane can't move an inch without coordinates. The three new cranes at Berth 7 stood frozen with their arms extended. Honestly, the sight of 40 meters of steel locked in midair was terrifying.

The radio crackled. It was Seon-woo Cha, the automation team lead. Thirty-seven, PhD in mechanical engineering, the smartest person in this port.

"Berth 4, can you do manual operations?"

I pressed the mic. "I can."

"Without coordinate data?"

"I've been doing this for 32 years."

---

I don't know what to call what we did that night.

The TOP was dead, so we went back to paper. An inspector walked the yard writing down container numbers by hand. When he called them in on the radio, the office wrote them down, then called them back to me. 1970s style. But it worked.

At 4:40 in the morning, I read the ship's draft with my eyes. The depth the vessel sits in the water tells you which side is heavier. You start unloading the heavy side first, or the ship lists. Computers calculate this from the stowage plan; I see it from the way the ship displaces water. Both give the right answer. I just work without a power grid.

Ji-hyun sat down beside me. He was on the automation team, but when the remote control center became useless, he came up here.

"Chief, what number are we on?"

"One hundred and ninety-two."

"How many per hour do you normally do?"

"Twenty-eight."

"We're at thirty-one now."

I didn't answer. I didn't want him to see my hands shaking. These are the hands of a sixty-one-year-old man. Hold a joystick for more than two hours and the inside of your forearm goes numb, and once numbness starts, the stopping error grows by five centimeters at a time. Five centimeters is still okay. Twenty centimeters, and people die.

---

10:30 a.m. One row left. Twenty-nine containers.

And then the wind shifted on the twenty-second box.

Peak gust speed: 18 meters per second. Regulations say stop operations over 15 meters per second. Even an empty 40-foot container weighs 4 tons, and when it starts swaying in the air, it becomes a sail, not cargo. There's a tractor operator below.

I stopped with the container suspended in air.

Cha spoke on the radio. "We should halt. It's regulation."

He's right. I remember an article from three years ago. Dockworkers at ports across the Atlantic stopped operations over automation gates being introduced. It lasted four days, automation was postponed, eventually delayed by a few years. Back then I thought: stopping doesn't change anything.

But what I was holding now wasn't that.

I just wanted to set down my last box.

"Chief," Ji-hyun called. "Let me check from below."

"Check what?"

"The wind. The yard flag isn't visible from here. Let me watch from the window and tell you. Three-second intervals."

He did. He counted eleven times. "Now—now—no—now."

I lowered on that fourth 'now'. Four tons landed silently on the yard tractor. Three-centimeter clearance.

We sat in that position until the wind died down. No one spoke.

---

Noon minus twelve minutes. The last container came down.

In 24 hours, Berth 4 of Baekha Port processed 1,180 containers. More than the automated berth's peacetime record. That's not something to boast about. Automation runs at the same speed 24 hours a day; people do this once and then suffer for days. It was strain, not a record.

When I stepped down from the cab, the ladder rung was hot. The sun was high.

Cha was standing on the dock. He held a stack of papers—container numbers hand-written by inspectors overnight.

"This," he said. "We scan it for training data. Everything about the order you processed things is right here. Our algorithm can't come up with this sequence. It can't see the draft."

I looked at the papers. My 32 years fit on forty pages of A4.

"Is that all?"

"No." He hesitated a moment. "We have to rewrite the emergency operations manual. When the TOP goes down, no one knew where a person should stand. You have four months left."

---

The next morning, I smoked a cigarette at the end of the breakwater.

The three cranes at Berth 4 were moving. Without people, without sound, precisely. The trolley slid, the spreader bar came down, and the box lifted. The cab window 40 meters up was empty. Only a handful of people at this port still know what that space smells like.

Machines don't remember when they stopped. The three cranes at Berth 7 that froze yesterday move today as if yesterday never happened. The memory stayed on forty pages of paper, and that paper went into a scanner.

I stubbed out my cigarette and walked to the administrative building. There is no seat for me in the third-floor remote control center. There is one in the conference room. A four-month seat.

I don't know if this is victory. But for 32 years, I believed the port needed people, and for one day, that was true. Some people matter for just one day, and there is a way to record that day.

The next ship was coming in behind the breakwater. The draft was deep. The starboard side was heavier.
