Nam Ki-chul pulled the document from his work uniform's front pocket at half past six in the morning.

It wasn't written as a summons. It was a notice from Hansol Parts' human resources department. "Important notification regarding strategic transition — 11 a.m., 3rd floor conference room." He folded the paper again and stuffed it back into his pocket. Oil still lingered on his fingertips.

Forty years. Since joining this factory in 1984, there was barely an engine block component that Nam Ki-chul had not touched. Crankshaft grinding was his specialty. Tolerance of 0.001 millimeter. Colleagues joked, "Does Engineer Nam read the scale with his heart?" He didn't laugh. Because it wasn't wrong.

The factory began operations at seven, but he always arrived two hours early. He loved the smell of the machinery when it was cool — the blend of cutting oil and metal dust — When he stood alone before the lathe during those quiet hours, it felt almost like prayer.

Today he breathed in that smell for a long time.

---

Two hundred factory workers gathered in the conference room at eleven. The CEO pulled up the presentation on screen. The EU's decision to ban new internal combustion engine sales from 2035 had been finalized two years ago, but it was only this year that automakers began demanding electrification roadmaps from parts suppliers. The screen displayed figures: order backlog, predicted decline rates, conversion investment scale.

"The engine-related departments will be gradually reorganized into motor assembly lines starting next March."

Nam Ki-chul sat in the front row. Behind him, the air stirred with unease. Someone swallowed. Someone wiped their nose.

He did not look at the screen. He looked at his own hands.

Oil stains don't wash away easily. They embed themselves in the creases of the palm, under the nails, lingering for days. He had never disliked it. He thought of it as a mark of work. A sign of being alive.

After the meeting, colleagues gathered in small groups to talk. Talk of retraining, talk of severance packages. Nam Ki-chul walked past the clusters and returned to the factory. There was afternoon work left to do.

---

A crankshaft lay on the machining table. He picked up the measuring instrument. His hand moved as it always did. He read the scale. 0.0008 millimeters. Within tolerance.

He could not count how many times he had done this procedure. It must have been hundreds of thousands of times. As long as internal combustion engine automobiles ran the roads, somewhere inside an engine sat a component that had been touched by his hand. That thought had always sustained him.

But now.

He finished the work and cleaned his tools. The cutting tool, the micrometer, the protractor. Each one returned to its place. He was not in a hurry. He did what he did every day.

As evening arrived, a junior colleague came and stood beside him.

"Senior, what will you do?"

He thought for a moment. Not because he didn't know what to say, but because he didn't want to speak of something unsettled as if it were decided.

"I'll come tomorrow too."

That was all. And it wasn't wrong. Tomorrow he would certainly come to work. As for what came after, he would think about it when that time came.

The factory lights went out. Nam Ki-chul locked the door last. Outside was a late September afternoon. The sky was clear. He walked toward the bus stop but stopped for a moment to look at his palm.

Oil still remained on it.
