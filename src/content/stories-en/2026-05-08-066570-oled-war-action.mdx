January 2013, the executive conference room on the top floor of the head office building in Yeouido, Seoul.

"A competitor unveiled a 55-inch OLED TV first at CES."

"Our product?"

"Still in mass production preparation. At the earliest, February..."

Vice Chairman Goo gazed out the window. Beyond the Han River lay Seoul's skyline. Somewhere in that city, the competitor's executives were celebrating their victory.

"We launch the production line within two weeks."

The conference room stirred. It was an impossible schedule. But there was no room for compromise in Vice Chairman Goo's voice.

Paju Display Factory. Around-the-clock three-shift operations began. Engineers abandoned sleep. The war to increase OLED panel yields had commenced. To produce a single panel, hundreds of processes had to be controlled perfectly. Temperature, humidity, microscopic dust—every detail mattered.

"Line 5 yield: 62%!"

"Still not enough. We need to exceed 70% for mass production."

Team Lead Kim darted across the production line. Where did problems occur in each process? Which variables reduced yield? He analyzed data, formed hypotheses, experimented, and analyzed again.

Two weeks passed. Or more precisely, the 13th night.

"Yield breakthrough at 72%!"

Cheers erupted. But the joy was brief—the next hurdle awaited. Price. A 55-inch OLED TV cost KRW 15 million. It was far too high for ordinary consumers to afford.

"We must reduce manufacturing costs."

The head of marketing spoke. But the research director shook his head.

"There are limits with current technology. OLED inherently carries high manufacturing costs."

It was a dead end. They had developed cutting-edge technology, but the market offered no price competitiveness. The competitor faced the same problem. In 2013, both companies launched OLED TVs, but sales were sluggish.

But Mir did not give up.

In 2014, Mir adopted a different strategy. WRGB method. By applying color filters to white OLED, they reduced manufacturing difficulty and increased yields. While color reproduction was somewhat inferior to the competitor's RGB OLED approach, they could slash prices dramatically.

2015. Mir's OLED TV sales surged. 400,000 units. 600,000 units. 1 million units.

The competitor eventually discontinued OLED TV production in 2015.

2016 CES. Crowds thronged the Display Company booth in Las Vegas. A "wall TV" just 2.57mm thick. The very concept of a TV you could mount on a wall was revolutionary.

Vice Chairman Goo smiled as he toured the booth. That urgent, hectic 13-day war three years ago. By not giving up then, this moment existed now.

Mir was the victor of the OLED war.

As of 2025, Mir's global OLED TV market share exceeds 60%. In a race once lost, the one who ran to the end emerged victorious.
