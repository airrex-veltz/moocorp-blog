Two hours south of Seoul, in Seosan, Chungnam, the refinery complex never sleeps. White smoke rises above the flare stack, and orange flames color one corner of the sky. Kang Min-jun has watched this sight for thirty years. When he first joined the company, those flames seemed miraculous. Now they were just a clock—a signal that things were still running.

Korea Energy Partners wasn't as large as XOM, but it was one of the country's top ten by refining capacity. Min-jun had started as the most junior member of the process technology team and now carried the title of head of strategic planning. His business card said "director," but people in the office hallway simply called him Director Kang. That had been true for thirty-two years.

Min-jun realized too late that his daughter Su-a hadn't hung up the phone.

"Dad, are you listening?"

"Yes."

"Tell me what I said."

A brief silence fell.

"Climate Justice Alliance rally. Next Saturday."

"That's all you heard."

Min-jun rolled down the car window. Night air rushed in on the highway back to Seoul. Su-a's voice was clearer than usual. The voice of someone who had made up their mind.

"I'm going to do it in front of your company, too. The press will be there."

"I understand."

"Is that all? Just 'I understand'?"

"What else do you want?"

A long exhale came through the phone. Min-jun recognized it—it was Su-a holding something back. She had done the same as a child. When she had too much to say, she breathed first.

"I just want you to think once about what we're doing."

"I've been thinking for thirty years."

---

Min-jun first heard the term "energy transition" around 2015. Shortly after the Paris Agreement, someone at an in-house seminar pulled up a slide. Carbon neutrality by 2050, renewable energy at 80 percent. Most people in the room smiled. A target is a target. Reality is reality. That was the feeling in the room then.

A decade later, in 2025, the International Energy Agency released its annual report stating that new renewable energy capacity globally had surpassed new fossil fuel capacity for the first time. The numbers were precise. 3,300 GW of new renewable capacity versus 500 GW of fossil fuels. A six-to-one ratio. Min-jun printed the report and placed it in his desk drawer. He neither threw it away nor carried it with him.

He assumed his daughter knew about that report. Su-a, who studied at Seoul National University's Graduate School of Environmental Studies, cited such figures as casually as breathing. But Min-jun's lack of fear at those numbers wasn't born from arrogance about understanding reality better. It was a strange mixture of resignation and exhaustion—the feeling of a person who stands on a path already laid down, even as they watch the world change.

---

The following Saturday, Min-jun didn't go to the office.

Not because he knew about the rally. Saturday was his day off anyway. Yet he sat in his room all morning, toggling news alerts on and off.

Around two in the afternoon, Su-a sent him a text.

"300 people came."

Min-jun stared at that message for a long time. He didn't reply.

Su-a stopped by the house that evening. She took off her sneakers in the entrance, and her expression suggested she was waiting for something from her father. Min-jun pretended to watch TV on the sofa. The news was covering the rally briefly. The camera caught a banner for a moment. The Korea Energy Partners logo appeared faintly.

"Did you see?"

Su-a asked.

"Yes."

"How do you feel?"

Min-jun set down the remote.

"I don't know."

It was the truth. Something incomprehensible—shame, anger, pride, sadness—was quietly mixing in his chest. Thirty years is not a simple stretch of time. Within it are overtime shifts, a colleague's death, a solitary evening at a steakhouse on a business trip. If someone asked whether that time had ruined the world, Min-jun could not answer. He knew it had also moved the world.

"Do you regret it, Dad?"

Su-a asked as she pulled water from the refrigerator. Min-jun couldn't tell if she was trying to continue the conversation or genuinely wanted to know.

"I'm not sure what regret even means."

Su-a took a sip of water and sat across from him.

"I've never thought you were a bad person."

"I know."

"But there are bad choices. Separate from the person making them."

Min-jun looked at his daughter. Su-a was twenty-six. When Min-jun was that age, he had started at the Seosan plant. The world was different then. A time when cities would stop without oil. He still didn't know whether he should accept having his choices from that era judged by today's standards.

"Su-a."

"Yes?"

"Can you see it clearly?"

Su-a thought for a moment.

"Not completely. But I can see the direction."

---

Min-jun couldn't fall asleep that night.

He pulled the IEA report from his desk drawer. The paper was slightly wrinkled. 3,300 GW of new renewable capacity. The number hadn't changed. But what it meant depended on who was reading it. For some, it was the future. For others, it was already a turning point that had passed.

Min-jun closed the report. Through the window, he could see Seoul's lights. Most of that light was still the result of fuel burning somewhere. Quietly, from a distance, like the flames at the plant.

He didn't call Su-a. Not because he had nothing to say, but because he didn't want to speak words that hadn't been fully formed. Thirty years of a career is not easily explained. And a daughter's conviction is not easily refuted.

The two of them were looking at the same facts. Only the ground they stood on was different.

The window had become cloudy. Min-jun gazed at it without checking whether it was moisture or dust.
