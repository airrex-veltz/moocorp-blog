Lee Soo-hyun pressed the doorbell for the third time.

As soon as the elevator doors opened, he walked all the way to the end of the hallway. Unit 501. The manila envelope was already damp with sweat in his hand. An old eleven-story apartment building. On his first visit, he had heard that the view of the Han River from the windows was this home's pride. It had been spring then too.

"Who is it?"

"It's Lee Soo-hyun from Saemul Asset Management. As I mentioned last week—"

The sound of a chain unlocking cut him off.

Oh Myeong-cheol was sixty-eight. After spending thirty years as a high school geography teacher, he had retired. Four years ago, he had entrusted his severance pay and his wife's insurance money to Lee Soo-hyun's fund. His wife had passed away two years ago.

The living room was clean. A faded map hung on one wall. Two cups of tea, still steaming, sat on the table. He had brewed them in advance, knowing Soo-hyun would come. This fact pressed slowly against something in Soo-hyun's chest.

"Sit down."

Soo-hyun perched on the edge of the sofa. He opened the envelope.

"Sir, I've come to bring you this month's management report. Based on March—"

"How much was lost?"

There was no edge to Oh Myeong-cheol's voice. It simply wanted to know.

"12.4 percent, based on portfolio valuation."

"In one month."

"Yes."

Soo-hyun didn't set the papers down on the table. Still holding them, he continued his explanation.

March saw two of the harshest market crashes of the year overlap. Signal after signal that global short-term rates would stay higher and longer than expected triggered institutional investors to request redemptions en masse. From the small- and mid-cap growth fund alone that Saemul Asset Management operated, over KRW 37 billion evaporated that week. Scale wasn't the issue. Timing was. To raise redemption funds, the firm had to sell the stocks it held, and it just so happened that the days when it had to sell were the four days when the index bottomed out.

"You sold the good holdings too?"

"Yes."

Soo-hyun paused there.

"We had no choice but to dispose of some positions we'd held for the long term. We needed cash to meet the redemptions, and we sold on days when the market was collapsing. At a loss."

Oh Myeong-cheol took a sip of tea. His eyes looking at Soo-hyun were gentle.

"You must have had a hard time too."

Soo-hyun had not prepared for that remark.

"I'm fine. Your assets are what matter."

"I'm worried about you right now."

Through the window, the Han River was visible. Afternoon sunlight scattered across the water. Soo-hyun finally set the papers down on the table. His fingers lingered on the edge of the documents for a long time.

Four years ago, when they first met, Oh Myeong-cheol had spread out his severance account statement before Soo-hyun and asked: This is what my wife and I saved over thirty years. What would be the best course? Soo-hyun had spent two hours that day explaining the fund. He had talked more about risks than about returns. Oh Myeong-cheol had said he liked that about him. He said that someone who frightens you is the most trustworthy.

"Sir, have you thought about what you'll do going forward?"

"Why does that matter to you?"

"Because I'm still managing it."

"Is that why you came all the way here in person?"

Soo-hyun stopped speaking. The envelope and papers, the subway with two transfers, the hesitation as he pressed the doorbell three times in the hallway. It could have been done over the phone.

"That's not the only reason."

Oh Myeong-cheol made a thoughtful expression.

"I think of this money as my wife's. Half of it is her insurance. So if I get scared about the loss and withdraw it, that somehow feels like an even greater mistake. I don't know."

Soo-hyun didn't nod. He didn't say it was right.

"I hear your heart."

"Lee Soo-hyun, how old are you?"

"Thirty-five."

"Then you're about my daughter's age. How many people do you think would come to see me in person like this?"

"That's…"

"None. Probably none. You could have just called, but you came all the way here. With the envelope."

Soo-hyun couldn't find an answer. The teacup was growing cold.

"I'm not sure either."

Oh Myeong-cheol laughed softly.

"That's the most honest thing you've said."

The two sat in silence for a while. Wind sounds seemed to come from the direction of the Han River. Somewhere in the map's borders would be the mountains and rivers that Oh Myeong-cheol had once pointed to and shown him.

Just as Soo-hyun was about to stand, Oh Myeong-cheol spoke.

"I'll keep the fund invested. As long as you're managing it."

Soo-hyun spent a long time wondering whether that was a condition, a vote of confidence, or something else entirely. He wondered as he pressed the elevator button, as he descended to the first floor and breathed the outside air. He had left the envelope on the table. So that Oh Myeong-cheol could read it alone, at his own pace.

The Han River was still catching the light.
