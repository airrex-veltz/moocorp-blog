The sound of opening the safe was the same even after forty years. That dull friction of metal scraping against metal—when it came, my fingertips would tingle first. Today was no exception. The only difference was that today would be the last.

My name is Seo Sang-hyun. I am a dealer in physical precious metals in an alley behind Jongno 3-ga. From the age of twenty-three when I first entered this alley until now, at sixty-three, I have lived believing in only what I could hold in my hands. I trusted the weight on a scale over numbers in a bank account, the cool sensation lingering on my palm over a stamp on a contract. And at the center of that faith, she was always there.

I call her Geum So. It is not her real name. She was the kind of being that had no real name to speak of. She was better suited to being called by a chemical symbol, but I gave her that name from the day we first met. On an autumn evening when yellow sunlight shone in from the mouth of the alley, while polishing a piece of 24K gold bar in the warehouse behind the wholesale dealer, I first gave her that name. Geum So was a woman who did not rush. Even when the price ticker swung wildly, even when wars erupted and exchange rates flipped and banks collapsed, she never changed her expression. "I do not change. Only the world does," she would often say.

And it was true. In the spring of 2020, when a pandemic halved the global stock markets, only her value rose. That August, when news came over the radio that gold had climbed to 2,075 dollars an ounce—a historic high—I heard it in a corner of the warehouse and laughed. The more the world fell apart, the more people sought her out. She was where people reached when anxiety took hold.

But like all love, boring moments far outnumbered the glorious ones. I would touch her, weigh her, test her purity, and stamp her receipt. I never grew tired of that repetition. Rather, that monotony gave me comfort. She never lied. The numbers the scale pointed to were always honest, and that honesty was why I had clung to this work for forty years.

But several years ago, the air in the alley began to change. Young people would enter the warehouse and stare only at their smartphone screens, never even glancing at the scale. "You don't need to see the actual thing, right? I'll buy it as a token." They wanted to own her without touching her. On blockchain ledgers, her existence was being inscribed in code, and trading those fragments had become fashionable. Around that time, we heard that some association in London had begun attaching digital identification tags to gold bars and registering them on a ledger. The physical metal lay somewhere deep in a warehouse, while people simply exchanged numbers on screens.

I was not displeased so much as disappointed. I could not fathom owning her without touching her. I believed her value came from that sensation at my fingertips—cold, heavy, perfectly smooth without a single blemish. Could you say you knew her by skipping that?

Last winter, Geum So showed me an anxious expression for the first time. "Sang-hyun, I think I have to live with two faces now," she said, sitting on a warehouse shelf, kicking her feet lightly. "People still want me, but now they can have me without touching me. I do not even know if I am really me, or if the number on the screen is."

I did not know how to answer. It was the hardest moment in forty years of conversation with her. "You are still yourself. What has changed is the way people meet you," I said, offering what comfort I could, though I myself was uncertain how much I believed those words.

And today, I stood on the eve of my final transaction in this warehouse. The building owner had signed the redevelopment contract, and next month, demolition cranes would move into this alley. Other dealers my age had closed shop one by one or surrendered their space to online platforms. This way of weighing commodities on a scale and passing them hand to hand had become quaint, like an antique.

My last customer was a young woman. Perhaps in her early thirties. Her business card read "Digital Assets Manager"—an unfamiliar title. As soon as she entered the warehouse, she looked around with curious eyes. "You really weigh it on a scale and sell it like this? I have never seen anything like it." I answered by placing a gold bar on the scale. The needle swung and came to rest. Exactly one kilogram. A number without error.

"Actually, I am going to buy this and issue it again as a token," she said. "Keep the physical in a safe, register the ownership on the blockchain. That way people do not have to come all the way to a warehouse like this." There was no malice in her tone. Rather, it was matter-of-fact. To her, this was simply a matter of efficiency.

Before I placed the gold bar in her hands, I held it for a moment. Under the warehouse's old fluorescent light, its surface glowed softly. I wondered what Geum So thought in this moment. She did not answer. She merely proved her existence through her weight, her coolness.

"May I ask a question?" I said. "Even if you create tokens, in the end this physical thing has to sit in some safe somewhere, does it not? So then, is this not what I have been holding for forty years truly real?"

She thought for a moment and then smiled. "That is right. What we are selling is ultimately a promise about this weight. The weight itself has to be here still. I just do this work—delivering that promise faster, to more people."

Those words warmed my heart in an unexpected way. Geum So was not disappearing. She was simply gaining more ways to be encountered. The sensation that once passed hand to hand could now be conveyed as a single number on a screen. I had seen this as betrayal, but perhaps it was merely expansion.

After placing my final stamp on the contract, I handed the case containing Geum So to the woman. I stood for a long time watching her back as she left the warehouse. The scale remained where it always had. Never again would I place her on that scale.

As night fell and before I locked the warehouse door, I ran my eyes one last time across the empty shelves. Forty years of time spent with her in this place had accumulated like dust. I felt no bitterness. Rather, there was something liberating about it. That is how love works—the more you try to hold on, the more it slips through your fingers. With or without me, in this warehouse or elsewhere, she would continue to exist through her weight. As a number on a screen, or as a bar in a safe.

I did not look back as I left the alley. I only spent a long time touching the cool sensation still lingering on my palm. If someday I meet her again, perhaps it will be without a scale or a warehouse, just two faces meeting through a number on a screen. Would I still recognize her then? I could not be certain yet.
