A cursor blinked in the search box.

Park Jun-hyeok paused, staring at the laptop screen. A single cursor on a white background disappeared and reappeared once per second. It was an empty search box. Even without a search query, that rectangular box always seemed to wear an expression of waiting for someone.

When he first designed it in 2003, it was the same way. The cursor just waits, someone on his team had said back then. We can't know in advance what people want. So it just blinks and waits.

Jun-hyeok had been remembering those words for twenty years.

---

It was the third floor of a shared office somewhere in Pangyo. Through transparent glass walls, twenty-five-year-olds frantically scribbled something on a whiteboard. Today was the first day of a mentoring session. He had been invited to this place for one simple reason: he had worked as a senior developer at a **massive search service**. When the startup accelerator program manager sent an introduction message, Jun-hyeok couldn't reply for a long time.

*Don't you think a former search service development lead could share insights with MZ entrepreneurs?*

Insights. He reread that word several times. He couldn't decide whether what he had was insight, or just age.

---

The first team that came in had three members. Lee Eun-chae, twenty-six. Her voice was low and her eyes were sharp. Eighteen months into founding, she was running an AI-powered personalized meal recommendation service. The second team was Cho Min-jun and two of his friends, a commerce team that had pivoted three times.

"Our retention is dropping. There seems to be a structural problem, but I'm not sure exactly what it is."

Lee Eun-chae spoke. Her intonation was closer to a question than a statement. Jun-hyeok closed his laptop.

"Do you know what users search for the second time?"

"Sorry?"

"Not the first search, but the second one. After they see the results, what do people search for next? That tells you what they actually wanted."

Lee Eun-chae's lips moved and then stopped. Jun-hyeok continued.

"That was one of our mistakes in the early days of search. We obsessed over the first query. But people don't express what they want accurately from the start. On the second, third search—that's where the real intent shows up."

Lee Eun-chae quickly wrote something down. That sight reminded him of someone from long ago. Jun-hyeok quickly moved past that feeling.

---

Lunchtime came. There was a sandwich shop on the floor below the shared office. Lee Eun-chae was standing alone in line.

"Did you come by yourself?" Jun-hyeok asked. She turned and smiled slightly.

"My team members have a meeting. I was actually planning to look at some data while eating."

"Have you ever tried what I mentioned earlier?"

"Analyzing second queries? Not yet. I'd never thought about it that way."

They ended up sitting at the same table. It was natural. There was only one line, and only one empty seat.

Lee Eun-chae opened her laptop. The screen was full of numbers. Cohort charts, drop-off funnels, session-by-session churn rates. Jun-hyeok lost his words for a moment looking at that screen.

The structure was identical to what he saw in 2007. Only the color of the numbers was different, the interface had changed, but ultimately it was a chart tracking the point where people wanted something and then left.

"Here," he said, pointing to one spot on the screen. "At this point. The churn jumps sharply after the third session."

Lee Eun-chae tilted the screen. Both their eyes landed on the same spot at the same time. They were close. Jun-hyeok tried not to be aware of it, but that was exactly why he was aware of it.

"Why the third session?" she asked. The question was pure. It wasn't the gaze of someone expecting a particular answer. It was just the eyes of someone who really didn't know.

"After you update your expectations once," Jun-hyeok said. "The first time, people use it because it's novel. The second time, they use it for real. On the third time, they're asking themselves, 'Is this right for me?' If the service doesn't answer that question, they leave."

Lee Eun-chae stopped writing and looked at him.

"Is that from experience?"

"From failure."

She laughed. Jun-hyeok laughed too. An oddly natural silence followed.

---

In the afternoon session, Cho Min-jun's team gave a presentation. They confidently brought up the story of pivoting three times. His face was bright as he explained failure. Jun-hyeok found that unfamiliar. In his generation, failure was something you hid. Or at the very least, it was rare for someone to talk about it this comfortably.

"What did you learn from pivoting three times?" Jun-hyeok asked.

"We should have asked why they wanted something, not what they wanted, first."

Jun-hyeok paused. That sentence stuck somewhere.

Why they wanted it, not what they wanted.

He had designed search boxes for over a decade. He had analyzed tens of billions of what people searched for. But he had never once properly asked why they searched for it. There were queries, but there was no person behind them.

After the session ended, participants left one by one. Lee Eun-chae was the last to leave, writing something in her notes before standing up.

"I learned a lot today. Thank you."

Jun-hyeok nodded.

"So did I."

She hesitated. As if she hadn't expected that answer.

"Does the mentor learn too?"

"Much more."

Lee Eun-chae laughed again. This time, a little longer. She turned back once more before leaving.

"Come again next week. By then I'll have analyzed those second queries."

The door closed. Jun-hyeok was alone in the empty lecture room. Partially erased numbers remained on the whiteboard.

---

On the subway ride home, he gazed out the window for a long time. Tunnels stretched endlessly, and occasionally the lights of stations flickered past.

In 2003, when he first designed search infrastructure, there was someone who was with him. That person, like Lee Eun-chae, asked questions quickly. The kind of person who asked why people wanted something, not what they wanted. And while Jun-hyeok was fixated on results, that person quietly left the team one day. For a while afterward, he didn't know what he had missed. It took a long time before he dimly realized it had been something in the process, not the results.

The subway crossed the Han River bridge. The night river stretched out long before disappearing back into the tunnel.

He took out his phone. He didn't search for any name. He just stared at the blinking cursor in the search box for a moment, then turned his gaze back to the window.

The cursor was wearing an expression of waiting.

Because it still didn't know what people wanted.
