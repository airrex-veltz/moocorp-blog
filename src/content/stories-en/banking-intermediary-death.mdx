For a while, he believed that his existence was necessary.

**RBKM** — Riverbend Community Bank, the name compressed into a four-letter ticker that still lived somewhere in the stock market, offered him small comfort. Sixty-two years old. Twenty-two branches across five counties in southern Ohio. Farm equipment loans, mortgages, small business operating capital. He was the memory of that region. The father who bought his first tractor on corn fields as collateral, the paperwork from the day his son sat at the same counter to open a restaurant. He remembered all of it.

But then, one day, the paperwork stopped coming.

At first it was a small signal. In spring 2021, applications for small business loans in the region fell 18 percent year-over-year. Director Carolyn called it "pandemic aftermath." The following year, they fell another 11 percent. This time the excuse was different. "The interest rate environment." In 2023, another 9 percent. It was then that he first sensed something beyond the numbers.

It wasn't the pandemic that had disappeared. What had disappeared was the reason it had to go through him.

A platform called Bluestone Lending entered the region in mid-2022. One app. Twelve questions. Approval or rejection within 72 hours. That was all. FICO score, cash flow algorithm, social data layer, real-time account details viewed through the open banking API. They accomplished in four days what RBKM took six weeks to review. The interest rate was 0.5 percentage points lower. There was no need to visit a branch or meet a tie-wearing manager.

At first, he was indignant. He said they didn't understand relationships. They didn't understand people. How could an algorithm know the context behind the numbers—say, the temporary collapse of credit for a farmer who endured six months during the last flood?

But his indignation didn't last long.

One afternoon, Carolyn quietly came to him and handed him a report. Net Interest Margin (NIM) trends. That number falling from 3.41 percent in 2019 to 2.67 percent in 2024 felt like a blood pressure reading to him. Loan revenue was declining, deposit rate competition was intensifying, and the margins he had earned in the middle were being eroded little by little. He had stood between depositors and borrowers, taking fees from both sides. That was his reason for existence. Evaluating risk, transforming liquidity, pricing uncertainty.

But now that role was being replaced directly by meetings on platforms. On the platform, savers and borrowers found each other. Where the intermediary disappeared, he still stood.

On a Tuesday, Mike Darlington visited the branch. A customer with 22 years of business with RBKM. As the owner of a furniture factory, he wanted $200,000 in operating capital. When Paul, his account manager, prepared the paperwork and mentioned a two-week timeline, Darlington paused.

"Actually," he said. "I already got it from somewhere else. Four days ago."

Paul nodded and smiled, but RBKM knew what that smile was hiding. Darlington had come to apologize. It was courtesy for 22 years of relationship. But that courtesy itself was already a goodbye.

That afternoon, the board quietly passed one resolution. Consolidation of three branches. Early retirement recommended for twelve employees. As RBKM read that paperwork, he felt a strange sensation. It wasn't sadness. Sadness comes when something seems unjust. This was quieter and broader. Like watching a river finding a new channel. The water still flows, but not through him.

He began to question what he had done wrong. Digitalization was late. True. He hadn't invested in data infrastructure. True. But going deeper, it wasn't simply a failure. His very mode of existence had been designed on the premise of specific friction costs. When borrowers didn't have information, when savers couldn't find suitable borrowers, when somewhere in between, a trusted institution was needed. He had created value within that friction. But the friction had disappeared.

The algorithm beat him in how it evaluated credit. The relationship banking he had prided himself on, the meetings with managers, the sensitivity to local circumstances—these still had meaning. But the number of borrowers for whom this remained meaningful had shrunk as a share of the total market. Complex cases the algorithm couldn't handle. Immigrants with no credit history. Small farmers whose scores had been distorted by a single crisis. They still needed RBKM.

The other 80 percent did not.

Carolyn came to him again in the fall. This time it wasn't a report. She brought an old file. Records of loans extended during the 1984 drought. There was a memo signed by the bank president at the time. "If this region survives, we survive." Reading that sentence, RBKM felt something catch in his chest.

It wasn't a profit formula. It was a reason.

That reason hadn't completely disappeared even now. But the economics that supported it had changed. Before, the reason itself had been profit. Now he had to create profits in a different way to preserve that reason.

He took out the branch repositioning plan again. The proposal to convert one of the three consolidating branches into a small business finance center. Focusing on complex cases the algorithm couldn't handle. Not competing on standard loans with low margins. Acknowledging that meant a new shape could begin.

Acknowledgment. That was the hardest thing.

RBKM looked out the window. The hardware store that used to be across the state highway had disappeared, replaced by a logistics warehouse. Next to it, an electric vehicle charging station had appeared. The world had always changed this way. Not all at once, but slowly aging until one morning you found yourself already part of the changed landscape.

He was still in that landscape. But he was standing in a different place than before.

Some rivers change course but still reach the sea. Some rivers seep into the earth. Which it would be was not yet decided. The water still flowed.
