## The Promise of the Red Envelope

In the fall of 1997, Reed Hastings sighed as he held the late fee notice in his hand. Forty dollars. The price of returning the DVD of *Apollo 13* six weeks late. On the way home, he passed by a fitness center. 'If there were a monthly membership fee where no matter how much you use it there's no extra charge…'

That night, Reed called his longtime friend Marc Randolph.

"Marc, what if we made DVD rentals a subscription?"

"By mail? That sounds crazy."

"Let's try it. We can just mail a CD and check if it breaks."

The next day, the two men stood at a small post office in Santa Cruz. Inside a red envelope was a Patty Griffin CD. They wrote Marc's home address and affixed a stamp. As the envelope dropped into the mailbox, Reed felt a strange thrill.

Two days later, Marc called.

"Reed, it arrived in perfect condition. Not a single scratch."

And so Streamhouse was born.

---

At first, no one paid attention. People still went to Blockbuster stores. But Reed didn't give up. He began placing small messages inside the red envelopes.

"Did you love the movie you chose? We recommend this film next."

Customers began sending replies. Some sent thank-you letters, others sent lists of films they wanted to see next. Streamhouse wasn't just a rental service—it was a conversation with people who loved movies.

In 2000, Streamhouse received an acquisition offer from Blockbuster. $50 million. For Streamhouse at the time, it was an enormous amount. The board urged him to sell. But Reed looked out the window of the conference room and said:

"We are not simply a company that delivers DVDs. We are a bridge that helps people find content they love."

He declined.

---

In 2007, Reed faced a new challenge: streaming. The DVD business was still profitable. Many advised against it.

"Why take the risk when things are going well?"

But Reed remembered that day ten years ago, dropping the red envelope into the mailbox. What people really wanted wasn't movies—it was the feeling they got from movies. What if they could deliver that feeling faster and easier?

The streaming service launched. Initially, it was unstable and the content library was sparse. But Reed didn't stop. Every morning, he read user feedback. When someone said "loading is slow," he stayed up late with the engineering team. When someone said "I want to see season two of this drama," he contacted the content team.

In 2013, Streamhouse released its original drama *House of Cards*. Traditional broadcasters laughed. "An internet company making dramas?"

But the first weekend, 9 million people watched. Reed stayed alone in his office, gazing out the window. Twenty years ago, the moment he received that $40 late fee notice. What he felt then wasn't simple anger. It was a belief that there had to be a better way.

---

In 2020, a pandemic stopped the world. People were confined to their homes, and theaters closed. But Streamhouse was there with 200 million subscribers. People found comfort watching dramas, movies, and documentaries amid their loneliness.

One morning, Reed read a message from one subscriber.

"I lost my grandmother to COVID. In her last month, she watched *The Classroom of the Queen* on Streamhouse every day. Thank you for giving a small joy during difficult times."

He wiped his eyes. A promise that began with a red envelope. A promise to bring stories that people love. That promise was now being kept on 200 million screens around the world every single day.

Leaving the office, Reed remembered that day in 1997. The sound of the red envelope dropping into the mailbox. The thrill he felt then was still there. Today, someone would turn on Streamhouse and meet a new story.

That was enough.
