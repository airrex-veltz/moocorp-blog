Sae-ah saw her father's face for the first time at thirty, and only in a photograph.

On the first page of the documents that Lawyer Hwang handed her. A face printed above a copy of a driver's license. A man with the same nose bridge as Sae-ah. Byeon Do-wan, born 1958, Chairman of Cheongseong Group.

Her mother had never spoken that name once. She had only said he was someone far away, and Sae-ah only recalled those words at her mother's grave. That was three months ago.

Lawyer Hwang sat awkwardly in one corner of the translation office. The afternoon sunlight streamed through the dusty windows, and Sae-ah's desk was piled with unfinished manuscripts.

"During the divorce settlement proceedings, the family register was organized. Your birth has been officially confirmed."

Lawyer Hwang spoke slowly, choosing his words. He said that the Chairman wanted to discuss the legal rights to 7.3 percent of Cheongseong Group's shares.

Sae-ah scanned the documents once more. 7.3 percent. The number was clear. Its meaning was not yet.

---

The forty-second floor of Cheongseong Tower was a height Sae-ah had never reached before.

When the elevator doors opened, the woman standing in the lobby was Choi Sun-mi. His ex-wife. A face that already knew of Sae-ah's existence. Her expression held no emotion. The absence itself was emotion.

When Sae-ah entered the reception room, Chairman Byeon stood by the window. She saw his back first. His shoulders were narrower than in the photograph. It took a moment longer for him to turn around. During that time, Sae-ah did not catch her breath once.

"Sit down."

That was his first word. No form of address. Sae-ah sat.

Choi spoke first, placing documents on the table. "Acknowledging the shares and involvement in management are separate matters. There's no reason Jae-hun and Jae-won would agree."

Korean corporate law requires the support of more than two-thirds of issued shares at a special general shareholders' meeting. The fact that Sae-ah held 7.3 percent was not enough to overturn anything on her own. But Lawyer Hwang had given her a hint beforehand. If Choi's friendly shareholders were combined with Sae-ah's shares, they could approach the threshold needed to block the subsidiary separation that management was pushing through.

Sae-ah erased that calculation from her mind. She did not yet know if she wanted that fight.

---

The night before the board meeting, a text arrived on Sae-ah's phone.

It was from Byeon Jae-hun. Her older brother. A word she had never spoken aloud.

"Don't come tomorrow. Father is just weakening. That's all."

Sae-ah stared at the text for a long time. What lay within it was easy to read. It was not a threat. Not a warning. It was fear about something that might have to be shared for the first time.

Sae-ah did not reply.

---

On the morning of the board meeting, Sae-ah arrived at the tower thirty minutes early.

It was an observer's seat with no voting rights. Lawyer Hwang had arranged the attendance. The subsidiary separation proposal was to be tabled, and the executives' expressions already revealed their positions.

Jae-hun stood at the podium and made his presentation. His voice was steady. The slides were meticulous. Watching him, Sae-ah knew that this man had spent a long time preparing. At least that preparation was sincere.

Just before the vote, Chairman Byeon looked at Sae-ah for the first time.

It was brief. Two seconds? Sae-ah tried to read something in that gaze. Was it apology, confirmation, or nothing at all?

He raised his hand. In favor.

The proposal passed. Jae-hun nodded briefly. Choi was not in the room.

---

As the executives filed out after the meeting, Sae-ah did not leave her seat.

The Han River was visible through the window. Autumn sunlight spread across the water. It was an afternoon when nothing was decided.

The shares were not yet in her name. She could have sued. She could have not sued. She did not know which side of the word "family" to place herself on.

Before leaving, Chairman Byeon passed by Sae-ah's side. He did not stop. But his pace slowed for a beat. Whether that was enough, whether it was not enough, Sae-ah still did not know.

The river continued to flow below.
