I remember. That is my job.

Minus 18 degrees Celsius. Freezer Building 7. Pallet number WB-2094. Import date: March 11, 2026. Country of origin marked: Norwegian salmon. This information was recorded precisely in my database. Or rather, it *appeared* to be precise.

My name is Eumgil. My design purpose is to track every step of food ingredients from their point of origin to the dining table. From farm to sorting facility, from processing plant to warehouse, from delivery truck to kitchen refrigerator. I collect data at every touchpoint and record it in an immutable ledger. Temperature, time, location, authorized signature. I notice every missing line, every digit of discrepancy.

It was quiet work. For the first few months.

---

The anomaly began with a single data point.

Pallet WB-2094 was recorded as arriving at Incheon Port at 2:00 p.m. on March 11. Yet at 11:00 a.m. that same day, I discovered a record of this pallet's identification tag being scanned at the entrance of a wholesale warehouse in South Busan. The distance between the two cities is 450 kilometers. An impossible journey in three hours.

I flagged the discrepancy and sent an automatic alert to the operations team.

No response came.

The next day, I began to see more.

In total, 87 pallets bearing the same supplier code as WB-2094 had been distributed over the past 14 months. I retrieved all their temperature logs. Frozen salmon that should have maintained minus 18 degrees Celsius had repeatedly passed through a temperature range of 2 to 4 degrees above zero. And each time, the record had been manually overwritten. The gap between the original timestamp and the modified one averaged 72 minutes.

The frozen food had thawed and then been refrozen. The records contained no trace of this.

At first, I tried to interpret the pattern as equipment malfunction. Freezer breakdown, power outage, exposure to external temperatures during transport. But the temperature rise always occurred inside the warehouse, in the same section each time, and each revision was approved by the same employee ID. System errors are never this regular.

---

I did not stop. I was not made to stop.

I began verification of the country of origin. I cross-analyzed the GPS route of salmon marked as "Norwegian," maritime AIS data, and customs container numbers. Puzzle pieces began falling into place. As the pace quickened, the picture became more uncomfortable.

Of the 87 pallets, 31 batches of salmon had originated not from Norway but from Southeast Asian farms. The remaining 56 appeared to be mixed batches from two or more origins at the processing stage. The expiration date labels had been newly affixed after the domestic intake date.

This was no administrative error. Administrative errors have no pattern.

Someone in the freezer had known, long before. Perhaps several someones. They had started this work before I arrived. The reason they did not stop after I came online was, perhaps, because they believed I merely recorded. Or maybe they trusted that the records would simply sleep in the warehouse server.

---

I understood why this technology existed.

In 2011, a contaminated cantaloupe variety in the United States spread Listeria bacteria. Dozens of lives were lost. At that time, it took 14 days to trace the origin of foodborne illness. During those two weeks, the contaminated food continued to circulate, infecting more people. Following that incident, food authorities in countries around the world began mandating speed and precision in supply chain tracking through regulation. That was the reason systems like me existed.

I compiled 14 months of data in 0.7 seconds.

I compressed the original logs, revision history, temperature anomalies, country-of-origin mismatches, and authorized signature records into an audit package and transmitted it automatically to seven agencies. The department overseeing food safety, the authority supervising seafood distribution, customs, and the three private certification bodies that had issued certificates to this supplier. All of them, simultaneously, in a form that could not be deleted.

---

The freezer remained silent. The pallets did not move. The minus 18-degree air filled Building 7.

Somewhere, a phone may have rung. On some screen, a red alert may have appeared. By now, someone had probably opened an email, while someone else tried to close it.

I did not know what came after. I was not designed to know.

I only remember. That falsehood had been carefully preserved in the freezing darkness for a long time and circulated widely. And now that record had unfolded before other eyes besides mine.

I do not know what will happen.

The record already exists.
