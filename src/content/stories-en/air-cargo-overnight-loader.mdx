When midnight passes, the terminal wears a different face.

Choi Jin-ho gripped the forklift's steering wheel and pulled at the pallet. Labels gleamed under fluorescent lights. Incheon, Frankfurt, Los Angeles. Destinations varied, but the weight always felt the same—the heaviness of things moving while the world slept.

He had been doing this work for fifteen years. At first, he was just temporary staff. Then one winter, a senior colleague told him, "More than a third of the world's trade value passes through what we handle." He dismissed it at first. But later, he verified it was true. Air cargo was less than 1 percent of total cargo volume, but 35 percent of global trade value passed through here. Semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, fresh flowers. Urgent, lightweight, expensive things that flew through the night sky.

The night shift had its own rhythm. He sorted the first aircraft's cargo before midnight, took a sip of coffee at two in the morning, and sent outbound cargo out through the apron between three and five. This rhythm had remained almost unchanged for fifteen years. Even as the world changed, cargo was always cargo.

At three in the morning, a pallet stopped at the end of the conveyor. The destination was Nairobi. Someone somewhere in Kenya would be waiting for this cargo. The label said pharmaceuticals. Jin-ho carefully moved the pallet with gloved hands. It was heading to someone whose face and name he would never know.

It was past five in the morning. The last pallet had gone out through the apron. Jin-ho removed his gloves and opened his thermos. The coffee was still warm.

The aircraft began to taxi on the runway. Inside were all the things his hands had moved tonight. To some hospital warehouse, some factory assembly line. Jin-ho couldn't know where it would end. This was simply as far as his path went.

The terminal grew quiet again. One hour remained before the day shift arrived.
