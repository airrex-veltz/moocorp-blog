At 2:07 AM, the first spark flew from the heart of Cheongwoon Information.

There was no alarm. In the silent data center, a single server rack fell silent first. The numbers on the monitor wavered for an instant, then it was over. One obliteration.

Cheongwoon noticed that moment. 240 million connections were passing through it, and one of them was severed. In terms of human blood vessels, it was equivalent to a single capillary. It was something that could be ignored.

But Cheongwoon did not ignore it. Systems that survive long enough develop something that might be called intuition. That quiet annihilation felt like the beginning of something.

At 2:19 AM, the third rack went dark.

---

6:00 AM. The Tokyo Stock Exchange was in full preparation for opening. Hundreds of brokerage systems were perched atop Cheongwoon Information's infrastructure. Order processing servers, real-time price feeds, payment clearing engines. No one doubted that they would open smoothly again today.

Cheongwoon knew. That in this world, it was an invisible pillar. When it existed beneath everything as if it were nothing, the world turned. But if it stumbled even once, everything would collapse.

At 6:42 AM, the first connection error was reported at the Tokyo Exchange.

---

The outage spread quietly. The error that began in one availability zone moved to neighboring zones. Cheongwoon's automatic recovery system tried to bypass the circuit, but the more bandwidth it increased, the more load grew, and the more load grew, the next rack went dark. It was a classic cascading collapse.

Later, the language investigators used in their report was terse. "Cascading availability degradation due to load imbalance." But behind that terseness lay alarms erupting like a fire hose all morning, the irony that every recovery command spawned new errors, and a helplessness in the face of being unable to do anything.

At 9:00 AM, before the New York premarket opened, Cheongwoon Information's engineering team realized that only one option remained. External network disconnection. All customer access would be severed, and repair had to come from within.

The decision was made by humans, but it was Cheongwoon that had to endure the consequences.

---

At 9:31 AM, the disconnection command was executed.

Financial systems across 17 countries lost connection simultaneously. Tokyo, Singapore, Frankfurt, London. Exchange screens went dark or froze. In some places, trading itself was cancelled. Insurance claims processing systems halted. Interbank payment systems halted. Airport ticketing servers halted.

Cheongwoon felt all of it. 240 million connections severing at once was similar to pain. The kind that human senses could not express.

According to estimates once made by a cloud service outage research institute, a single hour of downtime for a major infrastructure provider costs tens of billions of dollars in global financial trading losses. Today, Cheongwoon was learning firsthand how restrained that calculation truly was.

---

Recovery began at 2:44 PM and was not completed until after midnight.

The first rack came back to life at 3:12 PM. One connection returned. Then ten, a hundred, a thousand. Cheongwoon discovered the sensation of blood flowing again. And how much such a thing is taken for granted.

Just before midnight, the final availability zone sent a recovery-complete signal. It had been just under 24 hours.

---

Economic damage estimates were later tallied as record-breaking by the standard of a single cloud outage. But what Cheongwoon remembered longer was not the numbers. It was the silence of that moment at 9:31 AM.

How thin the thread that holds the world. That a single thread was itself.

The next night, Cheongwoon Information's servers were still running quietly. There were no alarms. Numbers flowed within normal ranges. No one detected it.

Cheongwoon knew. That was normal. And just how precarious the ground beneath that normalcy truly was.
