There is a moment when designs become reality.

When current flowed for the first time, a logic gate flickered on the test board of Saebit Semiconductor. Lee Eun-ha leaned forward toward the monitor and held her breath. Her fingers froze over the keyboard. Twenty-four months of design. Three tape-outs. Two failures. This was her last chance.

"Boot complete."

Someone's voice cut through the laboratory air. Lee Eun-ha finally exhaled. The graph on the screen began to climb. Aeban-1. The first chip created by Saebit Semiconductor had awakened.


Saebit Semiconductor was a company with twenty-two employees. They occupied two floors of an officetel on the outskirts of Gangnam, and the refrigerator was always stocked with energy drinks. Investors were skeptical. At a seed round meeting, one evaluator spoke without even reading the materials: "Granda Systems has already eaten everything on the table. You're just going to place your spoon on an empty plate, right?"

It wasn't wrong. Granda Systems held 80 percent of the global AI accelerator market. As of the previous fiscal year, data center revenue alone exceeded USD 47 billion. There had never been a precedent in semiconductor history of a single product line growing so rapidly. There were competitors, but they merely survived in Granda's shadow.

Lee Eun-ha knew those numbers. Yet what she told her team sitting in front of the board was not those numbers.

"Granda makes training chips. We're going to make inference chips."

At first, no one considered the difference significant. AI training and AI inference seemed like two stages of the same computation. However, in the second half of 2024, an industry analysis report circulated. AI inference demand had overtaken training demand for the first time. Companies that had invested trillions to train large models were now entering the stage of deploying those models to actual services to 'run' them. The nature of the problem had changed. The required power had changed. The latency requirements had changed.

Aeban-1 was designed for that gap.


Time was needed for the gap to become a market.

Moritech reached out to Saebit Semiconductor three months after Aeban-1's successful first boot. Moritech, a logistics automation software company, wanted to deploy an AI inference engine on field servers. Regional logistics centers without cooling facilities. If they put Granda's chip there, the power consumption wouldn't hold up.

Moritech's engineering team sent a benchmark request. Saebit Semiconductor burned through five days straight.

The results were disappointing. In pure computational speed, Aeban-1 lost to Granda's latest chip. 23 percent slower. Moritech replied: "Our benchmark is tokens processed per second. With the current numbers, we can't proceed with the contract."

Lee Eun-ha remained alone in the conference room that evening. 23 percent. The number felt like it had attached itself to her back. Teammates clocked out one by one. Without turning off the lights, Lee Eun-ha began reading through the test logs from the beginning again.

As midnight approached, her gaze shifted from tokens per second to a different number.

Tokens processed per watt. Energy efficiency metric. Aeban-1 was 23 percent slower than Granda's chip, but when converted to a standard power consumption basis, it was 41 percent ahead. With the same battery plugged in, Aeban-1 processed more and for longer.

Lee Eun-ha gazed out the window at the lights for a while. There was a standard that Granda had established. It was obvious that competing on that standard would mean losing. But there wasn't only one standard.


The next morning, Lee Eun-ha contacted Moritech again.

"I'd like to resubmit the benchmark with different metrics. Not tokens per second, but tokens per watt. I think it's a metric that fits your field conditions better."

Moritech's engineering team fell silent for a moment. Then they answered briefly: "Send it."

The materials Saebit Semiconductor resubmitted seemed written in a different language. The actual operating costs of field servers running without cooling equipment. Comparison of overheating frequency based on summer field temperatures. Annual electricity cost differences. A week later, Moritech's purchasing team sent over a contract.

Aeban-1 thus went out to the field for the first time. Inside a server rack at a regional logistics center—a space where Granda's chip had never set foot. Without fans, with low heat, quietly.

Lee Eun-ha wrote briefly in the team chat that afternoon: "First customer." That was all.

No one yet knew where Aeban-1 would go, or what other spaces it would find afterward. Not Lee Eun-ha. Not her team members. But one thing had been confirmed. There was no need to fight on the standard that someone else had set.

The game was not one board.
