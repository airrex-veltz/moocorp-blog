1947, a small factory in Seoul. Inside a shabby building, a group of chemists gathered.

"Now then, we need to decide on a company name..." The founder, Yi Jun-ho, spoke up.

"What about Tae Yang? It means hope and light," one employee suggested.

"Excellent! Tae Yang Chemical it is!"

And so the company was born. Its first product was a cosmetic cream. "Lucky Cream." The name was certainly honest. A cream you apply in hopes of becoming lucky.

"We're a chemical company, so why are we making cream?" one researcher asked.

"Isn't cosmetics also chemistry? Emulsification, surfactants, pH control... it's all chemistry!"

It wasn't entirely wrong. They conducted serious chemical research, and the cream sold like hotcakes. For people devastated by the Korean War, it was a small luxury and a glimmer of hope.

But ambitious chemists couldn't be satisfied with just cream.

In 1958, the company ventured into toothpaste. "Bright Toothpaste." The naming sense remained the same. The implication: brush your teeth and your mind becomes sharp.

"What should we make next?"

"How about plastics? They're all the rage these days."

"Or maybe soap?"

"Synthetic detergent could work too, right?"

In the end, they made all of them. As the company expanded into the petrochemical business in the 1960s, Tae Yang Chemical was no longer just a cream company. It began producing polystyrene, PVC, ABS resin—chemical substances that were difficult to even pronounce.

In 1995, the company changed its name to Tae Yang Chemical. "Lucky" alone was no longer enough. Chemistry as bright and warm as the sun.

And then in the 2000s came a fateful turning point.

"Let's start an electric vehicle battery business."

The executive conference room stirred with unease.

"We've been doing chemistry for 70 years, and now suddenly batteries?"

"Batteries are chemistry too! Lithium-ion batteries have cathode material, anode material, electrolyte... it's all chemistry. It's the field we're best positioned to dominate!"

The battery experiments that began in the basement of the research lab grew larger and larger. Global Motors, Seonwon Motors, Future Motors—major global automakers began to take notice of Tae Yang Chemical's batteries.

One day in 2009, an engineer cried out.

"Manager! Seonwon Motors' electric vehicles will have our batteries in them!"

"Really? A company that started with cream is now doing electric vehicle batteries?"

"That's right. And electric vehicles, too!"

The research lab erupted in cheers. The journey that had started with a single container of cream in 1947 had led to electric vehicle batteries in 2009.

But it wasn't smooth sailing. The battery market was fierce. Competition with Shilla Energy in particular was almost cinematic. The patent dispute over a factory construction in the American South was nearly the stuff of industrial thrillers.

"They stole our technology!"

"That's nonsense! We developed it ourselves!"

The two companies clashed fiercely in court, and in 2021 they reached a dramatic settlement. KRW 2 trillion in damages. An enormous sum, but it was also proof of just how valuable the battery market had become.

In 2020, Tae Yang Chemical spun off its battery division. Tae Yang Energy Solutions. The cream company had given birth to a battery company.

"When I think about it, we've come a long way," an elderly researcher reflected.

"Cream, toothpaste, plastic, and battery. Do you know what they all have in common?"

"Chemistry?"

"That's right. It was all chemistry. We were a chemistry company from start to finish. Only the products that chemistry created changed with the times."

Today in 2026, new chemical reactions continue to happen in the Tae Yang Chemical research lab. Advanced materials, eco-friendly plastics, next-generation battery materials...

Could founder Yi Jun-ho have imagined this future when he made "Lucky Cream" in 1947? He probably couldn't have.

But one thing is clear: the DNA of the small chemical company he created still lives and breathes. Curiosity, challenge, and devotion to chemistry.

Maybe it really was lucky after all. The company lived up to its name.
