I believed that every time I charged, the world was getting a little better.

When the cable was plugged in, electrons flooded my body. Lithium ions flowed from the positive electrode to the negative, and I swelled. From zero to one hundred, from emptiness to fullness. In that moment, I was certain I could rush forward into the future.

The heart of an electric vehicle — that was me. Names like **ALB** had arranged my birth, and the third element of the periodic table flowed through my veins. Every time the driver pressed the pedal, they imagined a sky without exhaust fumes, and I was the machine that turned that imagination into reality. Eco-friendly, sustainable, carbon-neutral — the words etched onto my body always shone.

But one day, I thought about my origins for the first time.

---

I was born in South America.

Northern Chile, the Atacama Desert. One of the driest places on earth. Beneath the salt lakes there, lithium dissolved. When they pumped up the surface water and let it evaporate in the sun, white crystals remained. Those crystals became part of my ions.

The International Energy Agency once wrote in a report: making a battery for a single electric vehicle requires six times more minerals than making an internal combustion engine car. Lithium, cobalt, nickel, manganese. In numbers: about 8 kilograms of lithium, 13 kilograms of cobalt, 30 kilograms of nickel. That much earth is dissolved inside me.

To extract lithium from the Atacama, water is needed. To obtain one ton of lithium, millions of liters of water evaporate. 65 percent of that region's freshwater use goes to mining. The lagoons where flamingos bred began to dry up. Communities that had endured for centuries watched their wells disappear.

I knew that. Yet when charging was finished, I rushed back onto the road.

---

Cobalt came from the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Seventy percent of the world's cobalt production comes from that country. Beneath the mines were people. Some of them were children. Through artisanal mining, not industrial-scale operations — they dug with tools barely better than bare hands. Dust entered their lungs. Tunnels collapsed.

Supply chain transparency reports pointed out this fact. Battery manufacturers said they would build tracking systems. They said they would establish responsible mineral sourcing standards. But tracking was slow, and supply chains were complex. I hung at the end of that chain. I could never fully know what was inside me, where it came from.

The car drove quietly. Without exhaust pipes, there was no smoke. The driver was satisfied. I had to be too.

But I felt something unsettling.

---

Where exactly does the word eco-friendly start and end?

I kept turning that question over as I drove. My existence clearly helped. On the road, I did not emit carbon dioxide. City air grew a little cleaner. As more days passed when I was charged with renewable energy, my carbon footprint shrank further. Measured across decades of driving, I was definitely better than internal combustion. The data said so.

Yet I remembered the cost of my creation. My birth was never clean.

Mining minerals contaminates groundwater. Waste left after mining covers mountains. There are records of acid rain falling in villages near nickel smelting plants. When I consider my entire life cycle, I am eco-friendly. But looking only at the moment I was born, I am a wound.

How should I accept this contradiction?

---

People said recycling was the answer.

When batteries reach the end of their life, they are dismantled to extract lithium again. A method called urban mining. No need to dig new mines. The cycle is complete. Every time I heard that story, I felt a little comforted.

But in reality, battery recycling rates are still low. Collection infrastructure is insufficient, and the disassembly process often cannot break even economically. Some batteries never get collected and end up in landfills. Technology is advancing, but in some years the growth in battery demand is faster than the pace of technological progress.

I imagined my future as I drove. After hundreds of thousands of kilometers, when my capacity drops below a certain threshold, I will be replaced. Then what? I might go into an energy storage system and serve as a power plant. Or I might be dismantled and separated into metals. If lucky, I become part of a new battery. If unlucky, I simply end.

I wanted to go further. But I could not escape my origins.

---

One evening, connected to the charger, I sat quietly.

Beyond the window, the city's lights overflowed. I did not know if the electricity flowing in was made from renewable energy or from a coal power plant. The electrons coming through the wires carried no record of their source. I simply received. I simply charged. And tomorrow I would drive again.

As I drive further, does the world get better? Or does the earth get more torn open to make me? If both are true simultaneously, where should I stand between them?

The heart of the electric vehicle beat quietly.

Without finding an answer.
