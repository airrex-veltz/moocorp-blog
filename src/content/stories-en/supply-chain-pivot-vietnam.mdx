The moment the Hanoi-bound plane lifted off the Incheon runway, Lee Jae-hyun leaned quietly back against his seat. Morning fog slid slowly across the West Sea below. He reviewed the numbers displayed on his laptop screen one more time. Guangzhou plant capacity utilization: 68 percent. Delivery delays: 14 percent. And this quarter's tariff-driven cost increases on U.S. imports—roughly USD 22 per finished unit.

That number had been grinding down Daon Tech for three years.

---

Lee Jae-hyun had taken the CEO post at Daon Tech in 2022. Then it was a mid-size electronics manufacturer with its main factory near Guangzhou, China, producing mid-range appliance components on a subcontracting basis. Delivery was fast. Unit costs were competitive. The problem was that every advantage sat on a single geographic condition.

"We can't do it without China," Operations Director Park Soo-yeon used to say.

Slowly, the world showed this might not be true.

After 2023, as tariffs and trade restrictions ping-ponged between the U.S. and China, mid-size manufacturers like Daon—dependent on a single Chinese factory—fell into the same dilemma. Cut risk and costs explode; maintain status quo and watch for the next shock. That period saw electronics parts companies relocating to Southeast Asia jump 30 percent year-over-year. Was it fear-based decision-making, or rational calculation? Lee Jae-hyun still wasn't sure.

---

When Lee Jae-hyun first mentioned supply chain diversification, Park Soo-yeon's brow furrowed.

"CEO, shifting even half of Guangzhou's operation needs over KRW 30 billion in startup capital. Vietnam's power grid is unstable. Skilled workers are short. India's logistics costs double."

"I know," Lee Jae-hyun answered in a low, flat voice. "But if tariffs rise again, what's our margin?"

Park didn't answer. The silence was the answer.

---

The factory site near Binh Duong, Vietnam disappointed on first sight. The local partner Nguyen explaining the area size cheerfully, but Lee Jae-hyun's eyes fixed on the bare land beyond the site boundary.

"Logistics flow is the problem," he said quietly to himself.

Park received him immediately. "Distance to port is farther than expected. Container unloading time plus factory entry time—minimum two days."

"And India?"

Park pulled up a tablet, rattling off numbers. "Two candidate sites near Mumbai. One has unstable power supply. The other's land permit is still pending. Either way, due diligence takes at least six months minimum."

Lee Jae-hyun walked the site slowly. Heat rose through his boots. It was dry season, no rain coming, yet sweat beaded his forehead.

Nguyen, trailing behind, held up his smartphone. The screen showed photos of already-operating nearby factories. "Companies entering this zone exceeded 80 percent capacity utilization within three years," he said in careful, accented Korean. "The beginning is the hardest."

Lee Jae-hyun studied him for a moment. There was a composed calm to Nguyen's face—the kind that comes from accumulated experience. It was strangely comforting.

"This isn't relocation," Lee Jae-hyun muttered walking away from the site. "This is building new."

---

Back in Seoul, Lee Jae-hyun stood before the board and presented three scenarios.

First: maintain the Guangzhou plant, absorb tariff risk. Second: Vietnam-only shift, three-year plan. Third: parallel Vietnam and India investment, five-year roadmap.

"The first is running with a bomb," he said. "The second cuts risk but creates a different kind. The third costs the most—but it's probably the most serious answer."

A board member raised his hand.

"In five years, the company itself transforms. Can you preserve current delivery and quality through that process?"

Lee Jae-hyun paused. Behind the presentation, only the projector's cooling fan hummed.

"I don't know," he answered honestly. "We might keep them. We might lose them. But if we change nothing—I don't know where we'll be in three years either."

A brief silence fell across the room.

---

The conclusion didn't come that day.

Park Soo-yeon knocked on Lee Jae-hyun's office door late that evening. He was alone, watching Seoul's night view from his window.

"CEO, may I ask something?"

"Ask."

"Aren't you afraid of this decision?"

Lee Jae-hyun thought for a moment, then smiled quietly. "Fear is natural. If you're not afraid, you're not thinking."

Park asked nothing more. As she closed the door, she glanced one last time at the gap where light spilled out. Inside, Lee Jae-hyun was already studying numbers again.

---

A supply chain is not merely a line on a map. It is the weight of someone's choice, descending onto some terrain, in some season. And that weight does not disappear after the decision is made.

Binh Duong's surveying teams were already on the site. Mumbai's permit paperwork was under review at legal. The Guangzhou plant ran on at 68 percent capacity today.

Daon Tech's final board resolution was scheduled for two weeks later.
