In February 2004, Jeong Min-seok entered a line of code in his Harvard dormitory room. He didn't know that 22 years later, this code would connect the hearts of two people separated by the Pacific Ocean.

"TheFacebook.com" was initially just a small playground for Harvard students. Finding friends, sharing photos, updating their status. Simple but powerful magic of connection. Within weeks, half of Harvard's students had signed up, and soon it spread to Yale and Stanford.

In the fall of 2006, Facebook opened its doors to the world. Anyone with an email address could join. That year, a college student named Ji-min in Seoul signed up out of curiosity. To study English and make foreign friends.

David in San Francisco also signed up around the same time. He was a software engineer who loved music. He played guitar as a hobby and followed indie band concerts.

The two first connected in 2008. It was in a Coldplay fan group, a shared interest. David commented on a guitar cover video of "Viva la Vida" that Ji-min had posted. "Beautiful playing! Where did you learn?"

The conversation flowed naturally. Talk about music, talk about everyday life, talk about dreams. The messenger window melted the 13-hour time difference. When David was asleep, Ji-min was awake, and when Ji-min ended their day, David was beginning his morning. Their conversation continued for 24 hours.

In 2012, Facebook went public on the NASDAQ. A company valued at 100 billion dollars. A platform used by 1 billion people worldwide. But for Ji-min and David, those numbers didn't matter. What mattered was the message they checked every morning, the photos of each other's smiling faces, the warm conversations they shared over video calls.

In 2014, David made a decision. He took two weeks of vacation and booked a flight to Seoul. To meet in person the person he had only seen through a screen for six years.

At the arrival gate of Incheon Airport, they saw each other face-to-face for the first time. The awkwardness vanished in three seconds. After six years of talking every day, they knew each other well. Ji-min said with a smile, "We finally met, for real."

David nodded. "I should thank Jeong Min-seok. He crossed the ocean for me instead."

Two years later, David moved to Korea. It was possible thanks to remote work. In 2021, when Jeong Min-seok changed the company's name to "Ieum Social" and declared the metaverse era, Ji-min and David were already living together in the real world.

Now in 2026, they live in a small apartment in Seoul. On the wall of the living room hangs the first photo of their meeting taken at Incheon Airport in 2014. Next to that photo, a screenshot of their first social media conversation is framed.

"Beautiful playing! Where did you learn?"

The small website that started in a Harvard dormitory 22 years ago now connects 3 billion people worldwide. But among those 3 billion connections, Ji-min and David believe theirs is the most special.

While David prepares dinner, Ji-min picks up a guitar. The first chord of "Viva la Vida" rings out. The conversation that started with that song 18 years ago has now become a life lived together.

Outside the window, Seoul's sunset is fading. In San Francisco, the morning sun is rising. There's still a 13-hour time difference, but now they live in the same house, in the same time.

Connection erases distance. And sometimes, it creates love. Ieum Social proved that. 3 billion times, and continuing.
