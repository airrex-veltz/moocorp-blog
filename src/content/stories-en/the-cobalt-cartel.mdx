I am cobalt. Element 27. Once I was a pigment that dyed pottery blue, and nowadays I live inside batteries. Every electric vehicle carries tens of kilograms of me. The positive electrode needs me to last long. That's why everyone is looking for me. The problem is where I lie.

Half my body sleeps underground in a land on the far side of the earth, where red soil runs thick. On that land lives the Haneulkol family. Though they call it a family, it's really just a few mines and several dozen brokers, plus one ledger. But the voice of the old man of Haneulkol, who held that ledger, echoed deeper than any mining shaft.

"Blue diminishes, people increase. The price rises without me lifting a finger."

Every morning the old man spoke these words before the scale and drank tea. The tea was always cold. His speeches were long.

One year the old man raised a single finger. He shut the export doors for three months. More precisely, he erected a low threshold called a 'quota'. It resembled something that had truly happened. When a major mining nation restricted cobalt exports, prices had swung within days, and the world's battery factories all rushed to search my name at once. I felt a little smug. To be honest, I confess it. No one had ever called upon me with such desperation before.

Then Saesol arrived. Saesol was a young woman named 'Green Transition'. Her shoelaces hung loose, and she wore an Earth-shaped backpack across her shoulder. Saesol sat before the old man, took a sip of the cold tea, and frowned.

"Sir, without your mine I cannot take a single step."

"I know."

"But if I cannot walk, the blue you dug has nowhere to go. It will only pile up in a warehouse."

The old man made no reply, only touching the scale. I held my breath in the mine shaft. This was the paradox at the heart of this story. A procession crying out to save the earth lined up before this family's ledger, and the longer the line grew, the narrower the door they opened. The old man did not know that as the line lengthened, people would begin looking not at the door but at the walls surrounding it.

Beyond those walls, things were already unfolding. Humble batteries made of iron and phosphorus began to run without me, and my manganese friends shifted their mix to slowly claim my share. More than anything, used-up batteries had begun flowing back from junkyards. From there, blue emerged again. Me—yesterday's version of me.

I found that last scene the most amusing. When I, freed from an exhausted battery, entered a new one, I was recorded in numbers that never appeared in the Haneulkol old man's ledger. Blue that had never been mined and that no one had authorized.

One day Saesol came to find the old man again. This time her shoelaces were tied. The old man poured hot tea into a fresh cup. This time his words seemed brief.

"The price has dropped somewhat."

"Yes. These days blue comes from many places."

The old man gazed long at the scale. On one side lay his blue; on the other, the blue that had returned. The scale, for the first time in a long while, did not tip to one side. It simply trembled.

I am blue, whether underground or inside a battery or on a shelf in a junkyard. Wherever I am, I am myself. Now it seems people have finally understood that. The tea was still warm, and Saesol drank it slowly.
