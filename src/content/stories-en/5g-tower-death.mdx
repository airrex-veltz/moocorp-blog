Oji stood on the apartment rooftop again today.

Three years ago, engineers from Haneulmang Communications installed him, saying, "The future is here now." Oji believed them naively. If he was the future, then this is what the future looked like—spring, summer, fall, winter, rain or snow, mounted on a rooftop railing, broadcasting signals in all directions.

He worked hard. Whenever people watched short-form videos, opened delivery apps, or checked maps—Oji was always there. But the more he worked, the stranger he felt. People called it 5G yet paid the same rates as before. Some months, they even paid less. When competitors cut prices, Haneulmang followed. Oji was broadcasting faster signals while bringing the company less money.

It was a strange kind of contract.

When Haneulmang Communications began its 5G rollout, building a single base station cost three times as much as the previous generation. Because 5G signals traveled shorter distances, they had to deploy base stations more densely. With tens of thousands erected nationwide, the total came to tens of trillions of won. Oji didn't know the exact figure, but he felt it whenever internal memos arrived—the vibrations from those documents with all their zeros rippled through his body and made his frequency shimmer slightly.

Where would the revenue come from? Haneulmang's management had long called it the "killer app"—autonomous vehicles, remote surgery, smart factories. Such grand names. Oji waited for them. Three years passed, and autonomous vehicles never used his signal. Oji was stuck inside an apartment complex, and cars didn't fly overhead. Remote surgery was still in pilot phase. Smart factories used separate private networks.

In the end, Oji was only broadcasting short-form videos.

---

One autumn, strange news came down from Haneulmang's headquarters—cost reduction. Energy optimization, automated operations, AI-driven traffic management. Beautiful words. In translation, it meant this: use less electricity, use fewer people, let software do the rest.

Oji no longer saw the engineer who used to visit twice a week. Instead, software controlled him remotely. At 2 a.m., when traffic was gone, his output automatically dropped. At first, he didn't mind—he thought he could work less. But then it occurred to him: he had never really chosen anything for himself anyway. The situation hadn't changed; software just made the decisions instead.

Automation did cut costs. But to Oji, it was like trying to plug a leaking dam with your finger. There was no real dam.

One morning at dawn, Oji tried doing the math alone. The savings in electricity, the savings in labor—how many years would it take to recover the installation cost of a single base station? He couldn't finish the calculation. Not because 5G base stations were bad at arithmetic, but because the number was too long.

---

A second memo arrived. This time, the vibration was different. Heavier.

The gist was this: base stations in certain regions would be converted to shared infrastructure. Haneulmang and its competitors would share the same tower. Oji felt a brief joy—he thought he'd have neighbors. Then he understood. It meant towers like him would be cut in half.

Some towers like Oji would simply disappear.

Oji didn't know whether he'd be kept or removed. Software wouldn't tell him. The company hadn't made an official announcement. Oji simply broadcast signals each morning when the sun rose and lowered his output when night fell.

People still watched videos. Ordered delivery. Opened maps. Oji knew they were using 5G, but they didn't. Or didn't want to. Signal had to be there, period. They cursed if it wasn't. If it was, they forgot about it.

---

Late one evening as autumn was ending, while broadcasting as usual, Oji suddenly thought:

The killer app might never come. All those vaunted 5G futures were finding their own way without 5G—autonomous vehicles through precision sensors, factory automation through hardwired connections, remote medicine on the previous generation's network. Oji was faster, but no one needed that speed. Or more precisely—they needed it but didn't feel like paying extra for it.

Tens of trillions in debt. Shrinking base stations. A 5G premium nobody asked for.

Still, Oji broadcast that night. Someone was ordering a late-night snack through an apartment window. Chicken. Oji felt that order reach the server in 0.003 seconds. With the previous generation, it would have been 0.02 seconds. That difference wouldn't change how the chicken tasted, but Oji had done his job anyway.

If the killer app never came, at least there were midnight snack orders.

Whether it was enough wasn't for Oji to decide. The company that had spent tens of trillions was still searching for that answer.
