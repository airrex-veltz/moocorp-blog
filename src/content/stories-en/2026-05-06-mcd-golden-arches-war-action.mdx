In the summer of 1954, beneath the scorching sun of San Bernardino, Kroc stood motionless. The parking lot was filled with cars, and an endless line stretched before the ordering counter. "Fifteen seconds."

Park Hyung, the older of the Golden Food brothers, explained. The time it took for one hamburger to go from the grill to a customer's hand. What a traditional restaurant took 20 minutes to accomplish was done in 15 seconds. Kroc's heart began to race. The kitchen moved like a precision machine. One person grilled the patties, another prepared the buns, and yet another wrapped them. An assembly line. It was what Henry Ford had applied to automobiles now applied to hamburgers.

"I want to spread this across the country," Kroc said.

"We like it here," Park Dong, the younger brother, shook his head. "We're already making enough."

But Kroc had already made his decision. In his mind's eye, he could already see a map of the entire United States unfold before him. Hundreds, thousands of golden arches standing beside highways, in the heart of cities. The next day, he persuaded the brothers to sign a franchise agreement. 1.9% of annual revenues to the brothers, 0.5% to himself. It was an unfavorable deal, but it didn't matter. Speed was everything.

On April 15, 1955, the first location opened in Des Plaines, Illinois. Kroc stood at the counter himself. Hamburger 15 cents. French fries 10 cents. Milkshake 20 cents. "Fast, clean, accurate." His orders were military-like. That first night, as he cleaned the store, he thought to himself, 'This is just the beginning.'

But the war was just beginning. Competitors started to move. Kingsburger attacked with aggressive competition, and Wendis wielded 'freshness' as its weapon. Kroc did not retreat an inch. Instead, he accelerated further. He opened more stores, faster. 100 in one year. 200 in two years. The entire United States was blanketed with golden arches.

His secret was systems. He standardized everything. The weight of patties, the time for frying fries, even the greetings of employees. The same taste, the same experience, at every location. This was Kroc's weapon.

In 1961, the decisive moment came. Kroc went to find the Golden Food brothers.

"$2.7 million. I'll buy all the rights."

The brothers hesitated. But Kroc's gaze was unwavering. He had already secured a loan from the bank. The deal was done. That night, Kroc remained alone in his office and spread out a map. Red pins marked locations across the nation. Each one was his store. But there was still so much empty space.

"Now it begins."

Golden Food stood in Tokyo's Ginza, on Paris's Champs-Élysées.
