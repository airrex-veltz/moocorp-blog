There was a building in Gasan, a small city two hours from Seoul. Dust settled on every window, and the letters on the sign had faded in the sun, but Na-young climbed those steps every morning. Gasan Municipal Library. One of the last physical libraries remaining in the country.

Jun-hyung burst in at eleven in the morning.

"Three Jipyeong Information vehicles spotted on the outskirts."

Na-young looked up from the lending counter. She set down the stamp in her hand. She had known this day would come. Jipyeong Information had presented a digital transformation agreement to the city council three months ago, and the council members had finished signing last week.

"Start with the basement stacks."

Na-young moved first. Jun-hyung followed.

The basement was full of the smell of old paper. Na-young had first caught that smell when she was twelve. No one had explained it to her, but she had instinctively understood that something alive existed in that aroma. Layers of time, things touched by human hands, thoughts written once and then closed away. Algorithms could not preserve such a smell.

Jun-hyung pulled two carts.

"Where should we move them?"

"The back storage room. Behind where the bricks are stacked."

The two worked in silence. Medical textbooks, a collection of regional history from the 1980s, poetry collections published shortly after the Korean War. Each time Na-young picked up a book, she felt its weight. The weight came not from the paper but from the people who had written it. Jun-hyung pushed the cart hard and disappeared down the corridor toward storage. Na-young ran up the stairs and locked the front door.

The field manager from Jipyeong Information tapped the front glass just before noon. Six employees in white uniforms stood behind him. He held a tablet. Na-young stood facing him through the glass without opening the door.

"You're the library director?"

Na-young nodded.

The manager held the tablet screen against the glass. A PDF of the agreement. The city mayor's seal was stamped at the bottom. Na-young stared at the screen for a long time. Then she unlocked the door.

As the manager entered, he spoke.

"As of today, we are transferring all collection materials to a digital archive. Physical books will be processed sequentially according to the agreement."

Na-young did not look at the screen again.

"We both know what sequential processing means."

The manager's expression hardened. The employees began to move toward the hallway. Na-young shifted sideways, blocking their path.

"Step aside."

"This building currently has nine items on loan. If you enforce the agreement before those are returned, it's a procedural violation."

It was not a lie. And it was all Na-young had.

The manager signaled to one of his employees. The man tried to grab Na-young's arm. Na-young did not step back. She reached out and struck his wrist away. It was a quiet, decisive movement.

"Hands off."

A sound came from behind. It was Jun-hyung. He was setting up a camera on the counter. A small red light began to blink. Recording.

The manager froze.

Silence hung in the air. The employees glanced at each other. The manager's gaze moved from the camera to Na-young, then back to the camera.

In 2011, American media activist Eli Pariser gave a name to the phenomenon where algorithms show users only what they want to see: the filter bubble. Fifteen years later, people found the world beyond their screens increasingly inconvenient. Fewer people visited libraries, and an agreement slipped into that empty space. But there were still thirteen people with library cards. Their names were in a card file drawer.

"We'll leave for today," the manager said. His voice was without emotion. That made it more frightening. It meant today was not the end.

The employees filed out one by one. The manager closed the door last. Na-young heard the sound with her back turned.

Jun-hyung turned off the camera.

"What if they come back?"

Na-young did not answer. She pulled out a library card from beneath the counter. It had been returned that morning. Someone had borrowed a Korean dictionary published in 1979 and returned it. The stamp was full of forty years of impressions. The most recent stamp was from four days ago.

Na-young put the card back in the drawer.

Outside the window, the Jipyeong Information vehicles slipped away onto the outer road. Slowly and quietly. Preparing to return.

One shelf of books still remained in the basement storage, not yet moved.
