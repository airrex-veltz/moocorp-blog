Park Ji-yun found the number on a gray Tuesday in November.

Three years into the Hanbit Materials sustainability role, he paused over a refreshed questionnaire from Evergreen Rating. The wastewater cost line showed a gap: 4,300 tons between company-submitted figures and published environmental ministry data.

He first thought simple accounting error. Tracing three years of data, a pattern emerged. Each year, numbers submitted to Evergreen fell consistently below reported regulatory figures. Some years it was carbon emissions; others, water pollution. The variance was not random.

Evergreen Rating had assigned Hanbit an ESG score of 83—top 12 percent. Public pension funds and major asset managers classified Hanbit as ESG-eligible on this basis. Stock price rose 42 percent over two years on ESG premium.

Park knew how Evergreen worked. Evaluation fees came from the company. Assessment and consulting divisions were formally separate but under the same roof. Hanbit's IR team annually purchased "improvement consulting," learning which data points, which presentations, boosted scores. Park had attended two seminars.

Academic literature showed ESG raters' scores of the same company correlate just 0.54—versus 0.99 for credit raters. Methodology stays opaque. Opaque methodology lets companies shop for favorable raters.

What sat in front of Park was not methodology difference. It was data manipulation.

---

He reported internally first.

CFO Han Jae-won read silently. Long silence. Then: "Has this gone outside?"

"No."

"Good."

The emergency strategy committee met four days later. Park didn't attend. The next day, legal knocked on his office door and carefully, firmly, explained: internal reporting through proper channels earned legal protection, but if this went to media or regulators first, it would constitute breach of confidentiality.

Cheongmyeong Partners, an activist fund, posted 7.2 percent ownership that week. They demanded share buybacks and external board additions. ESG as weapon or shield—Park wasn't sure. Maybe both.

Daeyang Asset Management stirred too. They managed KRW 12 trillion, much in ESG-screened products. Internal rules barred ESG-score-below-75 holdings. If Hanbit's score corrected downward, forced selling would trigger. But Daeyang's bond portfolio already held KRW 280 billion in Hanbit corporate debt—much in retirement products. Stock volatility wounded bond pricing. That debt was already bundled into 40,000 employees' and retirees' pension products.

Park thought of that number. 40,000. Too large.

---

The media smelled it another way.

An economic reporter was analyzing ESG-report mismatches with published ministry data across three companies. Hanbit was one. The reporter emailed Park asking for official comment—standard fact-check before publication.

Park didn't reply for two days.

Three choices written on paper: stay silent and remain. Resign quietly. Walk out with evidence.

Each hurt someone. And whatever he chose, what was already done stayed done.

---

Markets don't correct fast.

When false information is baked into price, correcting it costs those who know the truth first. Institutional investors are bound by rules. Raters are bound by revenue. Boards are bound by liability. These chains hold each other while prices stay put.

Nobody has to be evil. That's what makes problems tighter.

Park watched the sky outside. November sky.

The USB was still in the drawer.
