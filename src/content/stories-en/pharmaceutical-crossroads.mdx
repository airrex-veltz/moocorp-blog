Sora Yoon had been sitting in front of the Phase 3 database lock screen for twelve hours. On the display was the final statistical analysis table for 'Jointex,' an anti-arthritis drug developed by Eunsung Pharmaceutical. She had been taught that numbers don't lie. But right now, these numbers seemed to be hiding something.

The primary efficacy endpoint showed a statistically significant improvement in pain reduction compared to placebo. It was the result the company had waited three years for. The problem lay in the subsidiary table, in the adverse events section. The incidence of elevated ALT—a marker of liver toxicity—in the high-dose group was nearly three times higher than in the placebo group. A footnote still classified this as 'not statistically significant,' but Sora knew that footnote existed only because the sample size was insufficient. If Phase 3 had run for just six more months, that asterisk would likely disappear.

"Team Lead Sora, when can we get the initial board report draft?" Dae-young Han, the head of clinical development, cracked open the door and asked. There was a hint of impatience in his voice. Eunsung Pharmaceutical had seen its stock price cut in half over the past two years due to repeated pipeline failures, and Jointex was the company's last card to survive.

"Still reviewing it. I'll have it to you by tonight," Sora replied simply.

As the door closed, she turned back to the screen. She recalled a case study from graduate school—in 2007, a multinational pharmaceutical company had downplayed cardiovascular risk signals for a diabetes treatment drug through late-stage clinical trials, only for the truth to emerge later, leading to massive recalls and litigation. Back then, it had felt like someone else's problem. An ethics dilemma from a textbook. Now it was a report with her name on the signature line.

She had two choices. First, describe the adverse events with statistical honesty but hide behind the phrase 'not statistically significant.' It would not violate regulations. The footnote would be factual. The approach would simply arrange the sentences in a way that prevented the reader from grasping the magnitude of the risk. Second, submit the raw data as-is—the threefold number alongside a clear statement of the confidence interval's width due to limited sample size—in the submission to the regulatory authority. Approval could be delayed, or at worst, the path could shift toward conditional approval.

At 10 p.m., Sora recalled a 68-year-old patient she had coordinated during her time as a clinical trial coordinator. Someone who couldn't climb stairs due to knee pain. For that patient, Jointex would be the drug that gave back quality of life. At the same time, if that patient's liver values were compromised, and they continued taking the drug unaware of this fact, they could suffer irreversible damage.

Standing before numbers with two faces, she recognized that a single sentence she was now writing could translate into something that actually happened in someone's body. Simultaneously, she knew that this single line of the report was connected to the employment of thousands of Eunsung Pharmaceutical employees and to the accounts of retail investors who had already suffered losses and endured. Neither was abstract.

At 2 a.m., Dae-young Han sent a message: "Good news is already circulating in the division. Looking forward to the report tomorrow." Sora read that sentence three times. Where had the rumor about good results started? Only three people had access to this database besides her.

She opened the document file and placed the cursor over the adverse events section. Her fingers paused briefly above the keyboard. She had not yet erased anything or emphasized anything. But somewhere in that moment, one thing was clear: whatever sentence she wrote now would never be erased again.

Beyond the window, dawn was breaking. Sora stared at the screen, the cursor blinking, her finger never touching the save button.
