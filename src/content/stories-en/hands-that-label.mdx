At eleven at night, Park Ji-eun's fingers did not stop.

Images flowed across the screen without pause. Highway lane markings, pallets in an empty warehouse, a child's facial expression, a suspected tumor on a medical chart. She judged them, classified them, and checked them off. About forty seconds per image. Three thousand won per hour.

Ji-eun had been doing this work for two years. Forty-four years old. Two children. Her husband had injured his back working in transportation and now stayed at home. When she first joined this platform, the onboarding screen had displayed a message: "Your one correct answer builds a better future." Ji-eun did not believe it. She simply clicked. Money came in when she clicked.

In the first year, she sometimes wondered why the world asked people to do this kind of work. Drawing rectangles around pedestrians in photos, distinguishing roads and sidewalks by color, attaching labels like "aggressive" or "neutral" to conversation sentences. No one told her where any of it was used. Her coworkers in the same task pool were similar. A woman from somewhere in Gyeonggi Province who occasionally messaged in the chat room, a man doing night shifts in South Gyeongsang Province. All three found it faster not to ask.

That afternoon, a notice appeared at the top of the work screen.

"Urban Pedestrian Recognition Task — AI accuracy reached 97.4%. This pool will close within the week."

Ji-eun read that sentence twice. 97.4 percent. She vaguely remembered what that number was when this task first opened. Somewhere in the low 71 percent range. It had been written small in the onboarding email two years ago. "Current model performance 71.2%. You will help us do better with your contributions."

26 percentage points. She now knew what had filled that gap.

She paused in the middle of preparing dinner and stood by the kitchen window. An afternoon last fall came back to her—a paragraph she had happened to read while watching news about an international AI conference. Millions of data labelers from around the world, most of them receiving between one and three dollars per hour, spending all day classifying images. Small cities in Africa, Southeast Asia, Latin America. When those clicks accumulated and the model became smart enough, it would no longer need those clicks. At the time, Ji-eun had felt as though it were someone else's story. She had vaguely assumed that at least she was in Korea, earning a little more than they did.

Now, after reading today's notice, she understood that the difference mattered very little. What was needed to climb from 71 to 97 was the same everywhere. Hands. Eyes. Judgment. And once judgment accumulated sufficiently, the person doing the judging was needed no more.

Ji-eun sat down in her chair again. A new task pool was already open. "Autonomous Driving Scenario — Tracking Urban Pedestrians on Rainy Nights." Forty-five won per image. Ten solid hours of work a day barely reached the minimum wage threshold.

She opened the first image. A rainy intersection. An elderly person with an umbrella under a streetlight. She grasped the mouse, drew a box, selected "pedestrian," and clicked.

Next image. Then the next. Her hands moved.

She knew what she was feeding. Yet she did not stop. Whether she couldn't stop or didn't want to stop yet. Today, she decided not to think about where that line was.

Rain fell outside the window.
