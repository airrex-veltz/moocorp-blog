Dr. Jung's fingers no longer moved as quickly as they once had. The hand that once traced delicate lines across silicon wafers now gripped a paper cup, unaware that the coffee was growing cold. The lab lights remained as white as ever, but the person sitting beneath them had changed.

In 1974, when he first turned his hand to chip design, it took days to draw a single logic gate. Drafting pens lay neatly on the ink shelf, and the smell of correction fluid filled the lab. Back then, all semiconductor companies spoke the same language. Integration density was low, but trust was high.

Now, everything was different.

"Dr. Jung, the meeting is starting."

His secretary's voice filtered through the doorway. Dr. Jung stood up. His knees ached. That too was an unfamiliar sensation. His body remembered its age.

As the video conference opened, faces from Beijing and Shenzhen appeared in a grid. Lee Hong-zhen, the CEO of the Chinese partner company, was wearing a tie that day. It signaled the formality of the occasion.

"I'd like to discuss your patent, EP2847631, Dr. Jung."

Dr. Jung's ears perked up. EP2847631. It was a low-power cache controller architecture registered in 2009. Back when mobile processors drained batteries like water, it was a design he had spent eighteen months perfecting through countless nights.

"We discovered that there are similarities between our independently developed IP and your design. Of course, our company had no idea about it..."

Dr. Jung stared at Lee's eyes through the screen. Similarities. That word was being used with remarkable skill.

After the meeting ended, he opened the patent search database. CNIPA, China's National Intellectual Property Administration. As he entered the search terms, results flooded the screen. Three fabless companies headquartered in Shenzhen were producing SoCs based on circuits structurally identical to his cache controller architecture. They were using SMIC's 28-nanometer process. The mass production cost was one-fifth of what it had been when he designed it.

He printed out the documents and stacked them; the weight pressed down on his palm.

That evening, his daughter Ji-eun visited his office. Ji-eun was a lawyer specializing in patent law. She was the only person he could discuss this matter with.

"You need to sue, Father. The evidence is there."

"Evidence or not, enforcement won't work."

Dr. Jung leaned back in his worn chair. Outside the window lay the night of Pangyo Techno Valley. Lights were densely packed together. How many of those lights, he wondered, were lit at the cost of someone's lost sleep?

"It happened back in the early 2000s too. Right after I finished the DRAM controller design. The copying was slow then. Five years to reverse engineer it, another three years to mass-produce. By then, we'd already moved on to the next generation."

Ji-eun interrupted her father.

"It's different now. Cloud-based EDA tools, published academic papers, open architectures like RISC-V. The copying cycle has shrunk to eighteen months. Your patent has a twenty-year lifespan, and half of it is already gone."

Dr. Jung nodded.

His daughter was right. The advances in design automation tools had accelerated not only the pace of innovation but also the pace of imitation. The SPICE simulator he had used when first learning VLSI design was now hundreds of times more powerful, and that power was given equally to all. Even to those who could not be trusted.

The next morning, he drove to the fab in Suwon. It wasn't the cleanroom he had first entered fifty years ago, but it was a ten-minute walk from the laboratory of the professor who first taught him semiconductors. Now this fab had advanced to 5-nanometer processes. While the line widths he had started with—10 micrometers—had shrunk to one two-thousandth their original size, he too had grown old.

Behind the cleanroom glass, wafer handlers moved quietly. Machines never tired. Dr. Jung gently pressed his forehead against the glass. It was cold.

He understood that his patents were embedded in each of these processes. In the thermal treatment sequences, in the photoresist patterns, in the interlayer dielectric thickness algorithms. The thought that his inventions were simultaneously running in fabs on the other side of the world was not unfamiliar to him. Rather, it felt strangely natural.

The court battle would begin. Ji-eun was preparing for it. But the reason Dr. Jung lingered long before the fab that day was not just anger. He felt the fact that the things he had left behind were alive and moving in the world in some form.

Without permission.

And that hurt him even more.

The envelope of documents from the Patent Office's customer service office was thick. The procedure would begin. International patent dispute arbitration, ITC litigation, negotiation tables. Ji-eun looked confident. Dr. Jung took his daughter's hand.

"We might win."

"Yes."

"Still, that design is probably already running on a million devices."

Ji-eun did not answer.

It was spring. Cherry blossoms in front of the Pangyo Research Institute bloomed white, then began to fall. Dr. Jung gazed for a moment at petals pressed onto asphalt, turning brown. Perhaps innovation was like that. Once it bloomed fully and was given to the world, the flower itself could no longer decide who it belonged to.

He walked into the research institute. A new design drawing lay on his desk. A cache architecture for 2-nanometer processes. He had thought it would be his last project, but his fingers were already holding the pen.
