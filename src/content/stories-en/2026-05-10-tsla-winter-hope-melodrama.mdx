Winter 2008. Park Jun-ho gazed out the window of a small office in California. The dark clouds of the global financial crisis had engulfed the entire world, and Raon Electric, the company he had bet everything on, stood on the brink of bankruptcy. The bank statement in his hand bore only cold figures. The company needed another 30 million dollars to survive, but that money existed nowhere.

"CEO, I'm afraid we won't be able to pay the employees' salaries this month," the finance director's voice trembled. Park Jun-ho nodded. He had already poured all his personal wealth into the company. SpaceX was wavering too, and his divorce proceedings were underway. He was the loneliest man in the world.

But in one corner of the factory, the Roadster prototype glowed quietly. A beautiful machine that had achieved everything deemed impossible for an electric vehicle. Park Jun-ho knelt down before the car. "I'm sorry. I may not be able to bring you into the world."

The engineers remained in the workshop through the night. It had been two months since they last received their salaries, but none of them left. They had not stayed for a job alone. They endured with just one belief: that they could change the world. A young engineer spoke. "CEO, we will stay with you till the end. We want to see this car come into the world."

That night, Park Jun-ho made his final choice. On Christmas Eve, he called every investor he knew. "I will invest my last remaining money in Raon Electric. Will you join me?" Most refused. The cold rejections echoed over the phone. "See reality, Park Jun-ho. Electric vehicles have no future."

But at 3 a.m., a phone call came. It was the voice of an old colleague and investor. "I remember what you said in 2003. That we would change the world. I still believe in those words. We are with you."

And so Raon Electric lived. In 2012, when the Model S came into the world, the entire globe was shocked. That an electric vehicle could be this beautiful, this fast, and this safe. Automotive critics praised it, and customers waited years for one. But amid all the acclaim, Park Jun-ho did not forget that winter night in 2008. That moment when he knelt before the Roadster. The light in the engineers' eyes as they endured together.

In 2017, when Model 3 production began, another crisis arrived. During what was called "production hell," Park Jun-ho slept on the factory floor. He fixed the lines with his own hands alongside his employees, solving problems. Someone asked him, "Why do you go this far?" Park Jun-ho replied, "This is not just a car. It is the future our children will breathe."

By 2020, Raon Electric had become the world's most valuable car company. But what Park Jun-ho was proudest of was not the market cap. It was the fact that hundreds of thousands of electric vehicles were driving on roads every day across the globe, cleaning the air.

One day, a customer sent him a letter. "My son suffered from asthma. As electric vehicles increased in our neighborhood, his condition improved. Thank you." Park Jun-ho read the letter and quietly wept. He framed it and hung it on the wall of his office.

The investor who had said he would stand with him on that Christmas Eve in 2008 came to visit. The two of them stood silently before the Roadster. "We did it," the investor said. Park Jun-ho nodded. "No, we are still doing it. There is still much road ahead."

What he preserved was not merely a company. It was the hope of people who believed in a better future, and the courage to turn the impossible into the possible. And Raon Electric showed how a dream born in despair could change the world. And that dream continued still.
