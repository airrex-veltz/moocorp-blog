## Red Alarm

Tuesday, April 23, 1985, 7:15 a.m.

Executives hastily summoned to the 38th-floor conference room of Coca-Cola headquarters had grim expressions on their faces. Chairman Roberto Goizueta slammed the table.

"Pepsi has caught up to us. We've already lost shelf share in supermarkets."

For the first time in 99 years, Coca-Cola faced an existential crisis. Pepsi had won blind taste test after blind taste test with its "Pepsi Challenge" campaign, and younger consumers were choosing Pepsi. Coca-Cola's signature red can was beginning to look like a relic of a bygone era.

"We have 90 days," said Sergio Zyman, the vice president of marketing. "If we don't counterattack before summer peak season, we could lose market dominance forever."

Silence fell over the room. Everyone knew. The file on the table contained a secret project that would change the company's fate.

**Project Kansas. Code name: New Coke.**

## Covert Operation

Over the past three years, Coca-Cola had conducted blind taste tests on 200,000 people in strict secrecy. The results were clear. The new formula beat Pepsi, and consumer preference was 6 percentage points higher than the original Coca-Cola.

"Science doesn't lie," Chairman Goizueta said. "We will win."

But Roy Stout, the R&D director, was uneasy. "Mr. Chairman, 10 percent of test respondents objected vehemently. They cursed over the phone and even sent threatening letters. This is not just a beverage. To people, Coca-Cola is..."

"Save the sentimentality for later," Goizueta cut him off. "The announcement is in one week. Get ready."

Noon on April 23, 1985—the most audacious product announcement in history began at Lincoln Center in New York. Seven hundred journalists from around the world packed the venue. Goizueta declared confidently:

"Today, Coca-Cola becomes new. Smoother, sweeter taste."

That evening, all three major American networks led with the news. A loyal customer in Seattle wept on camera. "They stole my childhood."

## The Rebellion Begins

Forty-eight hours later, Coca-Cola headquarters' phone lines were jammed. 40,000 protest calls flooded in daily. "Traitors!" "Give us back the real Coca-Cola!"

A lawyer in Atlanta formed a group called "Old Cola Drinkers of America" and prepared a class action lawsuit. Fidel Castro mocked it as a "sign of the collapse of American capitalism."

The marketing department spiraled into panic. Sergio Zyman rushed to the chairman's office.

"Mr. Chairman, sales are collapsing. Bottlers in the South have rebelled. They're stockpiling inventory and refusing to ship!"

Goizueta gazed out the window. Protesters with picket signs had gathered in the plaza in front of headquarters. "GIVE US BACK THE REAL THING"—give us back the real thing.

On the 77th day, night of July 10, 1985, 11 p.m.

Goizueta convened an emergency board meeting. The latest data lay on the table. Market share down 3.1 percent. The gap with Pepsi widening. Risk of bottler defection.

"We have lost," Goizueta said quietly. "But the war is not over."

## The Counter-Attack

The next morning at 9 a.m., a simultaneous press conference worldwide.

"We have heard the voice of the American people," Goizueta declared. "Beginning today, we will produce original Coca-Cola again. Under a new name. **Coca-Cola Classic.**"

At that moment, headquarters' phones rang again. This time with cheers. "Thank you!" "Finally!"

On the New York Stock Exchange, Coca-Cola's stock surged 8 percent within 10 minutes of opening. Pepsi's marketing vice president couldn't hide his bewilderment on CNN. "Was this... calculated?"

In fact, it wasn't calculated. But the result was perfect.

Three months later, Coca-Cola Classic had retaken the number-one position from Pepsi. Some consumers chose New Coke, but the majority celebrated the return of the classic. Through 77 days of crisis, Coca-Cola had proven it was not merely a beverage, but part of American culture itself.

Goizueta later reflected: "We made a mistake. But because of that mistake, we learned how much people love Coca-Cola. Sometimes losing is the path to winning."

By year-end 1985, Coca-Cola's market share had risen 2 percentage points above pre-crisis levels. The Pepsi Challenge was no longer a threat. Americans had clearly shown what they wanted.

**The red can. The original taste. Real Coca-Cola.**
