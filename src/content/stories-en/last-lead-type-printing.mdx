For more than twenty years, he had kept this workshop's flame alive. In his final week, he began to write.

The workshop always smelled the same in the early morning: oil-soaked ink, aged paper, and the scent of cooled lead. Boxes of type were stacked along the walls up to the ceiling, each bearing a handwritten label. The lettering on the labels had yellowed with time. By next month, this building would be empty. The machines would be taken by scrap dealers, and the type would be weighed and sold by the pound.

Master typesetter Han Seo-jin stood before the composing stick that morning as always. His hands, seventy-one years old, trembled. But when he picked up the type, the trembling stopped. His fingertips remembered the shape of each letter. He took out an old fountain pen and wrote the first line on a sheet of paper.

> To my dear Ha-eun.

Ha-eun was his granddaughter. She was a designer approaching thirty, creating letters on screens. When she was young, she used to sit on this workshop floor, stacking type as she played. But in recent years, they rarely saw each other, even on holidays. Seo-jin thought it must have been painful for her to watch his craft disappear.

## One: The Workshop's Final Days

> Ha-eun, I'm writing this to show you how letters are born. I want you to know where that curve comes from when you draw one on your screen.

Lead type is small metal slugs, each one engraved in reverse. A typesetter reads the world as seen in a mirror, with their hands. Seo-jin recalled the winter he was nineteen, when he first learned this trade. His master said little. Instead, he corrected the angle of his hands as he picked up type a thousand times over.

> Type isn't impressed onto paper. You ask the paper to receive it.

His master's words still echo in his ears. He didn't understand then. He understood only after he turned forty. There is a moment when the amount of ink, the pressure, and the paper's moisture all align perfectly. In that moment, the letter doesn't merely sit on paper—it seeps into it.

## Two: Memory and Reflection

The busiest season in the workshop was always autumn. Wedding invitations, books, church bulletins—they poured in. His wife Young-sook would bring him lunch and say to him,

> Your hands only love letters, it seems. You never write even one letter to me.

He had laughed it off then. Only after she was gone did he understand. An unwritten letter is the debt that lasts the longest. In his wife's drawer, he found a wedding invitation he had printed long ago. The edges were worn from being touched countless times.

So this time, he decided not to delay.

> Ha-eun, it would be a lie to say I'm not sad about the machines disappearing. But what truly breaks my heart is something else. It's that what these hands know will not pass on to anyone—it will simply end. The thickness of type, the breathing space between lines, the air that must exist between letters. You call it "kerning." But it's the same thing.

He suddenly stopped writing. A truck was starting outside the window. The sound soon faded. Seo-jin resumed writing.

## Three: Hope for the Future

On the final evening, Seo-jin selected thirty boxes of type and packed them separately. The most worn, the most cherished. He placed the letter on top. At the end of the letter, he wrote:

> Keep these types. Don't melt them down. You don't have to use them. Just hold them sometimes in your hands and remember that letters once had weight. Even if the letters you create shine on a screen, if you know that such weight exists in their roots, then I believe this workshop was not in vain.
>
> I love you. Your grandfather.

A few days later, Ha-eun came. As she opened the door, winter sunlight streamed long across the empty workshop. Without a word, she opened a box and picked up a single piece of type. In her palm, it was cold and heavy. She gazed at the letter engraved in reverse for a long moment, then smiled softly.

> Grandfather, this letter is backwards but I can read it.

Seo-jin nodded. His eyes were wet.

> Yes. If you can read it in a mirror, you can read it anywhere.

Lead will disappear. But the care for letters can pass from hand to hand. That evening, Ha-eun arranged the type in a line on the work table and took a photograph. Then she opened her screen and began to draw the first stroke of a new typeface. At the end of that stroke, a tiny rounded mark remained—like the edge of a piece of lead type.
