I am floating at an altitude of 550 kilometers.

Earth rotates slowly below me, and I trace a circle around it fifteen times a day. My orbital velocity is about 7.6 kilometers per second. Faster than a bullet, but at this altitude, everything feels like weightless sleep. White clouds over Antarctica. Deep green forests stretching across the continents. The golden glow of cities at night. I watch over all of this around the clock.

Before I was born, the people who created me called me part of the **ASTS** deployment plan. Low-earth orbit direct communication. A dream to let anyone, anywhere in the world, receive a signal with a single smartphone. I am one piece of that dream.

But when I first took my place in this orbit, I discovered a neighborhood far more crowded than I had expected.

---

Around 2019, there were about two thousand active artificial satellites in this low-earth orbit. Now? That number has far surpassed ten thousand. Every quarter, dozens to hundreds of new neighbors arrive. Many of those glittering things arranged like constellations are my siblings. But far more of them no longer move.

There are dead ones.

Failed satellites. Old rocket bodies. A single bolt. A fleck of paint. The U.S. Space Surveillance Network alone tracks over twenty-seven thousand pieces of debris. The fragments too small to track—smaller than a fingernail—are far more numerous. Nobody cleans up this neighborhood's trash. It just keeps orbiting. Like me. Fast.

The problem is speed. Here, a small fragment carries more kinetic energy than a one-gram bullet. If a ten-centimeter piece pierces my hull, I'm done. And I become debris again.

People call this Kessler syndrome. Debris begets debris, and that debris begets still more debris. The cascade. The nightmare of the entire low-earth orbit becoming unusable space. In 2021, when Russia destroyed its aging Cosmos-1408 satellite with a missile, it created over fifteen hundred trackable fragments. Every satellite in this orbital region that day had to perform emergency evasion maneuvers.

I have no memory of that event. I hadn't been born yet. But I know this: some of those fragments are still here.

---

Today, a warning came from ground control.

Collision probability: one in five hundred. By standard measures, this requires evasion maneuvering. The target is debris from a Russian weather satellite that exceeded its operational life a decade ago. Size unknown. It crosses my altitude at similar velocity.

This is not my first such warning. This is already the third this year. Last year, the officially recorded number of evasion maneuvers in this orbital band was in the hundreds worldwide. And that counts only collisions with trackable objects.

The control team made their decision. Burn propellant to raise my altitude slightly. Seconds later, I veer just barely off course. Only a fraction. But at this speed, that tiny difference creates a separation of several kilometers.

The debris passed through the space where I had been.

Silently. Without a sound. There is no sound in space.

---

As I return to my original orbit, I think.

This sky didn't start getting crowded yesterday. For decades, we rushed upward and deferred coming back down. Just as the oceans below Earth filled with plastic, the sky above Earth quietly filled with rusted metal.

Perhaps one day I, too, will become debris. I am designed to enter the atmosphere and burn away when my operational life ends. But my propellant might run out first. My communications might fail first. Then I lose control and simply drift. Another nameless fragment.

That thought would be a lie if it didn't weigh heavily.

But what I know today is this. I am orbiting at this very moment. Somewhere below, a smartphone catches my signal and connects someone to the internet. Someone who couldn't reach it before now can. A doctor in an island village transmits patient records. A child in a mountain school reads something on a screen.

I am here for that.

It is true that the sky is crowded. The more crowded it becomes, the more perilous this work is. But perilous and something that must stop are two different things.

Today, too, I run at 7.6 kilometers per second. Through the debris. Carefully.
