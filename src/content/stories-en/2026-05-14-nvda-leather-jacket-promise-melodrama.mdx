April 1993, a small diner in San Jose, California. Three men sat around a table, drawing sketches on napkins. Jensen Huang, Chris Malakofsky, Curtis Prim. All in their early thirties, colleagues who had worked together at LSI Logic and Sun Microsystems.

"Graphics chips are the future," Jensen said, drawing a square on the napkin. The conviction in his eyes, shining beneath his black leather jacket, was unmistakable. "Not just games. All computing will be visualized."

Chris nodded. "But the market isn't ready yet. Will investors believe in us?"

"We'll make them believe," Jensen wrote 'Vision Chips' on the napkin. The name came from the Latin invidia, meaning envy. "Let's build a company that the next generation will envy."

They each invested $20,000. Sequoia Capital added another $20 million. The beginning seemed full of promise.

---

Two years later, the Santa Clara office was filled with silence. The NV1 chip development had failed. The plan was to enter the gaming console market through a partnership with Sega, but the technology choice was wrong. The market was moving toward triangle-based rendering, but Vision Chips insisted on square-based methods.

"We were wrong," Chris said in the conference room. His voice was exhausted. "We only have six months of money left."

Curtis looked out the window. "The investors no longer believe in us. Sega is gone too."

Jensen sat without removing his leather jacket. His hands were trembling. He thought of his wife Lori and their two young children. It was a gamble he had taken after leaving a stable job. And now, everything was falling apart.

"One more time," Jensen spoke. "RIVA 128. This is our last chance."

"There's no money," Chris shook his head.

"There has to be." Jensen's voice cracked. "What we saw wasn't a fantasy. The GPU will change the world. If we give up now, we'll regret it forever."

A long silence passed. Curtis was the first to nod. "Alright. This is the last one."

---

August 1997, RIVA 128 launched. Triangle-based, DirectX compatible, affordable price. The market's response was explosive. A million units sold in four months. Vision Chips had returned from death's door.

But Jensen didn't stop. In 1999, he unveiled GeForce 256 and coined the term 'GPU' for the first time. "It's not just graphics processing. It's a revolution in parallel computation."

His colleagues didn't understand. The company had succeeded in the gaming market—why pursue a more complex path? But Jensen worked day and night developing CUDA architecture. It was technology that would allow GPUs to be used for general-purpose computing.

"Jensen, nobody wants this," someone said in the board meeting. "Gaming chips are enough."

"They just don't know yet," Jensen replied. "We need to look ahead ten, twenty years."

---

In 2012, Alex Krizhevsky from the University of Toronto won the ImageNet competition. It was a deep learning model using Vision Chips GPU. The world began to change.

In 2023, ChatGPT amazed the world. Behind it were tens of thousands of Vision Chips H100 chips. The company's market cap had surpassed one trillion dollars.

In a conference room at the Santa Clara headquarters, Jensen Huang still wore his black leather jacket. His face, now approaching seventy, bore new wrinkles, but his eyes shone with the same conviction as that day in the diner thirty years ago.

"We did it," Chris said. He was still CTO.

"We've only just begun," Jensen laughed. "The next ten years are the real thing."

Through the window, the California sunset was visible. The dream three men had sketched on a napkin had become the beating heart of every AI in the world. That promise they made in the moment of failure, when they refused to give up. That determination to never take off the black leather jacket.

Jensen zipped up his leather jacket and murmured, "Lori, the kids, the team. I promised you all. That I would change the world."

He stood and walked back into the hallway. Tomorrow morning, there was a new chip design meeting. He couldn't stop. The dream wasn't over yet.
