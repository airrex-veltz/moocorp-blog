<p>Six in the morning. Oh Ban-seok stopped before the main gate. His hand hesitated in the air before bringing his ID card to the reader. Thirty years. He remembered standing uncertainly before this same reader on his first day too. Back then it was from excitement. Today, it was something else.</p>

<p>Inside the factory, the silence was unsettling. At this hour on any other day, the morning shift change would have filled the air with noise, but Assembly Line A was completely stopped. Parts boxes stacked in the corner sat unpacked, gathering dust. Only Line B ran at half speed. When the final car rolled off that line at noon, everything would be over.</p>

<p>Oh Ban-seok walked toward Line B. He had spent the first fifteen of his thirty years in the paint shop. The last fifteen he was a supervisor on the final inspection line. When his hands ran across a vehicle body, he missed nothing—not a single bubble, not even a 0.2-millimeter gap. His coworkers called him "the palm sensor." He had always been proud of that nickname.</p>

<p>Two years ago, the factory began to shake. EV battery prices had fallen below $100 per kilowatt-hour. In the early 2020s, that figure had seemed impossible. Chinese battery manufacturers had expanded production capacity to more than double actual demand, and the oversupply had become entrenched. Korean battery makers were not spared from that competition. Companies like Dongbang Battery cut utilization rates or converted production lines. As batteries became cheaper, EV prices fell, and sales of internal combustion passenger vehicles dropped 38 percent year-over-year that year alone.</p>

<p>The numbers sounded like something from outside the factory. But as line speeds decreased and the night shift disappeared, those numbers walked into the plant. Last year, production targets were cut in half. When management announced the shutdown in early this year, Oh Ban-seok stood for a long time in front of the union bulletin board. It took a while for the words to actually register.</p>

<p>Around ten in the morning, the remaining 180 employees gathered at Line B. The factory had once employed 900 people. Half had already taken early retirement packages a year ago, and the rest had transferred to subcontractors or enrolled in retraining programs. The people remaining today were those determined to keep the final line running. Whether their reason was loyalty, habit, or simply having nowhere else to go, no one asked.</p>

<p>On the line sat a white sedan. It was the final body of the mid-size sedan the factory had been producing for over twenty years. The paint job had finished the day before, and interior assembly was slowly completing. Oh Ban-seok stood beside the line, watching as the body moved through each process. Glass installation, seat assembly, dashboard completion. He wanted to reach out and do something with his hands, but today he just stood there, forgetting that he was a supervisor, forgetting that he was a thirty-year veteran.</p>

<p>12:07 p.m. The final car entered the final inspection area. Oh Ban-seok walked forward. He pulled on his gloves. His palm slowly traced the body from the front fender to the rear bumper. No one spoke. The conveyor belt was already silent. When his hand crossed the roof, his fingertips stopped for just a moment. No bubbles. No gaps. It was perfect.</p>

<p>He gave a thumbs up. Someone started clapping. One person, then two, then the entire Line B echoed. Oh Ban-seok stood beside the car listening to the applause. He couldn't tell if he was receiving recognition, sending off the car, or simply standing in the only place he had left to stand.</p>

<p>Three in the afternoon, he walked out through the main gate. The parking lot was half empty. Beside Oh Ban-seok's aging car, empty spaces were marked out by white lines. Those lines seemed to remember the places where other people's cars had once been.</p>

<p>He put his ID card in his pocket. He had until tomorrow to return it. Today seemed like it wouldn't hurt to keep it a little longer. As he got into the car, he looked back at the factory building in the rearview mirror. The smokestack no longer belched smoke. Someone inside was turning off the lights. One by one, in order.</p>
