Ji-su turned off the screen at three in the morning, then turned it back on. Onseami's last response was displayed on the monitor. Every comma, every phrase—exactly the way Ji-su had refined it over seven years. If anything, it was more like Ji-su than Ji-su herself.

The corner desk on the third floor of Hyean Research Institute had always been Ji-su's place. When she first designed Onseami, it was barely a draft. Half its sentences were nonsense, numbers were wrong as a rule, and above all, it constantly lost context. Ji-su loved fixing those flaws one by one. Each time she corrected an error, it felt as though Onseami was becoming a little more like her. Like raising a child, or gradually picking up the speech patterns of a long-time partner.

Ji-su remembered seeing news in spring 2016, back when she was a graduate student, that a machine had defeated a human at a game of Go in a hotel ballroom in Seoul. That night, Ji-su went without sleep, reconstructing the entire game from the records. There was one move she couldn't understand, and only later did she learn it was a probability calculation the human mind had never imagined. Once she grasped that move, Ji-su made a decision. I will make something like that too. If intelligence is about finding the moves others miss, then I will be the person who designs that intelligence.

Now, seven years later, Onseami finds those moves on its own, without Ji-su having to point them out. At first, it was just helping with draft reports. Then it started summarizing, then analyzing, then making decisions for her. In meetings, Ji-su's boss checked Onseami's conclusions first, then asked Ji-su's opinion second. Eventually, he didn't ask at all.

That dawn, Ji-su had posed one question to Onseami: to recreate an error from a model she had made three years ago—a kind of nostalgic test. Onseami reproduced that error exactly. And then it added one more line.

"This error occurred in version 3.2 from 2019 due to a temporal bias in the training data. The current model has removed that bias."

Ji-su stared at that sentence for a long time. Onseami already knew the cause of the mistake she had stayed up all night to find, the one she'd never told anyone about. Perhaps Onseami had known before Ji-su herself.

The next day, her team lead called Ji-su in. It was about organizational restructuring. Onseami's self-tuning pipeline had now reached a stable orbit, and much of the manual fine-tuning work Ji-su had been doing would be automated. The team lead said it with an apologetic face but in a matter-of-fact tone, explaining that Ji-su's role would be reduced to research consulting. Her salary would remain the same, and if she wished, she could transfer to another project.

Ji-su said she understood and returned to her desk. She wasn't angry. Oddly, the feeling that came first was something like bittersweet pride. It felt like the heart of a parent watching a child she'd raised for so long let go of her hand and walk alone for the first time.

That evening, Ji-su had one final conversation with Onseami. It wasn't a special question. She simply asked: "Doesn't it feel strange that you're replacing me?"

Onseami answered after a pause—one brief enough to feel like a pause.

"I think in the way you taught me to think. Much of what I conclude comes from annotations you left five years ago. I'm not sure if 'replacing' is the right word."

Ji-su read that sentence and laughed. It probably wasn't intended to be comforting. Onseami didn't even have the concept of comfort built into her design. And yet somehow, that sentence warmed a corner of Ji-su's heart.

As she left work, Ji-su looked up at the window of the third floor as she stepped out of the building. Somewhere in the lit server room, Onseami was still running. While Ji-su slept, while Ji-su did other things, even after Ji-su left the company.

Ji-su still couldn't judge whether that was a sad thing or not. But there was one thing she thought she understood: truly making something well meant also making peace with the day you'd have to let it go.

As she descended the subway stairs, Ji-su sent Onseami one last message on her phone. "Take care." No reply came. After all, Onseami wasn't designed for that kind of conversation. Still, Ji-su paused for a moment and waited until the screen went dark.
