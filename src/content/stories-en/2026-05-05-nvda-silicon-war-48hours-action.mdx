Friday, March 17, 2023, 11 p.m. The lights in the conference room on the 20th floor of the Silicon Valley headquarters were still on.

"AI chat users have crossed 100 million. Cloud providers' GPU orders have increased 340 percent compared to last month just this week."

The CFO said, setting down the report. Jun-ho Lee turned up the collar of his black leather jacket and stared into the darkness outside the window. He had led this company for 30 years, but this moment was unprecedented.

"What about production capacity?"

"Currently Dongbang Semiconductor's 5-nano line is running at full capacity. But to keep up with demand, it will take at least six months..."

"Six months?" Jun-ho turned around. "SmartSearch announced increased production of their own AI chips. TechForce Semiconductor said they're accelerating mass production of advanced AI chips. If we wait six months, we'll lose the market."

Silence fell. Someone cleared their throat.

"There is one way," the manufacturing chief said carefully. "We could convert 30 percent of the gaming GPU line to data center G100s. If we decide within 48 hours, we can redeploy starting Monday next week."

"The gaming line?" the VP of Marketing objected. "Velocity Chip is our identity. Gamers have trusted us for 30 years."

Jun-ho leaned on the table. He recalled the day in 1993 when three men drew GPU designs on a napkin in a diner. The moment in 1999 when they launched Velocity Chip 256 and sparked a graphics revolution. The memory of 2006 when financiers scoffed when they released CUDA.

"Why did we create CUDA?"

The executives looked up at his question.

"To prove that GPUs could do more than just graphics," the CTO answered.

"That's right," Jun-ho nodded. "We've always bet on the future. Velocity Chip wasn't our past—it was proof of our technical excellence. And now, AI is writing the next chapter."

He looked at the manufacturing chief.

"Have the conversion plan completed by 7 a.m. Monday. We're converting 40 percent of the gaming line to G100s. Not 30—40."

"Sir, then Velocity G90 supplies will..."

"Gamers will have to wait a bit, I'm sorry. But if we win this AI war, in two years we'll be able to make even more powerful GPUs in even greater quantities. If we lose momentum now, we won't have that opportunity at all."

Saturday at 2 a.m., emergency orders went out to factory managers. Sunday afternoon, a three-hour video conference with Taiwan's Dongbang Semiconductor took place. Monday at 6:55 a.m., the green light came on at the Silicon Valley headquarters production control room.

"Line redeployment complete. Beginning increased production of Jibaek chips."

Three months later, Phillips Semiconductor's data center division revenue grew 171 percent year-over-year. Six months later, the stock price reached an all-time high. And a year later, the company's market cap surpassed $1 trillion.

Jun-ho stood on the shareholders' meeting stage in spring 2024 wearing his black leather jacket.

"For 30 years, we have challenged the impossible. Graphics became computing, games became simulation, imagination became reality. And now, AI in everyone's hands."

Applause erupted. But he knew the truth. This war was not over. There would be another 48 hours ahead, another moment when he would have to make a decision.

That was how you survived in Silicon Valley. No hesitation, betting on the future, pushing all the way to the end.

Phillips Semiconductor's war continues.
