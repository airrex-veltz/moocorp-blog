Autumn 1997, the third-floor laboratory of the Daejeon Technology Research Institute.

"Failed again."

Suh Ji-young sighed and closed her experiment notebook. The lithium-ion battery cathode material development project had been stalled for three months now. Choi Min-ho at the next desk remained bent over his microscope, his face buried in the eyepiece.

"Min-ho, let's call it a day. It's already eleven."

"Just a little longer. This sample looks different in its crystalline structure."

Min-ho was always like that. Even in failure, he never gave up. When Ji-young was first assigned to his team, Min-ho had already spent two years immersed in battery research. Back then, Japanese companies dominated the mobile phone battery market, and Korean companies' challenges seemed foolhardy.

But Min-ho's eyes burned with a different fire. When he spoke—"One day our batteries will power the world"—his voice carried no hesitation.

Time passed. In 1998, the IMF financial crisis struck Korea. The research institute was swept up in restructuring, and the battery development project faced cancellation. Yet the company decided instead to pour more resources into battery business. Management saw crisis as opportunity.

"Ji-young, if we succeed... could this technology really change the world?"

One late night, Min-ho suddenly asked. Ji-young looked at his face. His eyes bore the weight of exhaustion, yet still shone with determination. In that moment, she realized how deeply she had fallen—not just into this project, but into him.

"Yes, it could. If we're together."

At her words, Min-ho smiled brightly for the first time.

In 2000, the breakthrough came. They discovered a new cathode material composition ratio. Ji-young and Min-ho pulled all-nighters running verification experiments repeatedly. Charge capacity improved by 20% over existing products, and stability was secured. When they confirmed the data, the two embraced. Beyond the laboratory window, the morning sun was rising over Daejeon.

"Congratulations, Min-ho. We did it."

"No, it's thanks to you. I couldn't have gotten here without you."

Late that year, Energychemistry commercialized its independently developed lithium-ion battery. Initially for small mobile phone batteries, the technology kept evolving. The team led by Ji-young and Min-ho expanded into notebook computers and power tools.

In 2009, the two married. They held only a small family gathering instead of a traditional wedding. "No time to waste on a ceremony when there's battery development to do," Min-ho joked, but Ji-young knew his sincerity. For them, research was not merely a profession but a shared dream for the future.

In 2010, Energychemistry fully entered the electric vehicle battery market. Ji-young and Min-ho had become team leader and senior researcher respectively. The "batteries that power the world" they dreamed of twenty years ago was becoming reality.

One evening on the institute's rooftop, Ji-young and Min-ho gazed out over the city of Daejeon. Electric vehicles running through the streets. A significant portion of the batteries powering them were Energychemistry products.

"Do you remember? What you said when we first met?"

"What?"

"That someday our batteries would power the world. It seemed like a distant dream back then."

Min-ho took Ji-young's hand.

"They say dreams are just dreams when you dream alone, but become reality when you dream together. We proved that."

In 2020, Energychemistry spun off its battery division as Energy Power Solutions. Ji-young and Min-ho were nearing retirement. On their final day of work, the two returned to the third-floor laboratory where they first met. Now preserved like a museum, they opened their old experiment notebooks.

"September 15, 1997. Cathode material experiment trial 23—failed."

Ji-young read aloud, and Min-ho laughed.

"Back then we didn't know. That every failure was a step toward success."

"And that we were falling for each other along the way."

The two held hands and gazed out the window. The institute's parking lot was filled with electric vehicles. The future their lifetime of work had created.

Batteries store energy. But for Ji-young and Min-ho, a battery was not merely a storage device. It was a vessel for dreams, soil where love took root, and proof of a life walked together.

As they left the laboratory, Min-ho asked: "Ji-young, if we could go back to 1997..."

Ji-young paused to think, then answered: "I'd choose the same. Both the battery research and you. It was the best decision of my life."

The evening glow painted the Daejeon sky. The two walked side by side, leaving the institute. Behind them, lights flickered on one by one in the laboratories. A new generation of researchers was nurturing yet another dream.
