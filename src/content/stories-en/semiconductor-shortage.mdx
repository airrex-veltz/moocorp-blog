## The Thirst for Chips

There are two kinds of thirst in the world. One comes from the absence of water. The other comes from someone hiding it away. In the autumn of 2021, Yoon Seo-jin learned with her entire body that the latter is far more cruel.

She was the supply chain director of HanTech. Or rather, she had become the organization itself in that role. A supply chain is like a circulatory system. No one thinks about veins during normal times. When blood flows well, the heart barely notices them. But the moment a vein clogs, the entire body becomes aware of its existence all at once.

That morning, Seo-jin's monitor was filled with warning messages.

---

"The supplier has adjusted the allocation again."

Min-jun handed over the report. Seo-jin scanned the numbers. 47 percent of the Q3 planned volume. Less than half. And it had come from DONBAND, their Taiwan fabless partner. The word "notification" itself seemed too generous. It was a single line in an email.

"Did they give a reason?"

"They just said demand surge and process bottleneck. No detailed explanation."

Seo-jin looked out the window. In 2021, the world was experiencing a new pain following the pandemic: a semiconductor shortage. Automakers had halted the production of hundreds of thousands of vehicles because they didn't have a single chip. Sony's PlayStation was impossible to buy even if you waited in line. In the midst of that chaos, HanTech's inventory of the critical MCU-7 series microcontroller was quietly, but rapidly, running dry.

HanTech was a consumer electronics startup. Three years after going public, its ticker **HTK** was a steadily growing mid-cap on the Nasdaq. Its scale was small, but it had carved out a niche in the IoT market for consumer appliances with its proprietary MCU design. The company's entire growth plan for next year rested on the launch of a new product.

The problem was that this new product had MCU-7 as its critical component.

---

The supply crisis had begun six weeks earlier.

At first, it seemed like an industry-wide problem. Beyond HanTech, other fabless partners had begun reducing their allocations in succession. Supply chain forums were flooded with anxious messages, and Seo-jin's phone rang with courtesy calls from competing procurement managers. While everyone shared comfort in knowing they faced the same situation, they also watched each other warily.

But three weeks in, strange signals began arriving.

The industry's second-ranked company, SinGyeong Tech, was moving quietly. Seo-jin first heard the rumor at a dinner with an old business partner. She was passing along what a KresTech sales representative had told her.

"Apparently KresTech is bulk-buying MCU-7 series inventory. They're even adding triple the markup."

Seo-jin set down her chopsticks.

"Since when?"

"Three months ago. They were already moving before the supply crisis officially began."

The weight of those words didn't fully settle until the next morning. Three months earlier was long before the supply bottleneck became public knowledge. Either KresTech had predicted the crisis, or they had created it.

Seo-jin sat alone in the conference room and went through KresTech's supply data again. Numbers never lied. HanTech's allocation cuts were more than twice the industry average. Meanwhile, KresTech's existing contract volumes remained intact. That wasn't all. Rumors were circulating that KresTech and DONBAND were discussing a long-term exclusive supply agreement.

The picture became clear.

KresTech was stockpiling scarce chips. Mid-sized competitors like HanTech naturally lost inventory. Product launches were delayed, investors lost confidence, stock prices wavered. KresTech was preparing, on the basis of ample chip reserves, to monopolize the market when chaos reached its peak.

Whether KresTech's strategy would succeed or fail was a question for later. The problem Seo-jin had to solve right now was different. HanTech's entire supply chain management division had to survive this situation. And the new product launch was 14 weeks away.

---

Seo-jin pulled out her partner list from the beginning.

Beyond DONBAND, there were only a handful of fabless manufacturers worldwide that could produce the MCU-7 series. Two of them had already signed long-term contracts with KresTech. One was Nagano Semiconductor, based in Japan. HanTech had never done business with Nagano. It had been excluded from the candidate list from the start because of its small scale and reputation for unreliable delivery times.

But different criteria were needed now.

Over two days, Seo-jin reduced her sleep and analyzed Nagano's public data. Two years of delivery history, quality assurance documents, technical specifications. Nagano's process used older technology, but they had an alternative chip design that was pin-compatible with MCU-7. The performance difference was about 8 percent. Power consumption was somewhat higher.

"This 8 percent is the problem," technical director Chae Rin said in a meeting. "We guaranteed our new product 18 hours of battery life. With this chip, I think we'd barely exceed 16 hours."

"What if marketing lowered it to 14 hours?"

"That would mean changing the entire product strategy."

"Think about which is worse—changing the whole thing or having no launch at all."

Chae Rin said nothing. Her silence was the answer.

Seo-jin called Nagano's sales representative directly. A man named Okamoto. She pushed ahead in English, without a Japanese interpreter.

"How much inventory do you have?"

"Right now it's limited. But..."

"We're prepared to discuss a long-term contract. Right now."

A brief silence hung. Seo-jin didn't fill it. In negotiation, whoever breaks the silence first is the weaker party.

"I'll hear the terms."

---

The negotiation with Nagano lasted four days.

Okamoto initially demanded a premium. He sent an invoice raising the per-chip price by 35 percent over baseline. Seo-jin countered. She proposed a long-term contract committing to volume in exchange for a 12 percent price increase. She anticipated pushback from the accounting team, but Seo-jin knew this wasn't about minimizing costs. It was about supply security.

The night the negotiation ended, Seo-jin stayed alone in the conference room, reviewing the contract. Beyond the corridor, she could hear her colleagues leaving for the day. Someone turned off the lights. Only the monitor's glow lit her face.

She found herself thinking again about what she was doing right now. When a company's arteries clogged, what did a supply chain manager do? Find a new route. A detour through the circulatory system. That was the reason her team existed. Work that nobody noticed during peacetime. Work that proved its existence only in times of crisis.

Whether KresTech's strategy succeeded or failed was something to be determined later. The role Seo-jin had been given in this moment was just one thing: to sign the contract tonight so HanTech could move tomorrow.

After signing, she hit send on the email.

One new message appeared in her sent folder. Nothing more, nothing less.

---

Fourteen weeks later, the new product launched on schedule.

The battery life was listed as 16.5 hours. The marketing team resisted at first, but eventually created a new slogan: "The power to get through the day." Market reaction was not bad. Perhaps the honest figure of 16.5 hours gained more trust than the original 18 hours would have.

KresTech announced a major expansion of market share in their Q4 earnings report. They had partially succeeded in pressuring competitors through their chip stockpiling strategy. HanTech was not on that list.

**HTK** stock rose 6.3 percent on the product launch day. Seo-jin checked the news alert, then flipped her phone face-down so she couldn't see the screen. Whether the stock went up or down was a result. The supply chain wasn't a structure that made results; it was a structure that maintained the flow so results became possible.

Min-jun knocked on the conference room door with two cups of coffee in his hands.

"Thank you for your hard work."

Seo-jin took the coffee and said,

"The chip shortage isn't over yet."

"I know."

"Then we're good."

Late autumn sunlight filtered obliquely through the window. The veins were flowing again. Quietly, as if it were obvious.
