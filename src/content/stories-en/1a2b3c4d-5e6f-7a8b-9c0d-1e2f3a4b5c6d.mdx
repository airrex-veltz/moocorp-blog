Park's screen was scattered with orange dots again today.

Not red, which signaled an alert. This was the color the system used when satellite pings dropped. The cargo hadn't disappeared—the tracking had simply broken. That distinction was what let him continue doing this work.

11 PM. The office was empty except for him and the security guard.

The left side of his screen displayed the entire Asian shipping route. The Yellow Sea, the South China Sea, the Strait of Malacca. His responsibility covered the East Asia–origin electronics component route. Semiconductor wafers, PCB boards, connectors. Small and light, but high value—delay by a day and an assembly line somewhere stops. So he'd developed a habit of never letting those orange dots out of his sight.

The pattern started changing in autumn.

The disconnections followed a consistent path. The signal would disappear somewhere in Guangdong, then reappear three days later at a northern Vietnamese port. Same container number. Same shipper. But the customs codes at the transit point had changed. New stamps had been applied that weren't there before.

At first he thought it was administrative error. When cargo passed through a different customs window, delayed updates on the codes were common. But when the same shipper's containers accumulated ten, then twenty instances of this, that explanation grew harder to maintain.

Late that year, he dug through an industry report. It was the quarterly shipping exchange compilation of capacity utilization by route. The loaded volume on the Guangdong electronics component route was quietly declining. Meanwhile, the numbers showed that cargo of the same goods originating from Hai Phong and Ho Chi Minh had nearly doubled compared to two years earlier. These were figures that moved before they made headlines. The kind of report no one ever wrote into an article.

Companies make no announcements. No shareholder meetings, no press releases. One day, cargo simply starts appearing from a different port. Same products, same customers, different point of origin. When those patterns accumulate, eventually a single flow becomes visible. Production is relocating. Quietly, without declaration.

When Park tried to include this in his report, his team leader tilted his head in confusion.

"Is this something we should be concerned about? The cargo arrived in the end, didn't it?"

He paused to think. If cargo is delayed, that's our job. If the reason it's delayed is a route change, that's something we need to know. And if the reason the route is changing is a shift in production location—where exactly does that stop being our concern?

"I'm not sure," he said honestly.

That paragraph was cut from the report.

After that, he continued watching the orange dots. The stretches where cargo disconnected shifted slightly with each season. New ports were absorbed into the flow. Old routes quietly diminished. The system recorded it all as 'tracking gaps.' Hours without signal.

Park understood that these gaps actually spoke more clearly about some things. Decisions that went unannounced. Relocations that were never disclosed. But containers don't lie.

When the port where cargo arrives changes, something somewhere in the world is quietly changing too.
