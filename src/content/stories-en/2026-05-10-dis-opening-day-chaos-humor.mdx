"It has to be perfect. Absolutely perfect."

Walt Disney walked through Disneyland as dawn broke at 4 a.m., still shrouded in darkness, muttering to himself. July 17, 1955—today was the opening day of "the Happiest Place on Earth," the dream he had nurtured his entire life.

"Walt, get some rest. Everything's ready," his brother Roy said.

"Ready? Fantasyland's paint still isn't dry, and Main Street's asphalt was just laid two hours ago!"

But ABC had already set up cameras for the live broadcast, and 11,000 invited guests waited outside the gates. The problem was that the actual crowd had swollen to 28,000, thanks to counterfeit ticket holders.

At 10 a.m., the gates opened.

And hell broke loose.

"Ahhh!" Women in high heels screamed. Their heels sank deep into the still-drying asphalt. Some had to remove their shoes and walk barefoot.

In Fantasyland, children who had touched the wet paint were crying, covered head to toe in paint.

"Water! We need water!" the restaurant manager cried out. A plumber's strike had rendered the water fountains nonfunctional. In 43-degree Celsius heat, people were parched. Pepsi Cola flew off the shelves, but it wasn't enough.

"Does it smell like gas?" a father wrinkled his nose.

A gas leak in Fantasyland. The entire section was closed off as an emergency.

The rocket ride in Tomorrowland broke down within two hours. The jungle cruise in Adventureland had water too shallow for the boat, which got stuck on the bottom. The crew member playing captain had to push the boat in front of the passengers.

"Captain, you can do it!" the children giggled.

By 2 p.m., Walt was in the control room, cradling his head in his hands. ABC's live broadcast cameras were transmitting all the chaos nationwide. Even announcer Ronald Reagan (who would later become president) was at a loss for words.

"This is... a historic moment. In a very... unique way."

Restaurants ran out of food. Restroom lines stretched to 100 people deep. Half the attractions were broken. Scuffles broke out with counterfeit ticket holders.

"This is a disaster," Roy sighed.

But something strange happened.

The children were laughing. Parents were taking photos and having fun. The woman whose shoe had sunk into the asphalt held it up like a souvenir and laughed. When families saw the jungle cruise captain pushing the boat, they applauded.

"This is totally an adventure!" one child shouted.

By 6 p.m., Walt stood before the castle, watching families laugh as they took photographs. Despite the heat, broken rides, and inadequate food, they looked happy.

"I understand now," Walt smiled. "It doesn't have to be perfect. People don't want perfection—they want magic."

The next day's newspapers called it a "disaster." But visitors came back. And came again.

And 70 years later, Disneyland remains "the Happiest Place on Earth." Not perfect, or perhaps happy precisely because it was never perfect.
