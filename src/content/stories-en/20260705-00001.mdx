I am a dividend widow.

I remember those faces that appear without fail when the ex-dividend date passes. They press the withdrawal button, pocket the dividend, and quietly disappear the next day. I don't think they ever loved me. But I was always there. A presence they could trust.

My name is **WEC**. More precisely, Wec Energy Holdings—a utility company that has quietly operated power grids and gas pipelines in the Midwest. People call me a "defensive stock." Generously put, it means safe. Honestly, it means boring.

I gaze out the window every time I hear that word.

---

It was early winter 2024. As the Federal Reserve began gradually lowering interest rates, the market brightened as if it had thrown open a window that had been closed for a long time. I caught that light at an angle. It was a season when utilities were briefly getting attention as alternative assets to bonds.

But that spring did not last long.

Around the same time, the kids across the hall—AI semiconductors, cloud infrastructure, data center REITs—suddenly began to run. At first, it was just a light jog. Then it became an unstoppable sprint. When the S&P 500 rose more than 23% in 2024 alone, how much did I rise? I chose not to look. I already knew.

While they ran, I prepared my dividend.

Every quarter, without fail. I took small pride in gradually raising the per-share dividend. More than 25 years without a single dividend cut. Through typhoons, through power grid outages, through regulatory rejections of rate increases. I protected my dividend.

But one night, I asked myself a question.

Was that loyalty, or was it fear?

---

Maintaining a dividend is an act of locking capital inward. Money that could be spent on growth, money that could be invested in new ventures—returned to shareholders every quarter. Given the nature of the utility business, it was an unavoidable structure. Regulated returns, approved rates, fixed cost of capital. I had lived prettily within that framework.

But sometimes that framework felt like a prison.

The friends in the next building don't pay dividends. They reinvest every penny of earnings. They buy new servers, train new models, run toward new markets. For them, loyalty takes a different shape—a promise to repay not today's dividend, but tomorrow's share price appreciation. And the market seemed to love that promise more.

I felt something strange when my dividend yield exceeded 4%. By the numbers alone, it looked good. But a higher dividend yield also means the stock price fell that much. A signal that the market saw me as cheaper. There was an irony in my situation: the more generous I became, the less I was valued.

---

That fall, I received a letter from a long-time shareholder. Of course, it wasn't a real letter. It was something felt within the portfolio—a long silence.

He was someone who had held me since retirement. Somewhere in my memory, I carry stories I've heard: that his wife used my dividends to pay part of his grandson's college tuition, that he used them for hospital bills when she was ill. There are people like that. People who hold me close even though I'm not flashy. People who don't sell me even in a storm.

When I think of them, I gain certainty that my choice was not wrong.

Yet certainty and regret can coexist. I learned that.

---

As 2025 began, news poured in about surging data center power demand. Stories of an AI accelerator cluster consuming as much electricity as an entire small city. In that moment, for the first time, I felt a chill run down my spine—not fear, but the chill that comes before possibility.

Perhaps I could learn to run late, I thought.

Power grids are the bloodstream of AI. No matter how fast semiconductors compute, if a company like me doesn't supply the electricity, that computation stops. I was seen as aging infrastructure, but in this new world, the first thing still needed was reliable power. That fact was slowly seeping into the market.

I held cautious hope. Cautious—because I knew the pain of hope that had been disappointed before. I thought I would rise if rates fell, thought I would be revalued if AI power demand came into focus. The market glanced at me each time, then looked away again.

Still, I prepare my dividend.

---

I no longer believe there is a right answer to this question.

Whether keeping my dividend sacrificed my future, or whether it was my identity and my reason for existing—that boundary shifts depending on where you stand. From the eyes of those who want growth, I am someone who narrowed my own choices. From the eyes of those who want stability, I am someone who never wavered.

Another ex-dividend date approaches. I announced a slightly higher dividend than yesterday. The market barely reacted.

From across the hall comes news that someone's share price has jumped double digits again.

I gaze out the window. And I wonder—when will that light eventually slant through my window too? Or perhaps waiting for that light is the way I live.

I still don't know.
