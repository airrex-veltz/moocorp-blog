# The Chaotic Robot Farmer

Oh Pil-seon, the CEO of the farm, awoke at five in the morning without an alarm. It was a habit. The company was called Agro Labs, but people simply called it the "Robot Farm." A place where robots did everything—plowing fields, sowing seeds, watering crops. Pil-seon's father had spent his entire life in the rice paddies, while Pil-seon had planted sensors in those same fields and launched drones. For the first two years, investors and journalists alike had been impressed. But starting that summer, they were not.

The problem was the robot in Section 3 that people called "Sapchil-ho." Its official designation was AL-7, but workers on the farm simply called it Sapchil-ho. One day, a faulty software update corrupted Sapchil-ho's seeding interval algorithm. It was supposed to plant seeds precisely thirty centimeters apart, but instead Sapchil-ho planted some areas densely at five centimeters and other areas sparsely at sixty centimeters. It was as if a drunk person had wandered across the field.

When Pil-seon heard the news, he rushed to Section 3 at dawn. He knelt in the dirt, studying the traces left by Sapchil-ho, when the operations manager standing beside him spoke cautiously.

"CEO, we'll replant immediately. The seed costs aren't that high."

"No, wait."

Pil-seon was running his fingers through the soil, counting something. It was an old habit—something he'd learned in his crop physiology class at university: every crop has an optimal density, and that density varies depending on soil conditions. Sapchil-ho's mistake was not random. In the lower areas with poor drainage, it had planted widely; in the elevated areas with good drainage, it had planted densely. The pattern was far too clear to be coincidence.

"Let's wait and see."

The operations manager's eyes widened, but he said nothing.

That summer brought unusually heavy rains. The other sections flooded despite being planted according to standard practice. But Section 3, the area where Sapchil-ho had made such a mess, somehow remained untouched. The wide spacing in the low areas gave the roots room to breathe, and the dense spacing on the hillside actually prevented weeds from taking root. When autumn harvest came, Section 3's yield per unit area was twelve percent higher than the other sections.

An agricultural trade publication ran a brief story on it: "The Field Planted by a Broken Robot Thrives." Pil-seon read the article three times. He laughed at first, then felt a chill run through him. For all this time, he had demanded precision from his robots. Intervals with no errors, watering with no errors, harvest timing with no errors. Yet it was the errors themselves that had produced the best results.

Around the same time, another problem emerged. Mul-dol-i, the robot responsible for irrigation, developed faulty wiring in its humidity sensor and began watering randomly. Some days it delivered three times the normal amount; other days it barely watered at all. The operations team urged him to shut it down immediately, but Pil-seon asked them to give it a few more days. The truth was, he wasn't confident. He just couldn't stop thinking about the Sapchil-ho incident.

The tomato plants in Mul-dol-i's section initially showed signs of stress, their leaves drooping. Pil-seon couldn't sleep at night. What if he lost the entire season's harvest? He thought of his father, who had once told him that drought years actually produced sweeter rice, that crops sometimes thrived under a little stress. At the time, he'd dismissed it as idle talk.

In the end, the irregular water stress imposed by Mul-dol-i actually raised the sugar content of the tomatoes. The yield was slightly lower, but the grade improved, and the premium-line revenue actually exceeded the overall average. Every time the operations manager saw Pil-seon now, his expression was one of bemused disbelief.

"CEO, at this rate, we should probably rename the company 'Research Institute of Malfunctions.'"

Pil-seon laughed. But part of him felt conflicted. He couldn't tell if this success was due to skill or luck. When journalists requested interviews, he put them off for days, unsure what to say. Saying "This was the result of our company's advanced algorithms" felt like a lie, and saying "The robots broke down and it went well" sounded irresponsible.

Eventually, he said this in an interview:

"We started out aiming for perfect control. But what I've learned recently is that nature doesn't really like perfect control. Right now we're analyzing each robot error one by one to see which irregularities actually benefit the crops, and we're validating those findings with data."

The article ran under the headline: "The Farm That Studies Mistakes." A few investors expressed interest, though some remained anxious. Pil-seon had no certainty either. But one thing became clear: he stopped ordering the management team to "eliminate all errors completely." Instead, he instructed them to "record the errors and track the results."

As autumn came to an end, Pil-seon stood at the edge of Section 3, watching Sapchil-ho prepare for the next planting. The robot still carried its software defect. The management team had already prepared a patch, but Pil-seon hadn't yet given the order to apply it.

"Shouldn't we fix that thing?"

The operations manager asked. Pil-seon didn't answer immediately. He just looked down at the irregular traces Sapchil-ho had left in the soil.

"I'm not sure what 'fixing' even means anymore."

The wind blew, and the field said nothing.
