The cargo terminal was busiest after midnight.

The porter pushed his hand cart past the conveyor belt at 1 AM, following routes his body had learned over decades. Which zone held which cargo, which door to bypass if an entrance blocked—such knowledge wasn't on any map. His hands and feet knew it.

But that day was different. Along the eastern wall of the terminal, unfamiliar machines stood in a row. They were transportation robots with yellow bodies and wheels. The sensor-studded front pushed pallets slowly toward the distribution area without any human guidance. Collision-avoidance lights blinked. There was almost no sound.

The operations team leader said that once the trial run was complete, this system would handle three zones. He cited numbers: 35 percent faster throughput, significantly reduced cargo damage. These were actual figures from unmanned conveyor systems that major hub airports had been adopting since 2023. His tone was matter-of-fact, and there was no time for questions.

The porter said nothing at the numbers. Most of the people who had long kept the terminal running felt the same. The numbers couldn't be denied. Machines didn't tire, didn't complain, didn't demand night-shift premiums.

The following week, transition training began. The name sounded impressive—Smart Logistics Operations Specialist Course. The content involved watching screens to monitor robot status, reading error codes, and following set procedures to restart them. It was work for the eyes and fingers, not the hands.

Sitting in front of a screen felt strange at first. When his body stayed still, he grew anxious. He wanted to walk the zones, to grip a pallet. Every time his hands—long accustomed to bearing weight—grasped the mouse, they hesitated for a moment.

But after a few days, other things began to reveal themselves. There was a zone where robots frequently stalled. It was the uneven floor. The machines hesitated when surfaces were irregular. The porter knew that zone well. A seam let rainwater seep in, and in winter the floor rose slightly. It was a sensation he'd felt through his shoe soles for years.

He logged the information in the inspection report. The next day, the maintenance team arrived. The floor was repaired. The robots moved normally again. It was a small thing, but something clicked into place.

The things the porter had learned through his body—which way cargo shifted, how the terminal's temperature changed with the weather, the locking characteristics of certain airline containers—all remained useful in monitoring robot operations. They were things machines didn't know.

The night the transition training ended, the porter walked through the cargo zone one more time. The robots moved pallets effortlessly. The terminal was quieter than before. His hand cart sat in the corner.

He felt at once a sense of loss and a sense of still being there. Which feeling was stronger, he couldn't yet say.
