The last time Gu Seong-hwan climbed that steel tower was early in the morning.

On a mountainside near Eumseong in North Chungcheong, at an elevation of 480 meters along the ridge, stood Cheongtap Infrastructure Base Station No. 217. A 38-meter-high lattice steel structure. Sixteen years had passed since the concrete foundation was laid, and that same year Seong-hwan had joined the company. He adjusted his hard hat and placed his foot on the first rung of the ladder. Behind him, dawn mist filled the valleys.

At the top, the view opened wide. To the east, warehouse lights glowed through the water vapor. To the southwest, another telecom company's relay tower flickered with a red warning light. As he inspected the antenna mounting bolts, Seong-hwan gazed long at the distant tower. Eventually that will be Daelyun Communications', he thought. Or else ours becomes theirs.

That afternoon, an emergency board meeting was called at the Seoul headquarters.

---

Cheongtap Infrastructure had been formed in 2009 when three small tower operating companies merged. It owned 4,800 steel towers and rooftop base stations across the nation, with 340 employees. It was not a large company. Compared to major telecom operators like Daelyun Communications or Hanbyeol Networks, it was pocket change. Yet coverage in the mountainous regions of Chungcheong, Gangwon-do, and Gyeongbuk was impossible without Cheongtap. If a hole opened in the mountains, even disaster text alerts and emergency response became unreliable.

Seong-hwan had taken the position of CEO since 2019. After the previous CEO suffered a sudden stroke, the board had promoted him from within. Until then he had been the facilities team leader. He didn't know finance, and M&A even less. But he knew steel towers. Which sites had icing problems in winter, which ridges drew the most lightning, which landlords tried to raise land prices at renewal. That knowledge had sustained him for five years.

The boardroom was on the 19th floor of the headquarters on Eulji-ro. Seven people sat at the long table, with two more connected by video. Seong-hwan sat at the far end, watching the slide that CFO Park Ho-yeon presented. The numbers were not unfamiliar. He had stared at the same figures every night for the past four days.

"Daelyun's proposed price is KRW 24,000 per share," Park Ho-yeon said, her voice deliberately flat. "That's approximately a 38 percent premium over the current stock price."

No one spoke immediately.

Board Chairman Song Dae-jin took a sip of water. He was the only surviving founding member. Seventy-one years old. Seong-hwan had been told on his first day that Song had personally supervised the foundation work for Eumseong's first steel tower.

"Is 38 percent a good premium, CEO Gu?"

"Based on operating profit from the past five years, the EV/EBITDA is 16x," Seong-hwan replied. "The U.S. tower market averages over 25x, Southeast Asia between 18 and 20. We're getting a discount."

"Then it means we could get more?"

"Or it means we don't have to sell."

Silence came again.

---

Daelyun Communications had sent its letter of intent three weeks prior. But warning signs had appeared six months earlier. As telecom industry reorganization accelerated, large operators were pushing to consolidate tower assets and spin them off as separate entities. The logic was that they would own the infrastructure while specializing in operations. The reality was calculation: improve financial ratios on the balance sheet by shedding fixed assets while simultaneously controlling network access for small independent operators.

Hanbyeol Networks moved first. It spun off its 2,200 towers into a subsidiary called Hanbyeol Tower and absorbed three small operators within six months. One of them was Saebyeok Tower, which had long maintained a joint maintenance contract with Cheongtap. Early that month, a message came from Saebyeok Tower CEO Choi Jae-yong. It was brief. "We had no choice. I'm sorry."

Seong-hwan had not deleted that message.

After the board meeting ended, he pulled Park Ho-yeon aside.

"What defense options is the legal team talking about?"

Park Ho-yeon opened her file. "Three. One, find a white knight. Two, revalue assets to make the premium meaningless. Three, conclude a strategic partnership first to make us unattractive."

"A white knight."

"There's no one domestically willing to step in. Hanbyeol is already on the other side, and the rest of the telecom companies are watching. A foreign infrastructure fund could show interest, but—"

"Then it's just a change of ownership either way."

Park Ho-yeon paused, then said, "Yes, essentially."

---

Acquisition pressure crystallized with the second approach.

It was Thursday morning when Kim Tae-won, head of business strategy at Daelyun Communications, came in person. Two people sat alone in a conference room that could fit twenty. Kim Tae-won was a man in his mid-forties. He wore no tie, and his speech was fast and precise.

"CEO Gu, I don't want confrontation," he began. "I'm proposing we look at industry reality together."

"I'm listening."

"When 5G advancement accelerates, the density of small base stations will increase more than threefold. Urban periphery, mountains, highway corridors—the areas where Cheongtap is strong. To maintain and expand that, investment needs would exceed double the current scale. Can you do that alone?"

Seong-hwan did not respond.

"We don't want Cheongtap's network. We want Cheongtap's locations. Four thousand eight hundred steel towers embedded in mountains. We're buying that," Kim Tae-won paused. "At a fair price."

"The CFO told me that fair price is a discounted price."

Kim Tae-won smiled. It was unforced, the familiar expression of a seasoned negotiator. "There's room to negotiate."

After the meeting ended, Seong-hwan walked out of the building. Eulji-ro was crowded with the lunch-hour rush. He stood in front of a convenience store across the street and drank bottled water. A major telecom company's sign hung a block away. Their competitors' products filled more of this city's streets than his company's did.

When he had joined the company, building a single tower took an average of forty days. Site acquisition, design approval, foundation work, tower assembly, equipment delivery, telecom testing. Now it could be done in as few as twenty-five days. In the time that period had halved, how much the world had changed. And now it was approaching another kind of ending.

---

The seed of resistance came from an unexpected place.

Ten days after the letter of intent was received, Lim Sun-chul, a field technician from Cheongtap Infrastructure's facilities team, sent a text. Sun-chul was the Gangwon-do branch manager and a fifteen-year tower specialist.

"CEO, went out for inspection at Hongcheon today and saw something odd. A Daelyun vehicle passed near our site twice. Carrying survey equipment."

Seong-hwan called immediately.

"Which site?"

"Between No. 209 and No. 213 on the ridge. There's nowhere suitable for a new base station, but they were surveying. It's the spot where a coverage gap would appear if our tower weren't there."

Seong-hwan thought for a moment. "Take photos."

After hanging up, he called legal team leader Jo Min-ah.

"Check our disclosure obligations. If competitive activity occurs within the target company's business scope after receipt of the letter of intent, are there Fair Trade Act implications?"

Jo Min-ah raised her eyebrows. "If the other party is trying to preempt alternative infrastructure during the acquisition—it could become an abuse of dominance issue. However, since the activity is occurring before the acquisition is complete, there needs to be evidence that can be viewed as 'prior coordination.'"

"Let's create the evidence."

That night, Seong-hwan asked Sun-chul to assign two more team members. The task was to photograph conditions and coordinates at twelve key Gangwon-do sites and record Daelyun's movements. It was not an official complaint or lawsuit. It was just a record.

---

Fourteen days later, a document was filed with the Fair Trade Commission.

It was an opinion paper prepared by Cheongtap Infrastructure's legal team on changes in the competitive market environment. The content was simple. If two large telecom operators acquire independent tower operators, approximately 68 percent of base station infrastructure in the Chungcheong, Gangwon-do, and Gyeongbuk mountainous regions would come under a single affiliate system. And there was risk that access costs for independent operators to shared towers would be set unreasonably due to negotiating power imbalance. Attached were photographs and coordinate files from Sun-chul's team.

Fair Trade Commission review typically took two months. Two months seemed like nothing, but at a negotiating table, two months meant something else. It meant the other side had to wait. That alone changed the weight.

Seong-hwan and Park Ho-yeon began working with numbers in a different direction this time. An asset revaluation report. The book value of 4,800 towers was based on 2009 acquisition cost. But if land leasehold rights, network location value, and increased rental revenue from 5G transition were factored in, the value changed. He commissioned two external valuation firms. The results had not yet come in.

In the meantime, daily work continued.

Sun-chul made the rounds at sites every morning. He repaired foundation settlement damage at No. 228 caused by heavy rain, negotiated with the landlord at the Hongcheon site who demanded a 50 percent land price increase. He placed purchase orders for six base stations where equipment replacement was due. Regardless of what happened with the acquisition, the towers had to stand.

A call came from a representative of a small MVNO customer of Cheongtap Infrastructure. If Cheongtap was acquired by a large tower company, would their rates increase? Seong-hwan replied that there were no changes for now. He himself couldn't fully say how much truth that held.

---

The final confrontation came at the third board meeting.

Daelyun Communications issued a public statement. It raised acquisition terms to KRW 27,000 per share, pledged to maintain Cheongtap Infrastructure's independent brand for three years, and guaranteed employment for all current employees. It included a timeline stating that the transaction would be completed by the first half of next year at the earliest, taking into account the ongoing Fair Trade Commission review.

KRW 27,000. A 56 percent premium over the previous stock price.

The boardroom session began at 9 a.m. and ran past 4 p.m. Seong-hwan said nothing for the first hour. Numbers circulated around the table. A finance director presented cash flow analysis, two outside directors each argued for acceptance and rejection with different logic. Someone raised again the possibility of negotiating with foreign infrastructure funds. Someone said the company would spend a year in uncertainty if Fair Trade Commission review extended.

Chairman Song looked at Seong-hwan. "CEO Gu, what do you think?"

Seong-hwan had no prepared words. Well, he had words, but he wasn't sure they were the right ones.

"I asked the people here," he said slowly. "Sun-chul's team is at sites in Gangwon-do, the Gyeongbuk manager is on a business trip in Andong, the Chungnam team went out for three inspections today. They all went to fix their towers. Regardless of what happens with the acquisition. Whether those people can do the same work tomorrow and the day after matters to me."

The room fell quiet.

"The employment guarantee Daelyun offered is for three years. After three years there will be workforce integration adjustments. Maintenance personnel are the first priority for outsourcing. That's not in the negotiation terms." He paused. "And if the 68 percent concentration becomes real, small telecom companies will have to negotiate access costs on unfavorable terms every year. No one can guarantee what impact that will have on our mountainous region coverage."

One outside director interjected. "CEO Gu, what does that have to do with shareholder interest? At 56 percent premium—"

"It's not that I don't understand shareholder interest," Seong-hwan cut him off. "It's that it might not be the only criterion for judgment. The external valuation report comes this week. Let's talk again after we see those numbers."

The chairman raised his hand. "Let's meet again in a week."

---

The report came five days later.

Both valuation firms' results matched. Reflecting 5G advancement scenarios, Cheongtap Infrastructure's fair value lay between KRW 31,000 and KRW 33,000 per share. The KRW 27,000 that Daelyun proposed was lower even than the bottom of that range.

Seong-hwan sent that number to Kim Tae-won. A single text line.

"Our valuation result. For your reference."

Two hours later came the reply. "Will review."

What conclusion that review would reach was still uncertain. Fair Trade Commission review was ongoing, and preliminary contacts with foreign infrastructure funds were quietly continuing. A board meeting date had been set, but what decision would come that day remained open.

Sun-chul sent a photo that evening. Base Station No. 217 on the Hongcheon ridge. Evening light touched the antenna tip. Clouds spread below, mountains overlapping in the distance. It was a routine inspection report. No equipment anomalies, signal normal, the message said.

Seong-hwan gazed at the photo for a long time.

The tower still stood. Whoever its owner became, the signal still flowed from above it. People's voices, disaster alerts, location data. Crossing mountains and spanning valleys. As long as that flow didn't stop, whoever won, that work would continue.

But the question remained: who would guard it?

---

A month later, the Fair Trade Commission announced the launch of a fact-finding investigation into the market for independent tower operators, including Cheongtap Infrastructure. The investigation period was specified as three months. Daelyun Communications issued no official statement.

Seong-hwan was confirming that announcement on his mobile phone. He was at the foundation repair site for Eumseong's Base Station No. 217. Sun-chul and two work crew members were filling a concrete crack. It was a small crack but couldn't be left alone.

He signed off on the construction status board. Inspection complete. Date: October 2026.

Wind descended from the mountain. The steel tower swayed microscopically. Within design tolerance. Within normal range.
