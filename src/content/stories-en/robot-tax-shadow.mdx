Lee Dong-ha looked down at the factory floor in the early dawn.

Beyond the third-floor glass window, welding robot arms moved without pause. Sparks flew, and the sound of metal striking carried faintly through the soundproof glass. The empty factory looked oddly beautiful, and that beauty made him uneasy.

His company, Chungkwang Machinery, had passed a three-stage automation transition plan through its board four days ago. Total investment of KRW 84 billion. The plan included phased workforce reductions of 42 percent among production-line workers over the next eighteen months. Lee Dong-ha had drafted it himself, presented it himself, and cast his vote in favor.

The numbers were clear. According to data released last year by the Global Robot Association, South Korea's manufacturing sector had already exceeded the world's highest robot density at more than one robot per 10,000 workers. With competitors showing no signs of stopping, Chungkwang standing still was not an option. At least, that was how he had explained it.

---

A few days later, the National Assembly's Industrial Committee released a draft proposal for a robot tax. The content was straightforward: impose an annual special tax of KRW 1.5 million per worker displaced by automation equipment, with the revenue directed toward retraining funds.

Lee Dong-ha read the article while having coffee. He set down his cup and read it again.

If the law passed, Chungkwang Machinery would have to bear billions of won in additional costs each year. The entire plan approved by the board came back as a cost calculation. His first thought was that the money was wasteful, and immediately after came shame at that thought.

He stared at the screen without closing it. The term "robot tax" was not unfamiliar. It had come up years ago in policy discussions in several European countries. The industry response at the time had been uniform: it would undermine corporate competitiveness. Lee Dong-ha had thought the same way then. Half of him still did.

But half remained.

---

A week later, the roster of the first team scheduled to receive layoff notices arrived from the human resources department. Lee Dong-ha opened the file and closed it. He opened it again and read line by line. Gang Man-soo, a 23-year welding technician. Oh Hee-jin, night shift foreman. Park Jun-hyeok, a new employee who had been assigned to the Sosa factory earlier this year.

He realized this was what the process looked like when names turned into numbers.

He looked out the window. The factory lights did not go out even at dawn. The robots moved with precision in the places where Man-soo had worked, where Hee-jin had stood. Without rest, without complaint.

That precision, that silence, did not bring him comfort.

That night, Lee Dong-ha printed the full text of the robot tax draft and placed it on his desk. He needed to write a statement of opposition. An association of business executives had asked for his input. The logic was already in his mind: automation is national competitiveness, taxes slow innovation, and funds can be secured through other means.

That logic was not wrong. But placed alongside the roster file on his desk, it felt somehow incomplete.

He opened a blank document and typed his first sentence, then deleted it. He typed again and deleted again. On the third attempt, his fingers stopped above the keyboard.

Factory light reflected off the window. Shadows moved. At that height, he could not tell whether they were the shadows of robots or of people. Lee Dong-ha stared at that boundary for a long time. He was not trying to find an answer. He simply wanted to know, with precision, where that boundary lay.
