The Blue Anchor Shipping headquarters 17th-floor conference room has no windows. The wall overlooking Busan's harbor was claimed by the executive lounge. So employees of a company dealing in the sea worked without seeing it.

Da-on, three weeks into the job, found this odd from the start. But week three isn't the time to say odd things are odd. After coffee runs came minute-taking.

That day's agenda was "Finding Auxiliary Revenue Streams for Second Half." It was after 2021's sky-high freight rates, and after the days when one giant ship blocked the Suez Canal and the world waited for containers. Rates had settled back down, and executive expressions had settled even lower.

"Ideas, people. Ideas." Director Ma said. He was the type to slip "for now" three times in one sentence. "For now, just speak. For now, anything. For now, you can be wrong."

No one spoke. That room gathered exactly the people who most couldn't be wrong when told they could be.

Da-on was typing minutes when he absently looked up. A photo on the opposite wall caught his eye. Tens of thousands of containers stacked red, blue, yellow in a port yard. His mouth moved first.

"Isn't that like a billboard?"

The conference room air froze. Director Ma turned slowly.

"What did you just say, O... Da-on?"

"Oh, no. Just, the container side is so wide. It's 12 meters long and over 2 meters high, and it's just steel plate sitting unused... so, I just meant, that's wasteful."

He finished and regretted immediately. Regret always arrives late.

Silence continued. Then Managing Director Kwak at the end raised his glasses. People said he dozed with closed eyes in meetings, but he claimed he was thinking deeply.

"Wasteful," Kwak said with eyes still closed. "Wasteful means it's an asset."

"Director?"

"Unused area. It's an asset. How much volume do we handle annually?"

"Millions of TEU," someone answered.

"Each one has two sides. No, four sides. The top, the birds will see it. Anyway, these are all moving billboards, right?" Kwak opened his eyes. His gaze was strangely clear. "Moving billboards. Draw this up as a document."

That's how Da-on's slip of the tongue became "Task Force Formation."

The task force was called the "Container Commercialization Task Force." Naming it took four days. Candidates included "Golden Side Project," "Opportunity on a Box," and "Blue Ocean 2.0." The last failed for being too obvious for a company already dealing in seas.

The first hurdle was a question Da-on carefully raised.

"But when containers are loaded on ships, don't they stack? Only the outside shows."

"How many layers?" Director Ma asked.

"Large ships stack over 10 rows high, about 20 rows wide."

"So what percentage shows?"

"Only the outside row, so maybe 5%... and you'd have to look from the sea, but who's in the sea? Seagulls?"

Director Ma wrote quietly. "For now, seagull-targeted advertising. For now, let's keep the possibility open."

The second meeting produced market research. The ad reach over water was nearly zero. Yet the report stated: "An early awareness-building stage, with infinite convergence of potential reach."

Kwak read that sentence aloud twice, then said, "Infinite. Good."

Da-on almost raised his hand. He wanted to say zero and infinity aren't the same thing. But his senior colleague grabbed his sleeve and shook his head. His senior's eyes said: in this company, numbers aren't facts—they're mood.

The third meeting divided directions. Sales argued for port yards—people, truck drivers, crane operators. PR opposed. What do we gain from chicken ads to crane operators? Sales countered: crane operators eat chicken after work.

The meeting ran over two hours. The conclusion was "review both options." Da-on wrote in the minutes: "Disagreement on chicken advertisement effectiveness. Crane operators' post-work dining habits unconfirmed." Director Ma saw this and said "excellent observation," and Da-on couldn't know what effect that would have on his evaluation.

Around then, office rumors spread: Blue Anchor would make hundreds of billions from container ads. The rumor's source was unknown, but once circulated, it couldn't be taken back. Finance quietly added "media revenue" to next year's budget. The line was blank, but the fact the line existed mattered. Once a line exists, people believe they must fill it.

A month later, things turned odd. Da-on, a mere trainee, had become "project originator." An unfamiliar executive in the hallway tapped his shoulder. "You're the box guy, right? Amazing. We never imagined it."

He started to say "No, that was just me..." but the executive was already in the elevator.

Reporting day came. The main conference room—this time with windows. The first slide showed a container photo overlaid with a slogan: "Your Brand Crossing the Seas."

But Managing Director Kwak asked a question. For the first time in a month, with open eyes.

"But who buys these ads?"

Everyone in the room looked at each other. The question was simple, and no one had prepared an answer.

"The advertiser pool—sales will..." "I thought PR..." "We only calculated media value..."

Director Ma wiped sweat. "Well, Director, for now, this part is for phase two..."

Then Da-on in the back spoke softly. He hadn't planned to speak, but again his mouth moved first.

"Actually, there is one thing. Yesterday a container fell off the dock, and for three days they can't find its owner. No one knows what's in it or who it belongs to."

"What's that got to do with ads?" Kwak asked.

"If there were words on the container side saying who it belongs to, they'd have found it fast."

A brief silence. Kwak nodded slowly.

"So what you're saying is, if you write on containers, it has purpose."

"...There's already writing. Numbers and the shipping line's name."

"Really? Then isn't that already advertising?"

Da-on opened his mouth, then closed it. For a month it seemed he'd been waiting for this sentence. And it was a surprisingly excellent conclusion. Every container side already had writing clearly marked, and Blue Anchor had been advertising its name in ports worldwide for decades.

"Conclusion reached," Kwak said, standing. "We've been in the media business all along. We just haven't monetized it. Director Ma, this project goes to phase two. Name it 'Monetization Task Force.'"

"Yes, Director. For now, understood."

The meeting ended and people filed out. Da-on alone went to the window. Busan's harbor appeared. Far away, a large container ship slowly entered port. The stacked boxes on its hull gleamed red and blue in sunlight. Somewhere on its side, the words "Blue Anchor" existed.

Who sees those words? Seagulls? Crane operators? Or has no one yet?

Behind him, Director Ma's voice came. "Da-on, for now, start the phase two minutes. For now, today if possible."

The ship continued slowly entering.
