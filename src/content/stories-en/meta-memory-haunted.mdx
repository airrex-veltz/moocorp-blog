After my brother died, I turned off notifications for a long time.

It was because of **META** app's 'Today's Memories' feature. People called it a gift of remembrance. To me, it was a minefield. October 2018, six o'clock in the evening, a photo with my brother at a café. Spring 2019, a short joke he left in the comments. Summer 2021, a video of my brother's last birthday party—in December that year, he disappeared.

I didn't delete the app. I didn't deactivate my account either. Maybe that was my first mistake. But back then, deactivating felt like killing my brother twice. His profile still existed, and META had converted it into a 'memorial account.' His cover photo hadn't changed. The last song he shared was still stuck on his playlist. I sometimes opened that page. I said nothing and just closed the window.

Three years passed that way.

---

In spring 2025, META made an announcement.

The press release was titled 「Strengthening Digital Legacy Protection: Guidance on Permanent Archive Transfer Policy for Memorial Accounts」. The point was simple. Photos, videos, and message data stored in inactive memorial accounts would be transferred to META's new server architecture, and during this process, an AI-based 'Memory Restoration' feature would be pilot-tested. The feature was called 'Memory Stream.' The company described it as 'a service to preserve loved ones' memories more vividly.'

I read the press release three times. The more I read, the more my stomach twisted.

The legal basis was already in Terms of Service Section 47, Subsection 8. 'If a user is converted to a memorial account status after death, META may utilize the data stored in that account for the purpose of improving platform services, and this is included in the data usage terms to which the user consented during their lifetime.' My brother had agreed to those terms in 2014. There's no way my brother read that item. I didn't either.

Reactions in the community were mixed. One side said, 'If I could hear the voice of a dead person again, I'd give everything,' and the other said, 'This is imprisoning a corpse on a corporate server.' Testimonies from bereaved families poured out on online communities. A woman from the United States wrote that after receiving a voice file reconstructed by AI from her husband's voicemail, she hadn't left her bed for three days. A man from Japan said he vomited after reading sentences generated by a bot that had learned his mother's texting style. But a woman from Seoul wrote gratefully that she could now re-edit 200 hours of footage of her late son. I saved all three posts.

META released a brief official statement. 'We prioritize the opinions of bereaved families. Memory Stream is optional, and can be disabled at any time if you wish.' The disable link was five menu steps deep. Most people couldn't find it.

---

That day I opened the app without thinking.

There was a new icon on my brother's memorial account page. A small blue flame shape. 'Memory Stream activated.' I tapped that icon.

Playback began.

A café in October 2018. A window seat. My brother sat with an espresso in front of him. The camera angle was exactly as I remembered it. The white crewneck he used to wear. A small stain on the right sleeve. My brother had spilled ice cream that day. I remembered that stain. META remembered it too.

My brother in the screen looked up.

I held my breath.

It wasn't video. It was a 3D reconstruction based on a photograph. His face didn't move naturally. But the angle of his gaze toward the camera, the ratio of the smile at his lips, the depth of the wrinkles at his eyes—they were all him. Or rather, his data. I couldn't tell if those two things were the same or different.

Background café sounds came from the speakers. The sound of a coffee machine. The low murmur of people talking. The noise of cars passing outside. META had recreated the acoustic environment based on that day's location data and time of day. My brother had location sharing turned on that day. Of course, with Terms of Service agreement.

I put the phone down on the table.

My hands trembled slightly.

---

A middle-aged woman sitting at the next table glanced at me. I pretended to look out the window at nothing. That woman also held a phone. I could see a similar blue flame icon on her screen. We didn't say anything to each other. She must have known that there were people like us in that café too. We didn't voice that commonality.

That was the most bleak aspect of this service now. That there was reason enough to use it. Billions of memorial accounts. Billions of bereaved families. Data held by the platform accumulates even beyond death. I'd read in a META research report that, as of 2021, the company estimated that approximately 30 million users die annually worldwide. That number was growing every year. There will come a time when the accounts of dead people on the platform outnumber the accounts of living people. That time may have already come in some corner.

My brother's data was in that statistic.

---

Memory Stream moved to the next scene.

My brother lifted the coffee cup. The motion of bringing it to his lips. Watching that motion, I remembered that whenever my brother drank coffee, he always held the cup with both hands. The brother on screen held the cup with one hand. It was a very small difference. But that difference felt like a huge crack to me. This wasn't my brother. This was an approximation combined by data about my brother.

But the face was my brother's. The voice wasn't there yet, but if META learned my brother's voicemails and voices in videos to recreate his voice—what would I feel then? And could I refuse it? Or am I afraid precisely because I already know I won't be able to refuse?

Playback continued. The light in the café tilted toward afternoon. My brother gazed out the window. The reconstructed gaze was directed somewhere far away. Where that gaze was looking wasn't in the data. It was the direction META had guessed.

I didn't turn off the screen.

---

Beyond the window was an ordinary street. People passed by. Each living in their own time. Some of them, like me, probably carried a small screen somewhere in a bag, one containing someone's data.

Memory is always incomplete. I might be misremembering my brother's habits. Some of the details I'm confident about might already be versions edited by my brain long ago. Perhaps META's reconstruction is more accurate in some parts than my memories. A face down to the pixel, coordinates recorded by location logs, expressions stamped with timestamps. Should I trust those? Or should I trust my sense that their very precision makes my brother feel unfamiliar?

The brother on screen closed his eyes for a moment. It looked like the face of someone thinking nothing.

That was what resembled him most. A moment of nothing.

Looking at that scene, I thought again about what forgetting means. Forgetting used to be a loss long ago. But in an era where everything is saved, to forget—perhaps it becomes a choice. Or a luxury. Or something that becomes possible only within the scope that META allows.

The delete button was five menu steps deep in settings.

I didn't close the app.

His reconstructed face was breathing on the screen. Whether it was respiration or data vibration, I couldn't tell. And to be honest—I didn't want to know now.

The afternoon outside the window slowly faded.

A few days later, I went back to that café. It was a café that actually existed. I wanted to check how the light came through at that time of day, during that season. No, that's a lie. I simply wanted to go there. Where my brother had been.

The window seat was empty. I sat in a different spot. I ordered coffee. I looked out the window. I sat like that for hours. Other customers came and went. Someone picked up a smartphone and put it down. The woman at the next table was looking at something on her screen. I couldn't tell if that blue flame icon was visible—the distance was too far.

That night I accessed Horizon again.

My brother's Memory Stream was still playing. I didn't turn it off. Instead, I set it to infinite loop. October 2018, the café. My brother drinking espresso. The small error in the motion of lifting the cup—the moment it should have been both hands, not one. Or I've misremembered it.

I still don't know which is the wiser choice: trusting META's data or trusting my memory. But perhaps 'choice' itself is no longer a meaningful word. The boundary between choice and compulsion has blurred.

I didn't turn off the screen. His face repeated the loop. Endlessly.

That's how another night began.
