## Premium Surges and Coverage Reductions Creating Polarization

As insurance premiums rise and coverage narrows, large corporations are leaving the insurance market. Instead of relying on external insurers, they are increasingly shifting toward self-insurance—managing risk directly through their own capital. This trend reflects a clear pivot away from traditional insurance. The shift is significantly restructuring the insurance industry's revenue model, with ripple effects appearing in the form of soaring operating costs for small and medium-sized enterprises.

## The Economics of Self-Insurance

Self-insurance is a practice where companies set aside funds to cover potential losses instead of paying premiums to insurers. For large corporations with sufficient capital and accurate risk assessment capabilities, this approach can cost less than external insurance. This is especially true for ultra-high-risk business segments that insurers refuse to cover or sectors where premiums have risen abnormally.

The problem facing the insurance industry is straightforward. As insurers' profitability deteriorated due to reserve depletion, investment losses from rate increases, and rising claims from major disasters, they began tightening underwriting standards, raising premiums, and expanding exclusion clauses. While a rational response, this creates options for large corporate clients.

## Four Drivers of Self-Insurance Expansion

**1. Sharp Premium Increases**: To cover losses, insurers are raising premiums steeply. The increases are particularly large in major categories such as workers' compensation insurance, liability insurance, and property insurance. Large corporations have begun concluding that covering premium increases through their own reserves is more economical.

**2. Systematic Reduction in Coverage**: Insurers are either excluding high-risk customer segments or inserting broad exclusion clauses. As coverage narrows, the practical value of insurance declines, reducing the rationale for paying premiums. Large corporations increasingly conclude that bearing risk entirely themselves is preferable.

**3. Large Corporations' Capital and Selection Capability**: Large enterprises possess data that allows them to precisely measure risk event patterns and have sufficient capital to absorb losses when they occur. If they can calculate risk more accurately than insurers, eliminating the insurer's margin and managing directly is rational.

**4. Insurance Industry Crisis and the Vicious Cycle of Selection**: The more insurers select customers, the more large corporations exit, leaving only high-risk small and medium-sized enterprises. A vicious cycle begins in which these companies face higher premiums and reduced coverage.

## Structural Polarization: Insurance Industry Weakness and SME Crisis

In this structure, small and medium-sized enterprises are the biggest losers. When large corporations exit, insurers must extract profitability from their remaining customer base. Premiums rise further and coverage narrows further. Small and medium-sized enterprises either bear steep premiums while remaining exposed to uninsured risks, or abandon insurance entirely. Either way, operating costs surge or unanticipated loss risks multiply.

From the insurance industry's perspective, this is also bad news. If the remaining customers are high-risk only, loss ratios rise, creating additional pressure for further increases. With large corporations gone, economies of scale disappear. Ultimately, the insurance industry is likely to be restructured around premium customers—specific elite firms.

## Summary

While premium surges and coverage reductions stem from the insurance industry's crisis, they drive large corporations' exit to self-insurance, which in turn creates operating pressure for small and medium-sized enterprises. This is the process by which financial market polarization transmits to the real economy.
