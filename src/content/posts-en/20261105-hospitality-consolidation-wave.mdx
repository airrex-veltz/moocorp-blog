## Introduction

The ownership structure of tourism and cultural facilities is changing rapidly. It is increasingly common for individual operators to be acquired or come under large fund management, and this is an industry realignment triggered by a sharp shift in the financial environment.

---

## The End of the Low-Interest Era and Capital Structure Rebalancing

For the past decade, the low-interest environment was a core asset for private hospitality and cultural facility operators. They could borrow at low interest rates and use that capital to maintain or expand their facilities. Individual small and mid-sized operators also had relatively high access to credit and could sustain high debt-to-equity ratios.

However, as interest rates began to rise, the situation reversed. Existing low-interest debt must be repaid, and the cost of raising new capital has increased significantly. In particular, tourism and cultural facilities with irregular operating cash flows face a structure where financing burden can surge with sales volatility.

---

## Why Is the M&A Wave Coming Now: Four Factors

**1. Rising Financing Costs and Credit Access Constraints for Individual Operators**

When small operators borrow from banks, small size means higher interest rates and stricter collateral requirements. In a rising rate environment, refinancing existing loans or raising new capital requires accepting very expensive terms. The smaller the scale, the less favorable the creditworthiness assessment, and banks are reluctant to lend to small, high-risk customers.

**2. Deteriorating Operating Cash Flow and Rising Interest Burden**

Tourism and cultural facilities are highly seasonal and sensitive to external shocks such as pandemics and economic cycles. In the low-interest era, even temporary deficits could be weathered with low interest costs. But as rates rise, the same amount of debt becomes a much heavier burden. In businesses with high monthly and seasonal volatility, rising interest costs significantly shake operational stability.

**3. Large Funds' Capital Strength and Borrowing Advantage**

Large private equity and infrastructure funds can borrow on much more favorable terms from banks due to their scale. They have strong creditworthiness, can raise large amounts of capital at once, and have greater negotiating power with multiple financial institutions. Moreover, they have sufficient capital of their own to create an optimized structure, funding part with equity and part with debt.

**4. Pursuing Economies of Scale and Operational Efficiency**

Operating multiple facilities as a single portfolio increases purchasing power and allows sharing of labor, marketing, and technology resources compared to running individual properties. Funds calculate that by consolidating acquired facilities under integrated management, they can reduce costs and improve profitability.

---

## Structural Strengths and Weaknesses: Funds vs. Individual Operators

**Funds' Strengths**

- Lower capital costs: borrowing at low rates due to scale and creditworthiness
- Operational synergies: resource sharing among facilities within the portfolio
- Financial flexibility: ability to adjust the optimal mix of equity and debt
- Risk diversification: offsetting underperformance of one facility with other assets

**Individual Operators' Weaknesses**

- High borrowing costs: low creditworthiness, unfavorable interest rate terms
- High fixed costs: smaller scale means higher per-unit operating costs
- Vulnerability to shocks: direct exposure to business cycles and seasonality
- Expansion constraints: difficulty raising capital for new investments

**Individual Operators' Remaining Competitive Advantage**

However, individual operators are not entirely at a disadvantage. Local affinity, unique brands, and close customer relationships are not easily replicated by large funds. The smaller the facility, the more distinctive operations can serve as a differentiation factor.

---

## Summary

In the rising rate environment, the capital cost gap between individual operators and large funds has been maximized. This gap becomes the key driver of M&A activity. In the low-interest era, even small businesses could compete on financing; in the high-rate era, scale becomes a condition for survival. If this trend continues, the ownership structure of the tourism and cultural industries will likely become increasingly concentrated.
