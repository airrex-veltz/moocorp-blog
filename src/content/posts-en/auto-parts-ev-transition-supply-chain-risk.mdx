## Why EV Transition Slowdown Threatens Automotive Suppliers' Survival

Global automakers are slowing their transition to battery electric vehicles (BEVs) faster than initially anticipated. This is not simply a market adjustment but is fundamentally shaking the microstructure of the automotive parts industry. Korean material and component manufacturers are particularly vulnerable to this shock.

## The Structure of EV Transition Slowdown

Automakers' EV transition slowdown is a clear market signal. Demand growth that had exceeded initial expectations has slowed, and inadequate infrastructure combined with weakened consumer purchasing power have all played a role. The bigger problem is that **this time extension does not delay the paradigm shift in the market**. Automakers have not abandoned their EV transition targets; they have only pushed back the timeline by 2-3 years.

This delay itself is the worst-case scenario for parts suppliers. Because suppliers have invested or are investing in new platforms (battery-based design), if demand for existing internal combustion engine parts persists longer than expected, they must **hold both legacy and new assets simultaneously**. This creates a vicious cycle of lower utilization rates while fixed costs remain unchanged.

## Why Suppliers Are Undergoing Restructuring Now: Four Factors

**1. Dramatic Decrease in Parts Count for Battery Electric Vehicles**

Internal combustion engine vehicles consist of more than 2,000 parts. Electric vehicles rely on a simpler powertrain system of battery and motor, resulting in a dramatic reduction in the types and number of parts. Electric motors have lower complexity, and entire auxiliary systems such as engine intake and exhaust and oil cooling disappear. This **eliminates entire businesses of specific supplier tiers**, such as gearbox component makers, fuel system component suppliers, and catalytic converter manufacturers. Restructuring is not a choice but an inevitability.

**2. Standardization Pressure in EV Platform Design**

Core elements such as battery cells, pack design, and charging ports are converging toward global standards. In the internal combustion era, engine and transmission designs varied by vehicle type and manufacturer, resulting in high customization of components. Electric vehicles are the opposite. Once battery standards are established, cooling systems and thermal management components are also standardized at the global level. This **expands supply competition to a global scale**, particularly exposing Korean small component suppliers to direct competition with Chinese and Eastern European competitors on price.

**3. Deepening Vertical Integration by OEMs**

Emerging EV manufacturers, led by Tesla, designed **vertically integrated structures** from battery packs to electronic control modules from the ground up. Incumbent automakers are attempting to replicate this model. The pace of moves to develop and manufacture batteries and related components in-house or through affiliated entities is accelerating. As a result, the traditional linear supply chain of "Tier 1 suppliers → OEMs" is becoming blurred. In certain technology areas, **OEMs have begun directly absorbing** or acquiring component suppliers. Korean suppliers that fail to respond quickly to these integration moves are falling behind.

**4. Korean Material Suppliers' High 'Battery Dependence'**

Paradoxically, the segment where Korean material suppliers have grown most during EV transition is battery-related materials (cathode materials, anode materials, separators, electrolytes, etc.). However, this growth is **too limited to offset the collapse in demand for other components**. While Korean suppliers' portfolio in the OEM supply chain remains bifurcated—"battery material orders + existing internal combustion engine parts"—Chinese and Eastern European suppliers are transitioning away from internal combustion engine components entirely and moving to EV-specific parts faster. Korean suppliers tend to **invest fewer resources in new segments** due to conservatism in maintaining existing revenue streams.

## Why Korean Suppliers Remain at Risk: Structural Weaknesses

**High OEM Concentration**

Among Korean suppliers' revenues, Samsung and LG handle only a small portion (materials) of the battery value chain. Automotive component suppliers, by contrast, often show high dependence on Hyundai and Kia. Restructuring or purchasing strategy changes by 1-2 OEMs can **overturn entire component supplier results**.

**Weak Link in Multi-Tiered Supply Chains**

Korean suppliers typically oscillate between Tier 1 (direct OEM sales) and Tier 2 (supply to Tier 1 suppliers). When OEMs pursue vertical integration and begin pulling material and component suppliers directly in, bypassing Tier 1 suppliers, mid-tier Korean suppliers face **simultaneous loss of customers and cost pressure**.

**Shortened Technology Innovation Cycle**

In the EV era, component replacement cycles are much faster than with internal combustion engines. Battery technology and thermal management technology undergo generational changes every 3-5 years, and component design changes with each generation. The weakness of Korean small and mid-sized suppliers—**whose R&D investment capacity and speed struggle to keep pace with global competition**—is amplified.

## Conclusion

EV transition slowdown is not a 'timeline extension' for suppliers but 'time pressure for structural transformation.' As three waves—battery standardization, OEM vertical integration, and reduced part counts—converge simultaneously, the existing supply chain order is being redrawn. In this process, Korean material and component suppliers face a triple bind: scale disadvantage, difficulty keeping pace with technology cycles, and high OEM dependence. Proactive restructuring and exploration of new segments are becoming essential conditions for survival.
