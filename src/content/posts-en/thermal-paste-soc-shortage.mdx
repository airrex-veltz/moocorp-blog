## TIM Shortage Crisis: Why Data Center CPU Procurement Faces 12-Month Lead Times

A $2 trillion overhaul of global data center infrastructure has hit an unexpected bottleneck. The supply of thermal interface materials (TIM)—the metal-ceramic composite adhesive commonly called thermal paste—is effectively depleted. The industry now depends on two APAC manufacturers, faces 6–8 month lead times, and has zero geographic diversification.

## Thermal Interface Materials: The Unexpected Bottleneck

To some, TIM is just adhesive. But for data center operations, it is critical material that withstands the harshest conditions. In an era when AI chips generate 150–400W of heat, the thermal transfer efficiency between CPU and heatsink directly determines equipment lifespan—a 1–2 degree difference can shorten operational life by years. Over the past three years, NVIDIA, Intel, and AMD chip shipments have grown over 40% annually, yet the chemical manufacturers of TIM materials (mostly in Japan and Taiwan) have delayed capacity investments. Now, as chipmakers face a surge in orders, lead times exceed six months.

## Why Now: Four Pressure Points Behind the TIM Crisis

**1. AI Infrastructure Overheating**: OpenAI, Google, and Meta are simultaneously building data centers worth tens of billions of dollars. Each company needs tens of thousands of H100, H200, and TPU chips per quarter, but TIM allocation falls short. Custom supply-chain production is impossible—the minimum order size exceeds 100 tons per month.

**2. Supplier Concentration**: Two makers—one Japanese electronic materials giant and one Taiwanese chemical company—control 60% of global TIM capacity. US and European competitors have not entered the market in the past five years, and Chinese producers lack supply security for high-purity ceramic feedstock, creating trust deficits. The result: acute geopolitical risk exposure.

**3. Raw Material Constraints**: The price of key TIM inputs—boron nitride (BN) and alumina (Al₂O₃)—has tripled over the past 18 months. Producers are hesitant to scale aggressively because of raw material import tariffs and delivery risk. Rising shipping costs between Japan and Taiwan have also lengthened lead times.

**4. High-End Specification Concentration**: High-performance chips demand more sophisticated TIM—better flowability and uniform thermal conductivity. Mid-range server TIM has relative abundance, but premium-grade material for high-end users like H100 is scarce. This concentration has extended average wait times.

## Structural Weaknesses That Make Recovery Difficult

**Avoiding Feedstock Diversification**: Even TSMC and Samsung, which once complained of equipment shortages, have not publicly pressured TIM makers. Semiconductor producers care less about materials supply than chip production margins. As a result, end customers like Meta and Google must negotiate directly with suppliers—and with only a handful of TIM makers, price collusion risk lingers.

**High Barriers to Entry**: Building a new TIM factory takes 2–3 years and $100 million or more. During that time, raw material import approvals, environmental and safety certifications, and production validation must all be completed. Even major chipmakers are considering in-house production but lack the core chemistry expertise; technology transfer negotiations remain stalled.

## The Bottom Line

The TIM shortage appears to be a material supply issue, but it is really the cost of a decade of semiconductor growth during which the industry ignored the small-stuff diversification. Companies planning AI infrastructure investments today must secure heat management capacity as urgently as they secure chips themselves.
