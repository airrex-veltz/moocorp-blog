## US and EU Rare Earth Localization Investments Erode Global Mining Companies' Market Share

As automotive, battery, and semiconductor manufacturing returns to developed countries, the supply chain for rare earth elements—a critical input—is being restructured. Decisions by the US and Europe to avoid geopolitical risk and strengthen technological independence are fundamentally shaking global mining companies' market structure.

## Why Rare Earth Localization Now?

Rare earth elements are a collective term for 17 elements essential to electronics, defense, and renewable energy equipment. Currently, most mining and processing is concentrated in China. If China restricts supply or imposes sanctions, advanced economies' industries face direct impact. This is why the US has accelerated self-sufficiency policies for strategic materials since the 2020s. The EU likewise enacted the Critical Raw Materials Act in 2023 and aims to significantly expand mining and processing capacity by 2030. This wave of investment threatens the profit structure and market share of existing global mining companies.

## Why Mining Companies Are Being Squeezed Now: Four Factors

**1. Geopolitical Risk Becomes Formal Policy**

In the past, Chinese supply was so cheap that even advanced economies had no incentive to get involved. Now risk trumps cost in decision-making. For the US and EU, materials sourced from "our own territory" or "allied nations"—even at a slightly higher price—carry strategic value. For global mining companies, geopolitical location has become the measure of their worth, superseding individual mining output.

**2. Government Support and Policy Protectionism**

The US CHIPS and Science Act and the EU's Critical Raw Materials Act come with more than ideology—they bring substantial funding and tax incentives. They support new mining and processing projects through state policy, not markets. Global mining companies must compete against new suppliers created with government capital. Competing on profitability against players whose losses are partially absorbed by the state is inherently unfair.

**3. Technology Investment and Infrastructure Rebuilding**

Rare earth mining and processing is an industry built on accumulated expertise and know-how. But as advanced economies step in directly, powerful technology and capital investment pour into the sector. Universities undertake research, new technologies are validated, and equipment is automated—areas where global mining companies spent two decades building advantage are now receiving concentrated R&D capacity from advanced economies. The pace of catch-up is incomparable to the past.

**4. Asymmetric Environmental and Labor Regulation**

Global mining companies already face environmental and labor regulations at their operating mines. Simultaneously, they must meet higher standards to gain permits for new projects. In contrast, government-backed advanced economy projects set regulatory standards from the start. The same regulations are easier for the newer entrant to accommodate.

## Yet Competition's Nature Is Permanently Altered

**Supply Monopoly Collapse—Irreversible:** Once nations develop self-sufficiency capacity, they will not return to relying solely on global mining companies. Even if profit margins fall, they maintain self-sufficient facilities for reasons of "national security." Global mining companies lose the room to raise prices through supply constraints as they did before.

**Extended Market Share Redistribution:** This transition takes more than a few years—it extends over a decade or more. The cycle is long: new mine development, processing facility completion, and quality certification. Throughout this process, global mining companies' market share gradually erodes. Supply chain diversification, once started, is difficult to reverse without policy changes.

**The Cost of Technology Convergence:** As advanced economies accelerate their supply entry, the profitability structure of the entire industry deteriorates. Cost-reduction competition intensifies, and as the technology gap narrows, prices fall. Global mining companies must sacrifice either economies of scale or technological advantage.

## Conclusion

US and EU rare earth localization investments are not short-term policy but structural transformation. It means the supply leverage that global mining companies have enjoyed for decades is slipping from their hands. The process is lengthy, but once begun, it cannot be stopped. We are entering an era where mining and processing profitability is determined by policy variables.
