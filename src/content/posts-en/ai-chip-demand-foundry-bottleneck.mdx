## As AI Chip Demand Creates a Foundry Bottleneck, Non-AI Semiconductors Fall Into Delivery Hell

As of 2026, the global semiconductor industry has split into two. On one side are foundries channeling all production capacity into the surging demand for AI chips; on the other are memory, automotive, and industrial chip makers waiting for deliveries amid the AI frenzy. As **TSMC** (**TSM**)-dominated foundry capacity becomes monopolized by AI chip production, lead times for general-purpose semiconductors are now exceeding six months.

## TSMC's Capacity Monopoly and the Dilemma of Non-AI Chip Companies

**TSMC** maintained the highest profitability across all industries in the first half of 2026, with revenue growth of 36.0% and net margin of 49.9% (as of 2026-09-25). However, all the momentum for this growth comes from a surge in orders from AI chip designers like Nvidia, AMD, Google, and Meta. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) for AI training and accelerator chips are concentrated on 3nm and 5nm processes, with most of TSMC's cutting-edge process capacity allocated to these customers.

As a result, delivery delays are intensifying even in mature processes (28nm and above) for smartphones, automotive, and IoT devices. Memory chips that typically have 4–8 week lead times are now delayed by 16 weeks or more, and automotive microcontrollers frequently fall 3 months or more behind schedule.

## Why Is This Bottleneck Worsening Now? Four Factors

**1. Surge in Advanced Orders from AI Chip Designers**: **Broadcom (AVGO)** is posting strong growth in 2026, driven entirely by demand for network and AI interconnect chips. For TSMC, the priority is clear: fast-growing customers take precedence over slow-growing automotive customers.

**2. Structural Barriers to Entry in the Foundry Market**: Alternative foundries besides TSMC (Samsung, GlobalFoundries, SMIC) face a large technology gap in cutting-edge processes and struggle to recover large capital investments, making them reluctant to expand capacity. In this situation, customers have no choice but to wait in line at TSMC.

**3. Delays in Intel's In-House Foundry Development**: **Intel (INTC)** achieved 25.4% revenue growth in 2026 but suffers severe losses, with a net margin of −19.8% (as of 2026-09-25). As its own processes lose competitiveness, it has increased outsourcing orders, which is further exacerbating the TSMC bottleneck.

**4. Lack of Alternative Supply for Non-AI Chips**: Although memory, automotive, and industrial chips could be produced on TSMC's downstream processes, they are stuck waiting due to cost considerations. Even **Qualcomm (QCOM)**, facing 2026 revenue decline of −4.0% year-over-year (as of 2026-09-25), is accelerating its shift from mobile processors to AI chips in its portfolio.

## The Bottleneck That Won't End: Structural Weaknesses and Strengths

**Risks of Foundry Monopoly**: The very fact that one company, TSMC, controls a significant share of global semiconductor production is a supply chain risk. If geopolitical tensions (Taiwan relations) escalate, this bottleneck could quickly become an industry crisis.

**Yet TSMC's Technical Advantage Is Overwhelming**: The 49.9% net margin demonstrates that TSMC's process technology is beyond competitors' reach. Customers queue at TSMC even at significant cost.

## Summary

The AI chip bonanza has consolidated the foundry industry, leaving non-AI semiconductors in a state of supply chain starvation. This structure is unlikely to change in the near term (2–3 years). For foundry competition to improve, either Samsung or Intel would need to match TSMC's process technology, or AI chip demand itself would need to stabilize—neither appears imminent.
