## Rare Earths and Cobalt Supply Chain Stalled: Chinese Regulations and African Mining Instability Constraining Advanced Manufacturing Capacity in 2027

Last June, China announced a 15% year-over-year reduction in rare earth export quotas, while cobalt mining in Congo's Kasai region faces instability, halting production at approximately 30,000 tons per month. A single-country bottleneck in critical minerals on which batteries, semiconductors, and defense industries depend is becoming real.

## Current State: Extreme Single-Source Dependency

China controls 60% of global rare earth oxide (REO) production. The Democratic Republic of Congo manages 70% of the world's cobalt supply. **MP** (Mineral Processors) and **NEO** (Rare Earths ETF) are indicators that expose this dependency. In 2025, rare earth spot prices rose 35% above historical average, while cobalt trades at a premium of around $15 per ton.

## Why Supply Chain Weakness Is Becoming Visible Now: Four Pressure Factors

**1. Strategization of Chinese Government Regulation**: China has reclassified rare earths as a strategic national asset. Export quota reductions, enhanced environmental oversight of mining and refining firms, and mandatory recovery of rare earth-containing waste (intended to neutralize Western recycling technology) are being implemented in phases through 2026–2027. This signals unpredictable volatility to intermediate suppliers (semiconductor and battery makers).

**2. Sharpening of African Mining Political Risk**: Congo's renegotiation of mining rights, activation of ethnic conflicts, and involvement of neighboring countries such as Rwanda and Uganda make cobalt production irregular. The probability of at least one shutdown lasting 30 days or more occurring within the next 12 months is assessed at 60% or higher.

**3. Surge in Transition Demand and Supply Gap**: Electric vehicle batteries (lithium and cobalt intensive), wind turbine magnets (rare earth permanent magnets), and AI chips (gallium and germanium) are forecast to grow over 20% annually through 2026–2030. Meanwhile, new mine development requires 6–10 years for environmental approval, pushing supply relief beyond 2028.

**4. Constraints of Australian and Indonesian Infrastructure**: Diversification alternatives (Australian rare earths, Indonesian nickel) also face constraints in ramping production (environmental opposition, community concerns, insufficient capital). Indonesia's nickel export ban ties the global market even more tightly to China and Congo without a complete substitution strategy.

## Yet Structural Weaknesses Persist: Long-Term Supply Reconfiguration Inevitable

**Immature Recycling Technology**: Recovery rates for rare earths and cobalt from spent batteries and e-waste remain at 70%. High dependence on Chinese technology makes building autonomous circular economy a post-2030 challenge.

**Insufficient Strategic Reserves**: The U.S. and EU are increasing deliberate stockpiles, but they amount to only three months' consumption. This is inadequate to buffer against supply disruptions lasting three months or more.

**Loose Coordination Among Allies**: OECD developed nations are technically pursuing joint procurement and supply chain diversification, but policy consistency is weak due to the cost of accepting higher prices and supply instability.

## Summary

The 2027 rare earth and cobalt supply chain tension is a structural event. While Chinese policy and African politics drive near-term variables, rising demand and delayed development of new sources are the fundamental pressures. Valuations of raw materials companies and funds like **MP** and **NEO** already partially reflect this supply shortage, but the risk that falling capacity utilization and rising production costs will erode manufacturing margins remains underpriced.
