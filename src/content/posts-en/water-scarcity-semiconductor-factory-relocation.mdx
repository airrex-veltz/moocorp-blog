## Water Scarcity Forces Redefinition of Semiconductor and Steel Mill Sites

Freshwater depletion is no longer an environmental issue but a survival matter for manufacturing. Semiconductor production and steelmaking are industries that consume massive quantities of cooling water, and operating costs in water-depleted regions are rising sharply. As water rights costs surge and regulations tighten simultaneously across the US West and Southeast Asia, multinational manufacturers are fundamentally reconsidering their factory locations.

### The Mismatch Between Cooling Water Demand and Supply

Semiconductor foundries and large steel mills require enormous quantities of freshwater for cooling. Cooling water is the heart of the production process, and the volume scales directly with factory size. As climate change intensifies droughts, water supplies are becoming constrained in some regions, while agricultural water demand remains high. In this context, industrial water rights prices have no choice but to rise steeply. In water-scarce regions, water availability is no longer treated as an "infinite resource" but as a "finite commodity."

### Four Pressure Points

**1. Tightening Water-Based Regulations**: Authorities in California, Texas, inland China, and Southeast Asian source regions are strengthening water discharge regulations. Trends include setting caps on industrial water intake volumes and tightening wastewater discharge standards after treatment. This raises operating costs for existing factories and makes approval of new plant construction more difficult.

**2. Alternative Water Supply Costs**: When supply is insufficient, industrial facilities turn to alternatives like recycled water, desalination, and rainwater harvesting. However, all of these are far more expensive than conventional surface or groundwater. The cost of building and operating desalination facilities can be significant enough to threaten a single factory's profitability.

**3. Regional Limits to Cooling Efficiency**: As climate warms, more energy is needed to lower cooling water temperatures. Rising energy costs directly translate to higher manufacturing costs. Because water-stressed regions are mostly arid as well, this vicious cycle compounds.

**4. Unpredictable Regulatory Risk**: It is uncertain which region will be subject to water restrictions next. Regulations can tighten without notice, and there is risk that the asset value of already-built factories could plummet. This uncertainty itself makes investment decisions difficult.

### Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**The pressure to relocate is unavoidable.** Manufacturers have no choice but to move to water-rich regions, which necessitates reorganizing entire supply chains. Investment concentration toward water-abundant emerging regions like India and Vietnam is expected to accelerate.

**Yet the choice of alternative sites is also limited.** Even where water is adequate, power supply, labor, or political stability may be lacking. Moving a single factory is not merely a capital transfer but involves comprehensive long-term risk reassessment.

In an era where water scarcity reshapes the manufacturing landscape, long-term site selection must now prioritize water security as a top consideration.
