## Structural Weaknesses in Container Shipping Exposed

The global shipping industry faces dual pressures from overcapacity and weak demand. As new ship deliveries have accumulated over years, international trade has grown slower than expected, causing spot rates to fall below historical averages. Major carriers' cash flows are deteriorating, and structural consolidation has become inevitable.

## The Structure of Carrier Profit Erosion — Supply-Demand Imbalance

**Major container carriers including Maersk** face a cyclical problem. Over recent years, newly built vessels have continuously joined the fleet. Meanwhile, international trade growth has slowed, compounded by economic weakness in some regions. Consequently, supply—available cargo capacity—continues to increase while demand—actual cargo to transport—falls short of expectations. In this gap, carriers' profitability erodes rapidly.

## Why Major Carriers' Profits Are Declining Now: Four Factors

**1. Cumulative Effect of New Ship Deliveries**: Shipping companies signed long-term construction contracts during past boom periods. These vessels are now being delivered sequentially, with additional capacity continuing to enter the market. From an individual carrier's perspective, this maintained competitiveness; for the industry as a whole, it deepens oversupply.

**2. Weakening Global Trade Volume**: U.S. and European consumption slowdown, economic weakness in some countries, and geopolitical uncertainty all suppress international maritime cargo demand. The recovery momentum following the COVID-19 pandemic has not been sustained, and importers continue inventory adjustments. Until cargo volumes recover, rate weakness is likely to persist long-term.

**3. Spot Rate Lows**: Spot shipping rates fallen below historical averages directly pressure carrier cash profitability. Long-term contract rates are also being gradually adjusted downward, making it difficult to recover margins on new pricing. Variable costs cannot be significantly reduced, so profit margins deteriorate even faster.

**4. Rising Relative Fixed Cost Burden**: New ships are fuel-efficient, but ship depreciation, financing costs, charter hire, port charges, and labor costs remain substantial. When rates are at low levels, these fixed costs consume a maximized proportion of revenue, and carriers' break-even points rise.

## Why Restructuring Cannot Be Avoided: Carriers' Constraints

**Limits to Charter Reduction**: Major carriers including CMA CGM, Evergreen, and MSC already own substantial numbers of self-owned vessels. Some charter reduction is possible, but decisions to idle or sell company-owned ships mean accepting long-term losses. Near-term earnings improvement options are therefore limited.

**Lack of Rate Increase Opportunity**: Market structure offers no room for rate increases. Shippers are already leveraging competition among multiple carriers, and carrier negotiating power is weak amid excess capacity. Individual carrier rate increase attempts only lead to shipper defection.

**Accelerating Merger and Acquisition Pressure**: Profitability deterioration destabilizes smaller carriers first. When capital raising becomes difficult or existing debt repayment pressure increases, acquisition by larger carriers becomes a natural path. Industry consolidation signals the market's attempt to structurally reduce oversupply.

## Summary

Shipping rate weakness is increasingly unlikely to be a 1–2 year cyclical fluctuation. Years are needed for natural elimination of oversupply through ship scrapping and depreciation, and major carriers' cash flow will remain under pressure during that period. Mergers, acquisitions, and long-term restructuring are now not a choice but a survival issue.
