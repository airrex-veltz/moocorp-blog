# Rising U.S. Dollar Strength Amplifies Emerging Market Debt Repayment Burden and Financial Risk

Emerging market investors remain unable to shake their unease. The sustained strength of the U.S. dollar is rapidly increasing the burden of emerging market companies and governments to repay their dollar-denominated debts, creating a vicious cycle that elevates the risk of debt default.

## Dollar Strength: Hidden Threat to Emerging Markets

Currency value fluctuation in foreign exchange markets is typically one of the key indicators of emerging market economic health. The recent relative strength of the dollar simultaneously signals weakness in emerging market currencies. In fact, FXE (Invesco CurrencyShares Euro Currency Trust), which tracks the euro, recorded 105.15 dollars as of September 25, 2026, declining 2.20% over the past month. This means the euro weakened against the dollar, and emerging market currencies likely followed a similar downward trajectory.

The problem with dollar strength is clear. Emerging market governments and corporations hold substantial dollar-denominated debt in international financial markets. Major commodity exporters—oil, minerals, agriculture—have largely issued bonds in dollars. Whenever the dollar strengthens, the amount of debt these entities must pay automatically increases. This occurs because exchange losses emerge when converting domestic currency revenues into dollars.

## Structural Vulnerability in Emerging Market Foreign Debt

The foreign debt problem in emerging markets is essentially structural. During early development stages, many emerging market governments required foreign capital inflows as essential to economic growth, which inevitably led to the accumulation of dollar debt. Infrastructure investment, industrialization funds, and crisis mitigation financing—all were borrowed in dollars.

The situation for emerging market corporations is not dramatically different. Major emerging market enterprises with international competitiveness had no choice but to rely on foreign capital. As a result, a substantial portion of their assets consists of dollar debt. The volume of borrowing in dollars by multinational company subsidiaries, energy and mineral extraction companies, and financial institutions in international bond markets is far larger than commonly imagined.

Dollar strength exposes this fragile structure. As emerging market currencies weaken, export revenues in dollar terms also decline, while the dollar debt that must be repaid actually increases. It resembles the vicious cycle of a household budget where debts grow while income shrinks.

## Default Risk Becoming a Reality

This structural problem is transforming into a genuine threat. The fact that default risk premiums on emerging market debt—the yield premium on emerging market bonds—remain elevated relative to historical averages demonstrates this. The market increasingly assesses the likelihood that emerging market governments and corporations will fail to repay their debts on time.

In reality, instances of emerging market debt default have occurred intermittently. When currency fluctuations and commodity price collapses occur simultaneously, the debt burden can spike to unsustainable levels. Dollar strength serves as a catalyst amplifying this risk.

From an investor perspective, credit quality assessments of emerging market bonds must be recalibrated. The possibility of credit downgrades for several emerging market countries has become a realistic concern. Once downgrades occur, what follows is not a virtuous cycle but a vicious one. Rising borrowing costs due to credit deterioration → increasing debt burden → slowing economic growth → further credit decline creates a downward spiral.

## Emerging Market Concerns Reflected in ETF Markets

The recent price movements of major ETFs tracking emerging markets compress all these concerns into a single narrative.

**EEM (iShares MSCI Emerging Markets ETF)** recorded 67.98 dollars as of September 25, 2026. It gained 1.21% over the past month and 1.18% over three months, but showed significant volatility over a six-month range of 54.16 to 71.57 dollars. This reflects sharp psychological fluctuations across the entire emerging market. A PER of 14.5 may appear to signal undervaluation, yet this also implies deteriorating profitability itself.

**VWO (Vanguard FTSE Emerging Markets Index Fund ETF Shares)** stood at 60.16 dollars with a 0.64% decline over the past month. Unlike EEM, it has shown weakness recently, suggesting specific deterioration in emerging market economic fundamentals. However, with a 2.89% gain over three months, it demonstrates a recovery trend following a correction phase on a medium-term basis. The six-month range of 52.04–61.42 dollars indicates recovery from lower levels.

**IEMG (iShares Core MSCI Emerging Markets ETF)** reached 82.55 dollars, gaining 0.86% over one month and 1.54% over three months. However, the six-month range of 66.45 to 86.49 dollars reveals extreme volatility. With the market now approaching recent highs, downside risk remains present. At PER 14.5, it suggests undervaluation similar to EEM, but this could also indicate that considerable default risk premium has already been priced into the asset.

## Mechanism of Deteriorating Investment Sentiment

The deterioration of investment sentiment in emerging markets transcends simple numerical decline. A sequential mechanism operates: dollar strength → currency losses → increased debt burden → credit concerns → capital withdrawal.

International investors begin preparing to pull funds from emerging market portfolios. When default risk rises, even high yields become less attractive than the potential risk of principal loss. Institutional investors in particular show a tendency to rapidly avoid emerging market assets ahead of credit downgrades.

This capital outflow further weakens emerging market currencies and makes dollar debt even heavier. Declining foreign currency reserves signal the onset of financial crisis. In extreme cases, this can escalate to structural crises similar to the IMF currency crisis.

## Underlying Reasons for Dollar Strength

Understanding why dollar strength persists is important. The relatively elevated U.S. interest rates, resilience of the U.S. economy, and geopolitical risk aversion work in combination. This is not a short-term phenomenon; it is likely to persist for some time ahead.

Unless the United States cuts interest rates or the economic outlook deteriorates, dollar strength is expected to maintain its structural character. From emerging market perspectives, this means an unavoidable headwind could persist for an extended period.

## Investment Decision at a Crossroads

The undervaluation signals in current emerging market ETFs present a crossroads for investors. Low valuations may represent an opportunity for gains after recovery, or they may signal an impending debt crisis.

Several key variables will determine the outcome: whether dollar strength continues, the policy response by emerging market governments, and changes in the international interest rate environment. The severity of emerging market debt problems varies by country. The gap between nations with sufficient foreign reserves and low dollar debt ratios versus those lacking such advantages is expected to widen.

## Conclusion: Careful Selection Required

Rising debt repayment burdens for emerging markets amid sustained U.S. dollar strength has elevated from mere market news to structural financial risk. The sideways movement in emerging market ETFs such as EEM, VWO, and IEMG precisely reflects these concerns.

Investors must conduct more thorough examinations of debt structures across emerging markets by country and sector. Comprehensive evaluation of foreign reserves, dollar debt scale, export structure, and policy credibility demands selective investment. In an environment of sustained dollar strength, emerging market investment has become a space where high returns and high risks coexist.

Until signs of U.S. interest rate policy changes or dollar weakness emerge, emerging market exposure requires careful management. Rather than indiscriminate emerging market exposure at this juncture, a strategy concentrating on countries and companies with demonstrated creditworthiness and debt sustainability appears prudent.
