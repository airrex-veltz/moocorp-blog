## Water Scarcity Fundamentally Transforms Factory Cost Structures

Chemical manufacturing has long been obsessed with water as much as energy. It's an industry that cannot operate a single production line without water—from semiconductor cleaning to petrochemical cooling. But now the price of that water is changing rapidly. Factories in water-scarce regions are beginning to invest in water recycling technology and desalination, and in this process, the existing capital-intensive manufacturing model faces margin pressure. At the same time, value reassessment of water treatment companies and membrane technology firms is beginning.

## Water Dependency Structure of Chemical Plants

Chemical manufacturing is fundamentally a triangle of water-energy-raw materials. Raw material costs and energy costs have long been transparently exposed, but water costs vary greatly by region. In water-abundant regions, water barely factors into the calculation. However, in water-scarce regions—particularly key hubs in the Middle East, India, and parts of Australia—the situation is different. As water supply constraints tighten, supply prices have risen, and usage restrictions from regulation have also intensified. Processes that waste water now mean high costs.

This change is immediately reflected in decisions about new plant locations. Previously, factories were built near raw materials and energy sources, but now water reliability must also be part of that triangle. As a result, the concentration of capital investment is shifting rapidly.

## Why Structural Transition to Water Treatment Technology Is Happening Now: 4 Factors

**1. Economics Shift for Water Recycling Technology**: From the perspective of costs for input water-energy-chemicals, recycling technologies like reverse osmosis and nanofiltration were expensive in the past. But as membrane technology efficiency improved and economies of scale were realized, profitability reversed. A point has arrived where reusing wastewater is cheaper than sourcing water from outside.

**2. Regulatory Tightening Becomes a Cost**: As water discharge standards tighten and usage limits are implemented, regulatory compliance now means technology investment. There is no way around restrictive regulations, and to avoid them, equipment purchase is the only choice. Regulatory arbitrage forces technology adoption.

**3. Technological Advancement in Desalination**: As energy efficiency of reverse osmosis membranes improves, the unit cost curve for desalination has declined. Especially when combined with renewable energy like solar power, total costs fall significantly. Now plants near seawater sources have reached a threshold where they can internalize desalination.

**4. Supply Chain ESG Pressure from Customers**: Brand companies have begun tracking suppliers' water footprints. Certification (e.g., Alliance for Water Stewardship) is becoming a transaction condition. Companies that don't use water efficiently face threats to contracts themselves.

## Problems That Remain: Structural Weaknesses and Implications of Restructuring

Even as technology transition becomes inevitable, adoption speed follows capital structure. **First, redistribution of capital intensity**: Existing plants' water treatment equipment was built to match the technology level at the time of factory establishment. To replace it requires additional capital. New investments can build new technology from the ground up, but existing plants must bear retrofit costs. This reduces the value of existing assets.

**Second, transition to technology supplier role**: As chemical plants internalize their own water treatment equipment, water treatment companies are likely to be redefined from suppliers to technology partners or contract operators. The shift to asset-light models is a process of dismantling existing capital-centric business structures. Membrane technology firms and water treatment suppliers must become the core high-value layers in this restructuring process; if they fail to do so, they will be pushed into marginal industries.

**Third, intensification of regional disparities**: Plants in water-abundant regions can still use water cheaply, so the urgency of technology investment is low. Only water-scarce regions are forced into structural transition. Over the long term, this will result in relocation of industrial sites and regionalization of supply chains.

## Summary

Water scarcity's economic impact is forcing a restructuring of chemical manufacturing's cost structure. Technology advancement has opened the path, while regulation and customer pressure are accelerating it. In this process, the shift to asset-light models is an unavoidable trend, and how much added value each business can capture in water treatment and membrane technology will determine its survival line.
