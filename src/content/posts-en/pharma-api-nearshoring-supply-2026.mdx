The global pharmaceutical industry's active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) supply chain is undergoing fundamental transformation. Production concentrated in China and India for decades is being dispersed to North America, Southeast Asia, and the Indo-Pacific region through accelerating 'nearshoring.' As regulatory tightening, geopolitical tensions, and supply risks converge, pharmaceutical companies are shifting their strategic focus from 'cost minimization' to 'supply chain resilience.'

For the past two decades, China and India have held an absolute dominant position in the global API market. Cheap labor, massive chemical and fermentation infrastructure, and low regulatory barriers formed the foundation. However, this structure contained inherent vulnerabilities. Environmental regulations in China have led to consecutive facility closures, while intensified GMP (Good Manufacturing Practice) monitoring of India's pharmaceutical industry has increased uncertainty. Following the exposure of global supply chain disruptions during the pandemic, pharmaceutical companies have begun reassessing risks.

**Let's examine four structural factors driving the acceleration of nearshoring.**

**1. Regulatory Policy Transition**: U.S. and European pharmaceutical industry policies are emphasizing supply chain domestication. The U.S. Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) provides tax incentives for domestic manufacturing facilities, and Europe is pursuing expanded intra-regional production citing strategic autonomy. As regulatory signals become clearer, the landscape of investment decisions shifts.

**2. Realization of Geopolitical Risk**: U.S.-China tensions, economic sanctions, and concerns about supply chain weaponization have moved beyond simple economic scenarios. Because pharmaceuticals are critical to healthcare security, major nations seek to relocate API production to their own territories or allied nations. Indo-Pacific countries like India, South Korea, and Australia are emerging as targets.

**3. Enhanced Quality and Transparency Requirements**: Advanced-country regulators are strengthening oversight of API production sources. Production transparency, environmental standards, and labor conditions have become criteria for supply chain selection. This reduces the relative attractiveness of low-cost regions like China and India.

**4. Economic Viability Reassessment**: Low wages alone are no longer competitive. When calculating the combined costs of supply disruption, regulatory risk, and renegotiation timeframes, nearshoring becomes advantageous in Total Cost of Ownership terms. This transition occurs particularly rapidly for critical medicines and high-value-added APIs.

**The divergence between beneficiaries and those at risk is stark.** Pharmaceutical companies and contract manufacturers (CMOs/CDMOs) in North America, Southeast Asia, and the Indo-Pacific region face new investment and production capacity expansion opportunities. Meanwhile, existing API makers in China and India face customer loss and price pressure. Pharmaceutical customers are investing in diversification strategies while restructuring relationships with existing suppliers and reconfiguring negotiating power.

Three conditions are necessary for this trend to persist. First, consistency in regulatory policy. Second, accelerated maturation of manufacturing bases in nearshoring countries. Third, formation of pricing systems that can accommodate cost differences. The inflection point will likely arrive when advanced-country pharmaceutical companies willingly pay a premium for 'supply chain stability.' This transition point has a high probability of occurring within three to five years.
