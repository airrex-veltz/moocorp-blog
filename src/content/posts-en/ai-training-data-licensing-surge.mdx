## AI's Data Hunger, Content Companies' Shifting Negotiating Power

As artificial intelligence models' appetite for training data has exploded, news outlets, publishers, and online media have begun dramatically raising their licensing fees for their original works. While large language models increasingly require vast amounts of text, the true owners of this data have never been adequately compensated. After two years of silence, the content industry has come to the negotiating table, and the ripple effects are shaking everything from AI companies' cost structures to the future of the web ecosystem.

## Training Data Sources and Copyright Gaps

Modern AI models learn from almost all publicly available text on the internet. Newspaper articles, academic journals, books, blogs, social media, and Wikipedia are all included in training datasets. These contents are copyrighted works created through intellectual labor, but until now, AI companies have freely collected and trained on them simply because they were publicly available on the internet. Under the logic that "scraping is legal," creators and publishers received no licensing fees whatsoever.

This situation changed for a simple reason: AI companies became large and powerful enough. Content owners transformed from non-negotiating parties to negotiating parties. News organizations have filed lawsuits against AI companies that used their articles as training data without permission, and publishers have demanded collective licensing agreements. The reality that "your model won't work without our data" has become negotiating leverage.

## Why Are Licensing Costs Surging Now: 4 Factors

**1. Emergence of Regulation**: The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act and strengthened copyright interpretations in various countries have begun explicitly mandating "creator consent." As the legal justification for data use crumbled, companies came to view licensing as structurally necessary. Paradoxically, regulation has actually accelerated negotiations by removing uncertainty.

**2. Litigation Pressure**: Newspaper coalitions (News Corp, New York Times, and others), author associations, and music copyright holders have lined up in court. While the scale of damages is difficult to predict precisely, the mere existence of lawsuits has made preventive licensing more cost-effective. Licensing fees are justified as risk hedging.

**3. Advancement of Data Competition**: Model performance differences are no longer determined solely by architecture or computational power. The quality and diversity of training data have become the primary competitive variable. High-quality data in specific domains—healthcare, law, finance—is scarce, so institutions that possess it wield negotiating power. The argument that "our data makes your model smarter" works.

**4. Strategic Shift in Content Companies**: The news and publishing industries, which struggled with faltering advertising revenue models in the past, have discovered data as a new revenue source. Paid subscriptions, licensing, and data partnerships are being added to the revenue mix. The content industry, which once suffered under the dominance of platform companies, is now benefiting from negotiations with AI companies.

## The Light and Shadow of Structural Change: Strengths and Weaknesses

**Winners**: Large publishers with extensive proprietary content, news organizations, and academic data owners. Licensing fees represent a new revenue stream, and simultaneously can be leveraged as a negotiating condition if they want their content excluded from model training.

**Losers**: When AI companies' learning costs surge, those costs eventually translate into higher user fees or product prices for corporate customers. Small AI startups lack the resources to afford licensing fees and may be squeezed out of the market. Additionally, small news outlets, independent authors, and academics have little individual bargaining power and fail to receive fair compensation.

## Summary: Web Ecosystem Reorganization

When the phenomenon of AI large-scale training data first emerged, it was "free." Now it is transitioning to "paid." This is not simply a matter of licensing contracts but a restructuring of the ecosystem itself—determining who extracts value from data and who receives that value back. Parties that proactively establish licensing agreements secure revenue, while those who act late lose negotiating power.
