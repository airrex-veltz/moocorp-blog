## Central Bank Currency Standardization Pressures Payment Intermediaries

As major central banks worldwide accelerate the development of digital currencies (CBDC), payment network companies face a fundamental threat to their business model. The scenario is that as state-issued digital currencies expand, the flow of funds that previously had to pass through banks and payment processors will shorten.

## Payment Networks Today: Still Strong but Growth Slowing

**Visa** (**V**) and **Mastercard** (**MA**) still maintain strong profitability. As of October 2, 2026, V closed at $360.66 per share and MA at $552.26. Over the past month, V declined 4.69% and MA declined 6.10%, but on a three-month basis, V was up 1.14% and MA up 3.77%. The two companies' revenue growth rates of 14.4% (V) and 14.1% (MA) respectively show they still maintain double-digit profitability.

However, this growth rate reflects the pre-CBDC expansion landscape. Once each country completes the digitalization of its national currency, the growth curve is likely to flatten.

## Why Payment Networks Face Threat Now: Four Digitalization Factors

**1. Peer-to-Peer Direct Trading of State Standard Currency**: Central bank-issued digital currencies enable individuals to trade directly with each other without intermediaries. Currently, V and MA are nearly essential channels for peer-to-peer remittances and consumer-to-merchant payments. If CBDC replaces this role, payment companies' transaction-based fee model will collapse.

**2. Intensifying Competition from Government-Led Payment Infrastructure**: When countries begin offering CBDC-based QR codes and API interfaces for free or at low cost, card networks' pricing power drops sharply. Particularly when merchants have choices at the point of sale, V and MA lose in price competition.

**3. Standardization of International Remittances via CBDC**: International remittances currently go through complex intermediary structures involving SWIFT, correspondent banking, and others. Payment companies like V and MA earn fees from this. However, once an international payment network connecting CBDCs becomes standardized, the scope for private payment companies' intervention shrinks. Bank-to-bank direct transactions become sufficient.

**4. Regulatory Uncertainty and Delayed Technology Standardization**: As CBDC adoption pace and technology standards diverge across countries, in the short term V and MA must support multiple standards simultaneously. This increases system investment costs and, long-term, risks their technology becoming obsolete when international standards are adopted.

## Why Visa and Mastercard Remain: Structural Residual Strengths

**Barriers from Existing Offline Payment Infrastructure**: Even if CBDC replaces online remittances, tens of millions of merchant POS terminals and physical credit card device networks worldwide take years to decades to replace. During this period, V and MA continue to function as the standard for physical transactions.

**Virtuous Cycle of International Payment Network**: During the transition period when multiple countries' CBDCs operate simultaneously, currency conversion between different national currencies is necessary. The role of managing this conversion and settlement is likely to remain with existing payment companies. Private networks are needed until trust relationships between countries are established.

## Conclusion

CBDC poses a real threat to payment network companies, but over the short term (5–10 years), a coexistence scenario is more realistic. If V and MA fail to evolve into CBDC-based payment systems, they could face growth constraints, but they may also maintain sufficient profitability through existing infrastructure and international settlement roles. The key is not coexistence but how quickly these two companies can reconfigure themselves for the CBDC era.
