## Subsidy Contraction Opens Fixed-Cost Trap for Battery Makers

As electric-vehicle subsidies in major markets globally phase down, battery makers are facing an intensifying overcapacity crisis. Companies that expanded production capacity from the late 2010s, betting on a surge in EV demand, now face simultaneous pressure from slowing sales and price competition.

## The Collapse of Subsidy-Dependent Growth

Over the past decade, government subsidies have driven EV market growth. The U.S. tax credit, China's NEV subsidies, and Europe's environment-linked incentives directly shaped consumer purchasing decisions. By lowering EV prices, these subsidies created steep demand curves for batteries.

Battery makers extrapolated this growth linearly and invested in large-scale new facilities. Since 2020, dozens of battery factory projects have been announced globally. Each company based capacity decisions on projections that EV sales would reach roughly 10 million units annually by 2030.

## Collapse Becomes Reality: Four Pressure Factors

**1. Policy Shift in Major Markets**: The U.S. narrowed tax-credit eligibility through income thresholds. China slashed NEV subsidies sharply from 2023 onward. The EU tightened battery-sourcing requirements and made support conditional. These shifts go beyond single-country policy—they hit EV price elasticity directly. Without subsidies, consumer demand collapses.

**2. EV Sales Growth Deceleration**: Early adopters and urban demand have been saturated. Remaining growth depends on price-sensitive mass markets. When economic growth slows, high-ticket EVs contract. Subsidy cuts plus economic weakness create compounded pressure.

**3. Anchoring of Excess Capacity**: Battery manufacturing is capital-intensive. Once a factory is built, it generates fixed costs indefinitely. Cutting capacity quickly is difficult—makers maintain minimal operations for risk hedging, R&D flexibility, and market-recovery preparation. But minimum operations cost money. Falling utilization sends unit costs soaring.

**4. The Price-Competition Death Spiral**: Excess capacity pushes battery unit costs downward relentlessly. Once pricing approaches marginal cost, all makers' margins compress. When margins fall, R&D and equipment modernization are deferred. Weakened innovation competition leaves only price competition—a vicious cycle.

## Structural Weakness and Possible Paths

**Fundamental Weakness**: Fixed costs in battery manufacturing far exceed variable costs. Small changes in capacity utilization swing overall profitability sharply. To grow sales in a subsidy-free EV market, makers must cut prices; to cut prices, they must cut costs. But if most costs are fixed, cost-cutting room is limited. Capacity expansion has halted, but existing facilities remain anchors.

**Survival Paths**: Technology innovation to fundamentally lower unit costs, structural restructuring that accepts redundant capacity, or betting on government policy support such as domestic EV protection or battery-sourcing mandates. All are painful in the near term.

## Conclusion

Subsidy cuts → slower sales → anchored overcapacity → intensified price competition → margin collapse. Breaking this structural loop demands more than simply "enduring until demand returns." Battery makers' 2027 is likely to be a time of final selection.
