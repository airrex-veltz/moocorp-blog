# Eco-Ship Regulation and Changes in Korean Shipbuilders' Order Structure

Since the International Maritime Organization (IMO) announced its 2050 carbon neutrality target, the global shipping industry has faced fundamental pressure to transition to alternative energy sources. As demand for next-generation eco-friendly vessels—such as ammonia and methanol-powered ships—grows steeply, South Korea's two major shipbuilders, Samsung Heavy Industries and Hanwha Ocean, are responding to this shift with different business models and order strategies. Changes in these two companies' financial structures and order characteristics are significantly affecting the ship industry's advance payment mechanisms and cash flow stability.

## IMO Regulatory Tightening and Concentration of Next-Generation Propulsion Fuel Technology

The IMO has mandated carbon intensity reduction for vessels by 2030 and requires full carbon neutrality by 2050. To achieve this goal, shipping companies have begun ordering vessels powered by carbon-neutral fuels such as ammonia, methanol, and hydrogen, moving away from low sulfur fuel oil.

In this process, ammonia and methanol-powered vessels have emerged as the most technically feasible options. Both fuels can leverage existing engine technology while meeting regulatory requirements, and their global supply chains are relatively mature. Consequently, shipping companies have concentrated their orders on these technologies, and Korean shipbuilders have had to reorganize their manufacturing capabilities accordingly.

## Different Order Strategies and Financial Impacts of the Two Shipbuilders

As of October 13, 2026, comparing Samsung Heavy Industries and Hanwha Ocean's 2025 annual results reveals clear differences in their adaptation to regulatory changes.

**Samsung Heavy Industries** recorded KRW 10.7 trillion in 2025 revenue (20.4% YoY growth), KRW 862.2 billion in operating profit, and KRW 535.8 billion in net profit. While its net profit margin of 4.9% is close to industry standards, its broad revenue base means the impact of eco-friendly ship order volatility on overall profitability is relatively cushioned. The company maintains a diversified portfolio of ship types and technologies while gradually responding to ammonia and methanol-powered vessel orders.

**Hanwha Ocean** achieved KRW 12.8 trillion in 2025 revenue (65.2% YoY growth), KRW 1.2 trillion in operating profit, and KRW 1.2 trillion in net profit. Its net profit margin of 13.8% suggests the company is focused on high-margin large-scale projects. Notably, the 65.2% revenue growth reflects shipping companies significantly expanding orders for technology-proven new propulsion vessels under stricter eco-friendly ship regulations. The company's high net profit margin implies that as technical concentration in the order portfolio deepens, volatility could increase.

## Restructuring of Advance Payment Systems and Cash Receipt Timing

Regulatory mandates for eco-friendly vessels have altered shipowners' advance payment patterns. Because ammonia and methanol-powered vessels carry higher technical risk than conventional ships, shipowners demand stricter technical verification at each stage of construction (keel laying, launch, sea trials, etc.). This manifests as either restructuring advance payment disbursements by verification milestone or deferring the timing of advance payments.

As a result, even with orders of similar scale, shipbuilders may face reduced or delayed cash inflows through advance payments. This creates a mismatch between cash needs during construction—such as material purchases, wage payments, and supplier settlements—and the timing of cash inflows. This impact becomes particularly acute for shipbuilders with high revenue growth but lower net margins, translating directly into working capital financing pressure.

## Conclusion: Structural Transition of Shipbuilders amid Regulatory Strengthening

Eco-friendly ship regulations have concentrated Korean shipbuilders' order portfolios on ammonia and methanol-powered vessels, forcing significant restructuring of advance payment systems and cash flow schedules. Companies like Samsung Heavy Industries that maintain diversified order bases and companies like Hanwha Ocean that take large volumes of new-technology orders at high growth rates are exposed to this structural change in different ways.

As long as regulatory requirements persist, shipbuilders will need more sophisticated cash liquidity management, and borrowing terms and advance payment negotiation capability will become key competitive factors. Investors should now conduct detailed monitoring not only of revenue and operating profit trends but also of cash flow indicators such as accounts receivable turnover, advance payment balances, and debt composition.
