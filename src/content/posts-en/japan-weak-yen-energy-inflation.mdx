## The Yen's Trap: The Structural Imbalance Tourism Revenue Cannot Cover

The Japanese yen has continued to weaken, and in the short term it appears to improve tourism income and export competitiveness. Yet for Japan as an energy importer, a weak yen has another side. As import costs surge, corporate profitability is being eroded, and monetary policy faces a dilemma.

## Diverging Signals: Japanese Equities and Energy Sector

**EWJ** (iShares MSCI Japan ETF) closed at $88.26 on November 6, 2026, down 9.31% over the previous month and 8.11% over three months. The range over the past six months was $87.67 to $99.98. **XLE** (State Street Energy Select Sector SPDR ETF), which tracks the energy sector, stood at $61.62 on the same date, down 5.55% over one month, but up 3.00% over three months. XLE's six-month range was $52.31 to $70.15, showing significant volatility.

This comparison carries an important signal. The fact that Japanese equities have fallen more sharply than the energy index suggests that concerns about Japan's economic structure are at play, beyond simple energy price movements.

## Why the Weak Yen Is Pressuring Japanese Companies Now: Four Factors

**1. Absolute rise in import costs**: Japan depends on imports for approximately 88% of its energy. A weak yen increases the competitiveness of Japanese exporters, but simultaneously, the dollar-denominated prices of oil, gas, and minerals are passed through when converted to yen. In particular, energy-intensive industries such as power generation, chemicals, and steel see their profit margins directly eroded.

**2. Weakening grip of monetary policy**: Even if the Bank of Japan attempts to raise rates, excessive increases harm economic growth. If rates do not rise, the yen will continue to weaken and import costs will keep climbing. This vicious cycle narrows policymakers' options.

**3. Tourism revenue is temporary, structural costs are permanent**: The increase in tourists and foreign exchange earnings from the weak yen are real. However, the rise in raw material costs felt by Japanese domestic companies goes unresolved. The tourism sector is only part of GDP, and in a manufacturing-based Japanese economy, cost increases have broad effects.

**4. Globalization of inflation**: Even if global energy prices have stabilized from their peaks, the import inflation Japan experiences is always amplified by the weak yen. Once filtered through the exchange rate, if dollar-based prices rise, yen prices rise by a multiple.

## Japan's Structural Vulnerabilities

**The ceiling on energy self-sufficiency**: Japan's energy self-sufficiency is among the lowest in the OECD. Nuclear power could be a solution, but reactors are being restarted slowly and face strong political resistance. The transition to renewables such as solar and wind requires time. In the short term, reliance on imported energy is entrenched.

**A passive position on exchange rates**: Japan is both an importer and an exporter. However, energy and raw materials are almost entirely imported, and when converted and exported after processing, the exchange rate gain does not fully offset the initial cost increase. Low-margin manufacturers in particular are exposed to this imbalance.

## Conclusion

The weak yen reveals the hidden duality of the Japanese economy. The short-term signals of rising tourism revenue and improved export competitiveness are clear, but the structural burden of surging energy import costs remains. As pressure mounts to normalize monetary policy, Japan's available options appear constrained. The decline in EWJ reflects this tension.
