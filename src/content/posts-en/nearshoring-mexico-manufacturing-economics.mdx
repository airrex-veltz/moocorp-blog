## Tariff Avoidance Costs Reflected in Prices

As the U.S. tightens tariff policy against China, the cost of imported goods rises. Companies seeking to avoid this burden are shifting production bases from China to Mexico. In this process, structural pressure is mounting that drives up prices for Mexican-manufactured goods. This is not a temporary phenomenon but rather a fundamental cost increase resulting from supply chain restructuring.

## Mexico's Position: From Safety Valve to Bottleneck

Mexico is the only large-scale manufacturing country adjacent to the U.S. and granted preferential status under USMCA (U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement). Electronics, automobiles, textiles, and mechanical components are major products, all in direct competition with Chinese goods. When tariffs are imposed on Chinese products and these costs are passed to final prices, Mexico becomes the first alternative sought by U.S. importers. To maintain margins, Mexican products must be supplied at the same price point, but this is not as easy as it sounds.

## Why Are Mexican Manufacturing Prices Rising Now: Four Factors

**1. Cost Passthrough from China Tariffs**: When the U.S. imposes high tariffs on Chinese goods, companies face two choices: absorb the cost and continue importing, or purchase from another country. Among alternatives, Mexico is the best option when considering logistics distance to the U.S., customs convenience, and access to raw materials. This shift in preferences increases demand for Mexican-manufactured goods, and if supply cannot keep pace in the short term, prices rise. As long as China tariffs remain structurally in place, this demand is also structural.

**2. Limitations of USMCA Rules of Origin**: USMCA grants Mexico tariff preferences but imposes rules of origin requirements. For example, automobiles must contain a certain percentage of North American components. Production bases moving to Mexico to avoid China tariffs require time and cost to establish new supply chains. The process of completely abandoning existing Chinese component networks and transitioning to Mexican and U.S. components drives up spot prices.

**3. Transition Costs of Supply Chain Restructuring**: To relocate production, companies must establish new facilities, workforce, and logistics infrastructure. When existing Mexican manufacturing infrastructure—industrial parks, ports, warehouses—becomes suddenly saturated, land lease costs rise, and building new facilities takes years. During this period, supply is constrained and prices increase. This is not a one-time occurrence but part of structural transition.

**4. Labor Supply Bottleneck**: Mexican manufacturing wages are higher than China's. When demand surges, wages rise further. Relocating large-scale manufacturing bases requires skilled workers, which take time to develop. In the short term, companies must attract workers with higher wages, which also drives up prices.

## Why the Flow Continues: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strength: Geopolitical Advantage and Policy Preference.** Mexico cannot compete with China on distance, but its proximity to the U.S. shapes American importers' decisions. As long as tariffs remain on Chinese goods, Mexico's relative advantage remains intact. USMCA is also an exclusive Mexican privilege. With these two factors in place, demand persists even as prices rise.

**Weakness: Limits of Long-Term Competitiveness.** Mexican labor costs are higher than China's, and it lags in infrastructure, technology, and capital. If China tariffs are ever lifted or weakened, production bases moved to Mexico could relocate again. In the long term, price increases may absorb demand and slow the pace of entry into Mexico.

## Conclusion

Changes in tariff policy appear on the surface to raise Chinese goods prices, but their real effect manifests as cost increases at alternative suppliers like Mexico. This represents economic inefficiency across the board: production cannot take place at the cheapest source. The upward price trend for Mexican-manufactured goods is a quantified manifestation of this inefficiency, and as long as U.S. tariff policy remains in place, it appears structurally sustained.
