## Why Refining Margins Flow to China Despite Mining Nationalization

Indonesia enforced mining sector nationalization, yet it is trapped in a structure where refining—the next stage—captures value for Chinese capital. The reality exposes that regaining control over mining extraction alone does not improve Indonesia's position within the supply chain.

## Indonesia's Nickel: The Gap Between Mining and Refining

Indonesia holds the world's largest nickel reserves. Since the 2000s, it has tightened mining extraction conditions and reduced foreign mining companies' stakes. After 2020, it banned unrefined mineral exports, making domestic refining mandatory. Indonesia sought to retain the added value at the mining stage.

Yet mining control does not govern the entire value chain. Converting nickel into refined form requires crossing a high technology barrier. Both wet and dry metallurgy demand massive capital investment, compliance with environmental regulations, and years of technical accumulation. Indonesia lacked domestic capacity at this stage, and China moved quickly to fill that gap.

## Why China Is Now Capturing Refining Margins: Four Factors

**1. Vertical Integration Pressure from Chinese Battery and EV Companies**

Chinese battery makers like CATL and BYD are pursuing raw material sourcing through supply chain integration. As battery demand surges, cost competitiveness at the refining stage determines final product price. The underlying calculation is that compressing margins at refining becomes the entire price competitiveness lever. To Chinese battery companies, Indonesia's refineries are merely raw material cost centers. Pressing margins is the default behavior.

**2. High Technology Barriers in Refining and China's First-Mover Advantage**

Nickel refining has higher technical difficulty than steelmaking or oil refining. Leaching solution purification, metal recovery, and wastewater treatment involve dozens of process variables. China began building refineries in Indonesia in the early 2010s and accumulated over a decade of operational experience. New entrants cannot survive initial batch defects, environmental violations, and yield degradation lasting two to three years. China's prior investment and technical accumulation become a high barrier to entry. Even when Indonesia tries to nurture new refining companies, they lose in the price competition stage to Chinese enterprises.

**3. Indonesia's Government Lack of Capital and Technology**

Indonesia's state-owned mining company, which owns nationalized mines, remains cautious about refining investment. Building a single refinery requires capital of around USD 1 billion, and environmental compliance and technical debt management costs are substantial. When Indonesia chooses Chinese technology partnerships to close technical gaps, processing know-how gradually transfers to Chinese partners through that process. Ultimately, Indonesia controls mining but becomes permanently dependent on China for refining technology.

**4. China's Scale Advantage in the EV Supply Chain**

China controls battery manufacturing, EV production, refining, and mining interests, integrating nickel refineries into its own supply chain vertically. By contrast, Indonesian refineries have customers concentrated in Chinese battery companies and are subject to their price signals and volume coordination. In a structure where scale advantage determines price-setting power, Indonesia is not a negotiating partner but merely a supplier.

## Structural Weakness: Why Resource Abundance Does Not Guarantee Profit

**The Limits of Mining Nationalization**: Controlling the extraction stage does not guarantee added value if processing technology is not controlled; most value goes to the technology holder. Indonesia's seizure of mining aimed to reduce revenue risk from raw material price swings, but refining margins are determined by competitive dynamics.

**Lack of Competitiveness Outside the Chinese Supply Chain**: Without pathways to trade with domestic or third-country battery companies, Indonesian refineries remain unilaterally subject to Chinese price-setting. Securing multiple buyers must be paired with expanding refining capacity itself.

**Time Cost of Technology Catch-Up**: Closing a ten-year technology gap with Chinese enterprises requires at least another five to ten years of independent research investment, workforce development, and regulatory learning. During that period, Indonesia will remain compressed in price competition.

## Conclusion

Mining nationalization represents state recovery of sovereignty over natural resources. Yet in modern supply chains, added value concentrates at stages where technology and scale cluster. If Indonesia does not independently secure refining technology and develop customer bases beyond China, mining nationalization itself will not shift Indonesia's underlying position as a raw material seller. Technological cooperation with China may be necessary, but the first step toward capturing real value is to diversify refining capacity within Indonesia and raise technological independence.
