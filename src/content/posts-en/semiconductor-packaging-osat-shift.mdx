## Packaging Emerges as Core Competitive Factor in Semiconductors

As semiconductors advance to nanometer scales, the importance of packaging technology—which extracts chips from the foundry and connects them to the outside world—is growing dramatically. Packaging, once treated as a subordinate final-stage process, has now become a core competitive factor that determines semiconductor performance itself.

The structural transformation facing OSAT (outsourced semiconductor assembly and test companies) is at the forefront of this trend. Historically, OSAT operated as an intermediary between designers and manufacturers. In that division of labor, designers were handled by fabless firms (design-focused companies), manufacturing by foundries (large-scale production facilities), and packaging by OSATs. But the rise of AI acceleration and chiplets has begun to disrupt that balance.

## Why Technical Competition Has Shifted to Packaging

As semiconductor manufacturing enters sub-3-nanometer processes, heat generation and signal interference per unit area have increased sharply. To leverage chip performance, that heat must be dissipated and signals must flow accurately—and all of this is determined in the packaging stage.

In particular, the proliferation of chiplet architecture has made it essential to combine multiple small chips within a single package. The old method of simply placing a chip and connecting it with wires no longer works. Advanced packaging technologies—2.5D and 3D stacking, ultra-short inter-chip routing, optical interconnects—have become competitive weapons. These technologies require years of R&D and tens of billions of dollars in equipment investment to master.

## Why OSAT is Undergoing Position Changes Now: Four Structural Factors

**1. Intensifying customer concentration**: In the past, small fabless companies dispersed OSAT customers. Recently, however, mega foundries (TSMC, Samsung, Intel) have begun building advanced packaging in-house, and mega fabless firms like NVIDIA are moving to develop packaging technology independently. This concentrates OSAT customers among smaller players.

**2. Technology advancement cuts both ways**: New technologies like chiplets are both opportunity and threat for OSAT. Without advanced packaging capabilities, companies fall behind; even with them, they lose to customer-owned technologies. As entry barriers rise, economies of scale become more important—a death knell for smaller OSATs.

**3. Geopolitical supply chain reconfiguration**: US-China advanced semiconductor restrictions have begun to fragment OSAT selection by region. US customers trust Korean and Taiwanese OSATs but distance themselves from Chinese ones; Chinese customers work to secure domestic alternatives. Technology transfer restrictions between nations split OSAT customer portfolios.

**4. Internalization of customer capabilities**: If large fabless firms develop advanced packaging in-house, or if foundries bundle packaging with their services, OSAT's middleman position shrinks. As vertical integration pressure rises, OSAT must focus on niche technologies.

## Why OSAT Still Exists: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strengths**: Manufacturing experience and economies of scale. Packaging requires not just technology but processing millions of chips monthly. Operational expertise and equipment utilization rates—difficult for newcomers to build—determine profitability. Moreover, even if a firm holds only advanced niche technologies for specific customers (e.g., particular chiplet packaging), that niche can generate a viable business scale.

**Weaknesses**: Declining bargaining power with customers and risk of technological obsolescence. As large customers concentrate, pricing power erodes. Today's advanced technologies become tomorrow's standard within 2–3 years, losing their competitive edge. Alternative technologies (e.g., mainboard packaging) can displace current methods.

## Summary

Semiconductor packaging is no longer a subordinate function—it is the competitive arena. OSAT companies must balance three factors: advanced technology ownership, production capacity, and customer diversification. In this process, smaller players will consolidate or exit, and the industry will likely polarize into a few strong incumbents and specialized niche experts.
