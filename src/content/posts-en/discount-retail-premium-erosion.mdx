## Deteriorating Disposable Income Drives Consumer Shift—Premium Inventory Crisis and Discount Retailers' Victory

US consumers' wallets are tightening. As disposable income stagnates, the middle class is turning its back on premium brands and flocking to discount retailers. This shift is starkly visible in the stock charts of several companies. **COST** (Costco) continues strong growth quarter after quarter, while **TPR** (Tapestry) and **RH** (RH) have seen share prices plummet. This is not a matter of individual company failure but a structural victor and vanquished determined by consumer migration.

## What Three Companies' Recent Results Reveal

**COST** trades at $946.92 as of October 9, 2026, up 2.37% over three months. This year's revenue growth stands at 11.1%, a solid performance. The discount membership model shows resilience even as the economy slows. Consumers maintain store visits because they believe they must make use of their already-paid membership fees.

**TPR** trades at $116.24 on the same date, down 14.29% over three months. This year's revenue growth is still positive at 8.9%, but business conditions are deteriorating. The company's core brands are premium fashion—Coach and Gucci—but weak sales of high-priced merchandise are severe.

**RH** faces a graver situation. Trading at $116.99, it has plunged 27.70% over three months. This year's revenue growth has stalled at just 2.6%. As a luxury furniture and home décor specialist, the company faces compounding headwinds from a weak real estate market and collapsing demand for high-end home goods.

## Why This Trend Is Intensifying: Four Factors

**1. Consumer Confidence Collapse and Disposable Income Cooling**: Inflation and interest rate hikes are eroding consumer psychology. Households with accumulated credit card debt are especially likely to defer or cancel premium purchases. Consumers cut back on "discretionary items"—expensive fashion and furniture—before economizing on staples and groceries. This directly translates into depressed sales for TPR and RH.

**2. Premium Brands Face Inventory Normalization Pressure**: Having expected stronger spring and summer sales last year, premium companies now face warehouses stacked with goods. To maintain brand image while moving inventory, they have no choice but to discount. But the deeper the discount, the greater the margin pressure. TPR's net profit margin is a healthy 19.1%, but the profit margin on marginal sales—the last unit sold—is far lower.

**3. Discount Retailers' Scale Economy Advantage Deepens**: COST maintains 11.1% revenue growth even amid these pressures. Why? Because it has a solid membership base, lower supply chain costs, and private-label products that protect margins. In a weak economy, "getting more for the same money" becomes the value proposition. The roles of discount and premium channels have reversed.

**4. Structural Vulnerability in Luxury Categories**: High-ticket categories like furniture and home décor—RH's domain—are economically sensitive. When fewer households can afford to redecorate, revenues crater. Furniture also suffers from slow inventory turnover. Many products sit unsold for six months or longer. Discounting is difficult; even when prices fall, demand does not return. The cycle becomes vicious.

## Will This Trend Persist: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**COST's strength lies in scale and habit.** Membership is a barrier to entry, and paying customers return. Economic downturns often bring more frequent visits from members (a deepening-discount effect). This year's 11.1% revenue growth reflects that defensive power.

**TPR and RH's weakness is the uncertainty of recovery.** Premium brands struggle to withdraw discounts. Once prices fall, customers expect them to stay down. Raising prices again requires new seasonal collections and fresh marketing investment. Margins face continued pressure in the interim. RH's 2.6% revenue growth is stagnation, and its 27.70% three-month decline suggests the bottom is unknowable.

## Summary

The gap between premium companies and discount retailers is likely to widen. Unless consumer disposable income recovers, demand for low-cost goods and basic products will persist. Membership-based discounters like COST will benefit from this trend, but premium companies like TPR and RH face substantial time needed for inventory normalization and margin recovery.
