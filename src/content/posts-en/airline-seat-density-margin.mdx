## Low-Cost Carriers' Seat Expansion Is Eroding Industry-Wide Profitability

Low-cost airlines are engaged in fierce competition to squeeze one more seat into each aircraft. The result is erosion of profitability across the entire industry. Particularly, full-service carriers with high cost structures are losing ground in price competition, and their profit margins are falling sharply.

## A Quiet but Structural Price War

An airline's revenue structure is simple: distribute the costs of a single aircraft across as many passengers as possible. Low-cost carriers push this logic to the extreme. They place seats closer together and convert cargo space into passenger seating. More revenue per flight is essential to lower the break-even point per aircraft.

As this competition accelerates, the average ticket price across the entire industry falls. **American Airlines (AAL)** closed at $12.94 on the reference date of October 2, 2026, having fallen as low as $10.55 in the past six months. More concerning is that the net profit margin hovers near -0.6%, and despite revenue growing 16.3% year-over-year, it is not translating into profit.

## Why Airlines' Margins Are Eroding Now: Four Factors

**1. Low-cost carriers' seat density expansion competition**: To maximize revenue per aircraft, low-cost carriers accept lower revenue per passenger in exchange for higher total revenue per aircraft. This enables them to pursue more aggressive pricing on their strong routes—typically short-haul, repeat demand segments. To compete on the same routes, full-service carriers must match these price points.

**2. Ticket price convergence and unit revenue decline**: As competition with low-cost carriers intensifies, the average ticket price across the industry converges downward. AAL's revenue growth rate of 16.3% indicates that passenger volume is increasing, but revenue per passenger—revenue passenger kilometer yield—is simultaneously declining.

**3. Sticky cost structure and pricing adaptation failure**: Full-service carriers carry higher baseline costs due to international networks, lounges, in-flight meals, and higher wage scales. When they lower fares to match low-cost carrier price attacks, their own costs do not fall at the same pace. The result is a sharp decline in profit margins.

**4. Asymmetric resilience**: **United Airlines (UAL)** closed at $112.51 on October 2, 2026, maintaining a net profit margin of 5.6%—a stark contrast to AAL's -0.6%. Despite similar revenue growth rates (UAL 16.0%, AAL 16.3%), AAL's profitability has deteriorated far more, suggesting that AAL operates more routes exposed to low-cost carrier competition or has struggled to adjust its cost structure as quickly.

## Full-Service Carriers' Structural Weaknesses and Relative Strengths

**AAL's deterioration**: A net profit margin near zero signals that economies of scale no longer help. When revenue grows but profit lags, a carrier must either overhaul its cost structure or concentrate on high-margin routes. The three-month share price decline of -27.10% indicates the market has already priced in this structural weakness.

**UAL's relative advantage**: Maintaining a 5.6% net profit margin and a P/E of 10.5 signal undervaluation. Within the same full-service carrier category, UAL may be weathering price competition better than AAL or has a higher concentration of high-margin routes—international service and premium-cabin passengers.

## Summary

The seat density competition among low-cost carriers is not merely a price war but a structural reshaping of industry profitability. As competition to maximize revenue per aircraft continues, industry-wide unit revenue must decline. Under this pressure, carriers with high cost structures will inevitably lose margin faster.
