# Europe's Nuclear Revival: Energy Security at the Crossroads Amid Fiscal Strain

Since Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2024, Europe's energy policy has undergone a radical transformation. That countries such as Germany, Belgium, and Austria—which have pursued "nuclear phase-out" policies for decades—are now reassessing nuclear power is more than a simple policy shift; it reflects a strategic choice in the face of an energy security crisis. Yet the path forward is unclear. A complex web of fiscal burdens, technological risks, and political resistance clouds the way.

## Unstable Supply Chains Call for Nuclear Energy

The most fundamental reason Europe has begun to reconsider nuclear energy is **energy supply security**. The cutoff of Russian gas caused natural gas prices in Europe to hit all-time highs in 2022, and each country's anxiety over energy self-sufficiency became acute. At the same time, to meet carbon neutrality targets, countries accelerated renewable energy expansion, but the intermittency problem of wind and solar power came to the fore. Shortfalls in winter wind generation and reduced solar output on cloudy days rekindled awareness of the need for baseload power.

This is where nuclear energy returned to the political table. Nuclear energy, with its high capacity factor and zero carbon emissions, is a key low-carbon baseload power source that can complement renewables. France stands as a prime example of energy independence secured through nuclear power, and this has carried strong persuasive force amid the energy security crisis.

## Political Realignment and Capital Signals

After 2024, the European Union's policy direction underwent a practical shift. By recognizing both natural gas and nuclear energy as "transitional low-carbon technologies" in the EU taxonomy, institutional capital has begun to take notice of nuclear energy companies. In particular, government subsidies and investment agreements for small modular reactor (SMR) technology development and lifetime extensions of existing plants have increased.

The uranium sector has responded sensitively to these policy signals. Looking at the case of the Global X Uranium ETF (URA), its closing price as of September 15, 2026 was 41.77 USD, but given the 6-month range of 37.18–58.97 USD, volatility has been considerable. The 3-month price change of -13.09% reflects recent price adjustments and reveals market concern about project implementation delays and rising costs, despite the positivity of policy signals.

More notable is the change in performance of companies involved in nuclear equipment and technology. **BWX Technologies (BWXT) recorded a decline of -24.24% over three months, priced at 146.70 USD as of September 15, 2026.** It is paradoxical that a nuclear-related technology company with a market capitalization of 1.34 billion USD has shown such a sharp decline. However, revenue growth of 18.0% (YoY) and net profit margin of 10.1% still suggest a solid foundation. A PER of 38.0 implies high embedded expectations, and the current decline shows that those expectations are adjusting in both timing and scale of realization.

## Finance and Technology: The Weight of Challenges

The largest obstacle to nuclear power reinvestment is **fiscal burden**. Lifetime extensions of existing plants require substantial modernization costs. New plant construction demands over 10 years of construction time and tens of billions of euros in upfront capital. EU member states' government finances are already under strain from pandemic management and inflation control. As individual nations find it difficult to bear these costs, discussions of joint funding mechanisms and long-term government-backed bonds have intensified.

Technological risks remain. Small modular reactors are still in the early stages of commercialization, and cost efficiency has not been proven to match expectations. Existing nuclear technology carries long-term challenges such as waste disposal and safety upgrades. Political resistance persists. Austria, Ireland, and Luxembourg continue to oppose nuclear energy expansion, and public acceptance varies unevenly by region.

## An Uncertain Future, Complex Choices

Ultimately, Europe's nuclear policy finds itself in a state of **tension between the imperative "we must" and the reality "can we?"** Double objectives of energy security and carbon neutrality appear difficult to achieve without nuclear energy. Yet within the constraints of finance, technology, and politics, it remains unclear how quickly and broadly those objectives can be realized.

Investors' attention reflects this uncertainty directly. While the long-term policy shift in energy is evident, skepticism about near-term feasibility and concern over project cost overruns are manifesting as price volatility. Whether Europe takes one path or another at this crossroads depends on policy decisions and capital mobilization outcomes over the next one to two years.
