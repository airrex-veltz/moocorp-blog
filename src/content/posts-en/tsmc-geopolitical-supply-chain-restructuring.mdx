## Semiconductor Supply Concerns and Government Support Shake TSMC's Monopoly

TSMC's long-held monopolistic position is fracturing under government subsidies and customer pressure for supply chain diversification. The company, which once bound customers through superior technology and reliable supply in the semiconductor industry, is now beginning to cede market share to competitors like Intel and Samsung.

## From TSMC Monopoly to an Era of Diversification

TSMC commanded over 50% of global semiconductor production capacity in the mid-2010s. Particularly in cutting-edge processes (5nm, 3nm), it maintained a technological gap competitors struggled to close. Customers had no choice but to depend on TSMC for their chip designs, and even amid supply shortages, TSMC wielded pricing power. However, the situation changed after 2022.

TSMC's 2024 global market share in advanced processes declined to approximately 60–65%. While absolute dominance remains, the 5–10 percentage point drop from the mid-70% level a few years ago signals structural industry change. As customer unease translates into action, Intel and Samsung are seizing the opportunity.

## Dual Pressure: Geopolitical Risk and Government Support

**1. US CHIPS Act and the Materialization of Financial Support**

Intel has secured approximately USD 20 billion in CHIPS Act funding from the US government. Using this, it is building advanced fabs at scale in Arizona, Ohio, and New Mexico. While cost efficiency lags TSMC, the increased preference of the US government and customer companies for "domestic supply chains" gives Intel competitive advantage with merely state-of-the-art process capability. Intel still lags TSMC in processes below 7nm, but its yield improvement rate is accelerating.

**2. South Korea's Samsung Support Policy and Global Semiconductor Strategy**

Samsung, backed by South Korean government support, is modernizing existing fabs in Yongin and Pyeongtak while expanding abroad (Texas). Notably, Samsung's 3nm process has reached parity with TSMC's N3, and it already possesses mass-production competitiveness at 5nm. Samsung enjoys government backing that views semiconductor export capacity as a strategic industry priority and is aggressive in customer development.

**3. Intensifying Supply Chain Diversification Pressure**

Following the 2020–2021 chip shortage, major customers like Apple, Qualcomm, and Samsung Electronics began worrying about supply chain concentration risk. As the realization spread that "dependence on a single company is untenable," customers began diversifying their design portfolios across Intel and Samsung in addition to TSMC. While this amounts to paying a form of "insurance," it is a rational decision given geopolitical risks (Taiwan concerns, US–China tensions).

**4. Convergence of Advanced Process Technology**

Both Intel and Samsung have accelerated their process node cadence over the past 2–3 years. Intel has disclosed its Intel 4 (formerly 7nm), Intel 3, and Intel 20A roadmap, while Samsung is developing 2nm. While not a complete catch-up, the notion that "TSMC is the only option" is weakening. Customers now have the luxury of balancing available capacity, price, and geopolitical stability over process node minutiae.

## Recalibration of Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strength: The Technology and Yield Gap Persists**

TSMC still maintains an 18-month-plus gap over rivals in its most advanced processes (3nm, 2nm). Its yield improvement curve is also the steepest in the industry. When customers demand "maximum performance and power efficiency," TSMC remains essential. TSMC is also the primary beneficiary of the AI chip boom.

**Weakness: Customer Concentration and Geopolitical Exposure**

TSMC's top 10 customers account for over 60% of total revenue. Fluctuations in orders from a specific customer (e.g., Apple) can significantly swing results. More pressing is the geopolitical risk embodied by Taiwan. As US–China tensions intensify, customers are accelerating supply chain diversification. Policy shifts (e.g., advanced chip export restrictions to China) also amplify business uncertainty for TSMC.

**Weakness: Margin Pressure and Eroding Pricing Power**

As customers spread orders across Intel and Samsung, TSMC's negotiating position weakened. Customers have begun wielding the "alternative suppliers exist" card to demand price cuts. Investor concern is growing that advanced process margins could compress into single digits.

## Conclusion

TSMC remains the strongest player in the semiconductor industry. However, as government support, supply chain diversification pressure, and technology convergence align, its monopolistic advantage faces gradual erosion. Over the coming years, expect market share loss, margin compression, and increased customer bargaining power. How TSMC responds to these challenges—through accelerated technology investment, customer relationship diversification, and government relations enhancement—will reshape the global semiconductor landscape.
