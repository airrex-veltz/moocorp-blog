## Structural Bottleneck in EV Battery Supply Chain

Rising EV sales worldwide are straining battery material supply. As nickel and cobalt supply fails to keep pace with demand, battery manufacturers face rising costs and supply disruptions. This is not merely a temporary shortage but a long-standing constraint created by structural limitations in mining and refining infrastructure.

## Why Nickel and Cobalt Are Essential

Nickel (Ni) and cobalt (Co) are core materials in lithium-ion batteries. Nickel is particularly critical for increasing energy density to extend driving range, while cobalt ensures chemical stability. EV batteries require over 50 kg of nickel per vehicle.

Today, most global nickel production is concentrated in a few countries—Indonesia, the Philippines, and Russia, among others. Cobalt is similarly dominated by the Democratic Republic of the Congo, which accounts for most global production. This geographic concentration is a structural factor that amplifies supply risk.

## Why Shortages Are Intensifying Now: Four Factors

**1. Explosive Battery Demand From Surging EV Sales**

As the EV market sustains double-digit annual growth, battery demand is rising exponentially. Battery pack manufacturing volume in a single year now exceeds the entire previous year. Traditional automakers are cutting internal combustion engine production and shifting to EV platforms, while multiple manufacturers simultaneously compete for battery materials. Supply chains structurally struggle to keep pace with this rapid demand surge.

**2. Long Development Cycles in Mining Infrastructure**

Nickel and cobalt mines undergo a long development cycle—exploration, permitting, and infrastructure setup—of five years or more once operations begin. Raw material prices cannot be instantly ramped up in response. Moreover, new mine development is capital-intensive, with initial investment reaching billions of dollars. Even when material shortages are foreseeable, actual output increases lag by years.

**3. Technical and Environmental Constraints on Refining Capacity**

Refining raw ore into battery-grade high-purity material requires advanced technology and environmental management capabilities. Hydrometallurgical and ultra-high-temperature smelting processes have high technical barriers to entry, and expanding refining facilities takes years. Nickel refining also has significant environmental impact. Major producing countries like Indonesia have suspended mining and refining operations due to stricter environmental regulations, further constraining supply.

**4. Geopolitical Risk and Supply Route Fragility**

Political and economic instability in high-concentration nickel-producing countries, trade conflicts, and export restrictions can block supply routes themselves. For cobalt, mining conditions in the Congo shift rapidly with geopolitical changes. Supply disruptions or customs delays to major battery manufacturing bases—such as China—are also possible. Such geopolitical risks cannot be fully hedged by long-term supply contracts alone.

## How Long Will These Structural Constraints Persist?

**Structural Persistence of Supply Shocks**

The four factors above have structural characteristics that cannot be resolved in the short term. Mining development cycles and refining capacity expansion require a minimum of 3–5 years, while EV sales growth is faster. Thus, supply-demand gaps are likely to persist for some time.

**Cascading Effects of Rising Battery Prices**

Rising material costs increase battery manufacturing expenses, which are passed through to final EV prices. Higher prices can slow EV adoption, which paradoxically helps ease battery supply pressures, but from a consumer perspective EV transitions are delayed and competitive dynamics shift. In price-sensitive markets, a vicious cycle may emerge where internal combustion vehicles continue to sell.

**Potential of Recycling and Alternative Materials**

As early EV batteries reach end-of-life, a recovery and recycling market is expected to grow. At the same time, development of alternative chemistries that reduce or eliminate nickel and cobalt content—such as LFP and sodium-ion—is underway. These can mitigate supply shortages over the long term, but they do not solve near-term supply-demand problems in today's dominant battery type (ternary).

## Summary

EV battery material shortages are not a temporary phenomenon but a structural constraint: mining and refining infrastructure cannot keep pace with EV demand's rapid growth. Geographic concentration of nickel and cobalt, long development cycles, high technical barriers, and geopolitical risks interact to create supply pressures likely to persist for years. This manifests as rising battery costs and adjusted EV production; only long-term measures—diversifying supply chains and accelerating recycling and alternative material development—can unlock this bottleneck.
