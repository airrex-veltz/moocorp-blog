# Wafer Fab Utilization and Capital Discipline Cycle Shift

Semiconductor wafer fab utilization rates have fallen below 70%. This marks the lowest level since the mid-2010s. Yet in this environment, leading foundries and memory manufacturers like TSMC, Samsung, and Intel are actually expanding capital expenditures. This gap is not merely a cyclical downturn but an inevitable consequence of structural characteristics and competitive dynamics in the semiconductor industry.

## The Fate of Lead-Time Investment Industries

Semiconductor manufacturing is fundamentally an industry where past decisions define the present. Wafer fab construction alone takes three to five years, and reaching full production capacity requires an additional one to two years of process optimization. Due to the nature of foundry contracts, this means investment decisions must be based on pure expectations without confirmed customer demand.

In a phase where fabs that broke ground during past periods of optimistic demand forecasts are now coming online, each company faces two choices: halt investment or complete what was planned. And reality consistently chooses the latter.

## The Competitive Arms Race Trap

The reason is clear. Once a wafer fab is completed, it must operate for at least 20 years to break even. The cost of stopping and later restarting construction far exceeds completing it from the start. More importantly, there is competitive positioning at stake.

If TSMC stops investing, Samsung will invest. If Samsung stops, Intel will invest. Since they compete over future customer demand from companies like Apple, Qualcomm, and AMD, one company abandoning capacity expansion is equivalent to abandoning negotiating leverage with customers. Thus, individual companies are rationally forced into what appears to be irrational choices.

## The Margin Pressure Chain

Utilization below 70% means fixed costs are not being sufficiently recovered. Semiconductor wafer fabs have high fixed cost dependency, making declining utilization rates cause per-unit production costs to spike.

Additional completed equipment in this environment only increases supply. In the short term, it further depresses utilization and squeezes margins. However, in the long term, it is a necessary choice. This is because if demand recovers and equipment capacity is unavailable, all that growth must be surrendered to competitors.

## The Period of Structural Disparity

How long this situation persists depends on the pace of demand recovery. Had the AI semiconductor cycle not overheated, conditions might be better. But having already experienced an overheating phase, demand normalization—rather than recovery—is likely to require several more quarters.

In the meantime, leading players must tolerate low utilization and margin pressure. At the same time, they must complete planned investments. This is not an anomaly but rather normal behavior within the semiconductor industry's capital discipline framework.

## The Path to Demand Recovery

What matters is that utilization below 70% is not permanent. This is a cyclical phase. Given sufficient time, accumulated equipment oversupply will be absorbed and new demand will emerge, normalizing utilization. Until that point arrives, margins for leading players will remain pressured, and equipment competition will continue.

This is the essence of the semiconductor cycle. Independent of individual corporate intent, path dependence exists that the structure enforces, and few companies can escape it.
