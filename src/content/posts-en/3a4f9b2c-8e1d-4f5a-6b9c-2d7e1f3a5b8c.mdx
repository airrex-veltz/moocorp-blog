## Fertilizer Price Wedge Collapses Sugar Margins

Brazil's sugar and bioethanol producers' margins are sinking to historic lows. The cause is not simple. Export prices are weak, but domestic fertilizer costs have become entrenched in an upward trend. In particular, the impact of input materials such as phosphate—where global supply is concentrated in a few countries—is significant.

## Brazil's Sugar and Ethanol Structure and Fertilizer Dependency

Brazil is the world's largest sugar supplier. From the same sugarcane fields, both sugar and bioethanol are extracted simultaneously. This structure appears to enjoy economies of scale, but in reality, when both products' global prices fall together, the region faces an unavoidable double squeeze.

Sugarcane cultivation is land-intensive and requires heavy applications of phosphate and potassium fertilizers. The reddish soils of the Northeast Paraíba region have low natural phosphate deposits, creating high dependence on artificial fertilizers. In an agricultural structure where fertilizer is a major cost item, fertilizer price increases directly translate to profitability deterioration.

## Why Margins Are Reversing Now: Four Factors

**1. Structural oligopoly in the phosphate market**: Half of the world's phosphate mineral reserves are concentrated in Morocco. India and China are also major producers, but with large domestic demand or export restriction policies, global supply elasticity is fundamentally constrained. When currency fluctuations or geopolitical tensions arise, the bargaining power of import-dependent countries like Brazil weakens sharply. Fertilizer companies can pass cost increases directly to buyers.

**2. Chinese low-cost alternative fertilizers and quality differences**: Over the past decade, China has significantly expanded chemical fertilizer production capacity and exports large volumes to Southeast Asia. However, Brazil's sugarcane is sensitive to specific micronutrient supplies (e.g., zinc and boron). Low-cost Chinese fertilizers alone can result in harvest shortfalls, eventually requiring mixed use of premium products—a vicious cycle. Prices don't fall while options shrink.

**3. Currency and emerging market cost bottlenecks**: The Brazilian real has sustained weakness against the dollar over recent years. When import fertilizer dollar prices rise, local farmers' local currency costs increase doubly. Meanwhile, sugar and bioethanol sales revenues are also dollar-denominated, but global oversupply keeps dollar prices themselves weak. With both costs and revenues in dollars, exchange rate risk accumulates in one direction.

**4. Simultaneous global sugar and ethanol price weakness**: India's sugar production recovery, uncertainty in U.S. bioethanol policy, and EU fiscal austerity dampening imports combine to push both commodities into prolonged global price weakness. Brazilian suppliers have no market power to raise prices, relying solely on cost-cutting to maintain profitability, yet input costs are locked in regional monopoly structures with no room to decline.

## Structural Weakness: The Paradox of Economies of Scale

**The comparative advantage trap**: Brazil possesses absolute advantage in sugar cultivation through land, climate, and infrastructure. Yet this creates oversupply. In an era of global low prices, larger producers face proportionally larger absolute losses.

**Input cost fixation**: Core input materials like phosphate cannot be fully substituted by other agricultural products. Maintaining sugarcane quality requires specific fertilizer combinations, and the global price of those fertilizers is beyond the control of Brazilian farmers. The cost structure is locked in.

**The paradox of scale**: Large-scale farming requires larger fixed input volumes (machinery, labor, base fertilizer quantities), making it harder to exceed breakeven when product prices fall. This is a structural limitation of Brazil's sugarcane industry, which lacks small farms.

## Summary

Brazil's sugar and bioethanol exporters now face a historically rare situation. Production advantages and global market share remain, but the absolute difference between costs and revenues has reversed. Short term, expanded alternative fertilizers and improving Chinese quality will gradually intensify input material competition. However, as long as geopolitical bottleneck materials like phosphate remain, the margin pressure structure in emerging market agriculture will not be fundamentally resolved.
