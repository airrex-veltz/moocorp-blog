## Rare Earth Wealth: Africa and Southeast Asia Break China's 70% Processing Monopoly

As Tanzania's new mining projects and Vietnam's refining facilities come online, the global rare earth supply chain that China has controlled for 30 years is shifting toward a new balance. This is not merely mining news. It signals that geopolitical control over the resources driving EV batteries, semiconductors, and defense industries is being redrawn in real time.

## China's Rare Earth System: The Weight of Over 70% Processing Share

Rare earths are a collective term for 17 chemical elements essential to electric vehicle motors, wind turbine generators, and defense radars because of their powerful magnetic, luminescent, and catalytic properties. While over 60% of global rare earth mining occurs in China, Vietnam, and Myanmar, China's leverage lies in **processing (refining) capacity**. As of 2024, China handles approximately 70% of global rare earth refining—a level that surpasses the combined refining capacity of the United States, Japan, and Europe.

The issue is economic. Rare earth refining is chemically complex and environmentally damaging, which developed nations have avoided. China consolidated control over the global refining industry in the early 2000s by relaxing environmental regulations and cutting labor costs. In doing so, it acquired a supply-cutting card. In 2010, it used rare earth export restrictions to manage tensions with Japan, and it has continued to wield "refining cutoffs" as an implicit threat in recent semiconductor competition between the United States and Japan.

## Why Are Tanzania and Vietnam Moving Now: Four Pressures and Drivers

**1. U.S. Supply Chain Realignment Pressure and the CHIPS Act**: Since the Biden administration, the U.S. has elevated rare earth supply chain self-reliance to a national security priority. The CHIPS Act and the Inflation Reduction Act have allocated billions of dollars to building U.S. refining facilities. Simultaneously, the U.S. has pressed allies to develop "non-China sources." Tanzania is actively negotiating mineral trade agreements with the U.S., while Vietnam, participating in Quad supply chain cooperation, has secured commitments for refining technology transfer.

**2. EV Demand Explosion and Supply Uncertainty**: Global EV production is projected to grow from 14 million units in 2024 to an annual average rate of 15% growth through 2027. A single battery cell requires 5–10 grams of rare earth elements. If China artificially restricts supply, battery prices worldwide will spike. Major automakers including Tesla, BMW, and Hyundai have accelerated supply chain diversification, resulting in purchase commitments for Tanzania's project.

**3. Tanzania's Manyoni Peak Project and Vietnam's Refining Capacity Expansion**: New rare earth mining at Tanzania's Manyoni Peak has been ramping up since the second half of 2025. At 50,000 tons annually, this represents 5–7% of current global mining output. Simultaneously, Vietnam is expanding its refining cluster near Ho Chi Minh City, planning to increase refining capacity to 120,000 tons annually by 2026. This equals half of China's current refining capacity.

**4. Environmental and Labor Standards Shifts and Cost Structure Transition**: China's rare earth refining plants were built to 1980s–2000s standards, and recent environmental regulation tightening and rising labor costs are worsening profitability. Meanwhile, Vietnam and Tanzania are adopting new refining technologies (hydrothermal leaching and solvent extraction), reducing water usage by 40%—a development attracting ESG investors. The possibility of a reversal in economic viability has emerged.

## Why China's 'Rare Earth Card' Isn't Completely Broken: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Enduring Strengths**: China still maintains absolute dominance in mining. Inner Mongolia has abundant naturally occurring deposits, and Jiangxi's ion adsorption mining technology is world-class. As of 2026, China's mining output still accounts for 35–40% of global supply. Additionally, China has 30 years of accumulated vertical integration and supply chain track record in refining.

**Deepening Weaknesses**: Yet the peak of processing monopoly has passed. Non-China refining capacity—from Tanzania, Vietnam, Malaysia, and others—is expected to reach 25–30% of global output by 2027. This will shrink China's regulatory authority to below half and erode the credibility of supply-cutting threats. Especially as the U.S. and Europe treat rare earths as "security items" and invest in expanding domestic refining capacity, the likelihood is high that within five years, "rare earth price-setting power" will shift to a multipolar structure.

## Summary

The reordering of the rare earth supply chain will become pronounced from 2026–2027 onward. China's monopoly will not disappear entirely, but its control at the refining stage is likely to fall from 70% to 50% or below by 2030. This will fundamentally alter transaction structures in the EV, semiconductor, and defense industries. From an investor perspective, this is the moment to monitor both the expanding refining margins of non-China rare earth companies and the eroding price competitiveness of Chinese refiners.
