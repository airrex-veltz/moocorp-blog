# EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) Year One: Compliance Cost Analysis

## Introduction: The Hidden Tax of Decarbonization

The European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) is set to take effect officially in October 2025. After a year-long transition period, steel, cement, and aluminum exporters are facing a new reality. What is unfolding is not merely a regulatory shift but the operationalization of a mechanism that will fundamentally restructure the economic foundations of global supply chains.

The core of CBAM is straightforward. The EU protects investments by domestic producers in carbon reduction while imposing equivalent tariffs on imports from outside the bloc that fail to meet low-carbon standards. This represents an attempt to extend the EU's regulatory standards for carbon reduction across borders in an increasingly competitive global decarbonization race.

As a result, the economics of the global materials industry are shifting drastically.

---

## CBAM Year One: The Transition Period Reality

### Transition Structure and Preliminary Reporting Framework

CBAM began in October 2023 with a preliminary reporting phase. Importers into the EU must declare the actual carbon content of steel, cement, aluminum, fertilizers, and electricity entering the bloc. No tariffs have been imposed yet, but during this period companies had to establish carbon accounting and tracking systems.

From October 2025, the transition period ends and the compliance phase begins. Importers must purchase CBAM certificates on a monthly or quarterly basis, with prices linked to the EU's emissions trading system (ETS) quotation. Given that current ETS prices fluctuate in the range of EUR 80–100 per tonne, the import costs for carbon-intensive products are expected to rise significantly.

### Data Collection Impact Over the Transition Year

The most important outcome over the year-long transition has been data standardization. Eurostat and the European Commission have collected approximately 17,000 CBAM reports. This has revealed the following:

- **Steel products**: 1.8–2.2 tonnes CO₂ equivalent per tonne on average (ranging from 1.2–3.5 tonnes by country)
- **Cement**: 0.6–0.8 tonnes CO₂ per tonne
- **Primary aluminum**: 12–15 tonnes CO₂ equivalent per tonne
- **Fertilizer (nitrogen-based)**: 1.5–2.0 tonnes CO₂ per tonne

These figures are not mere estimates but actual reported values. Based on this data, the EU has determined the calculation formula for October 2025 onward.

## Year One Compliance Costs: Real Calculations

### Steel Industry: EUR 144–176 per Tonne in Additional Costs

Steel is the largest impact sector for CBAM. Approximately 40% of steel imported into the EU comes from China, India, Russia, and Turkey.

**Base calculation:**
- Average carbon content: 2.0 tonnes CO₂/tonne
- Current ETS price: EUR 90/tonne CO₂
- CBAM certificate cost: 2.0 × 90 = **EUR 180/tonne**
- Reference spot steel price: EUR 600–700/tonne
- **Cost burden ratio: 25–30%**

Data from the transition period shows that exporters from low-carbon-intensity countries (such as Iceland and Norway with abundant hydropower) needed CBAM certificates at only EUR 50–80 per tonne. Conversely, exporters from countries relying on coal-based power generation had to pay up to EUR 200–250 per tonne.

U.S. steel (including Nucor and U.S. Steel) was also reported based on average U.S. carbon content, which came in slightly higher than the EU average at 1.9–2.1 tonnes CO₂/tonne.

### Cement Industry: Structural Vulnerability

Cement has lower carbon content than steel but higher transport costs and stronger regional characteristics, making CBAM's relative impact somewhat less pronounced. However, for low-cost cement exporters from Africa, India, and Southeast Asia, the situation is different.

- **Average carbon content**: 0.7 tonnes CO₂/tonne
- **CBAM certificate cost**: 0.7 × 90 = **EUR 63/tonne**
- **Reference CIF EU cement price**: EUR 100–130/tonne
- **Cost burden ratio: 48–63%**

This represents a larger burden than steel, because cement's absolute price is lower. As a result, cement exporters from Turkey, Egypt, and Vietnam face pressure to raise prices or reduce exports.

### Aluminum: The Worst Case

Primary aluminum is the most carbon-intensive.

- **Average carbon content**: 13 tonnes CO₂/tonne (including refining processes)
- **CBAM certificate cost**: 13 × 90 = **EUR 1,170/tonne**
- **Reference spot aluminum price**: EUR 2,200–2,500/tonne
- **Cost burden ratio: 47–53%**

This is extreme. Particularly for aluminum smelters in China, India, and Papua New Guinea, EU export competitiveness will be lost unless they transition to low-carbon technology.

A key observation from transition period reports: low-carbon aluminum (hydropower-based from Australia) was at around 4–6 tonnes CO₂ per tonne. This is only one-half to one-third of standard aluminum. Therefore, the price premium for low-carbon aluminum can be justified.

## Export Nations Face Pressure: Sequential Contraction

### Country-Level Impact Mapping

**High-impact countries:**
- China: 15–20% of EU's share in 200 million tonnes/year of steel exports and 10 million tonnes/year of aluminum exports
- India: 10–15% of EU's share in 10 million tonnes/year of steel exports and 15 million tonnes/year of cement exports
- Turkey: 40% of EU's share in 6 million tonnes/year of steel exports and 30% of EU's share in 5 million tonnes/year of cement exports
- Russia: Currently unaffected due to sanctions, but facing indirect pressure from intensified competition for alternative Asian export markets

**Protected countries:**
- Australia: Low-carbon technology base (wind and hydropower)
- Iceland and Norway: Hydropower-based energy
- United States: Not yet subject to CBAM (under WTO bilateral negotiations)

### Export Price Decline and Supply Chain Restructuring

Anticipated developments following the completion of the transition period in year one:

1. **Loss of price competitiveness**: Turkish and Indian steel will lose an approximately EUR 30–50 per tonne price advantage. This provides a protective effect for EU steelmakers such as ArcelorMittal and ThyssenKrupp.

2. **Narrowed choices for Africa and South Asia**: Exporters losing EU market access will need to pivot to China, Southeast Asia, and Middle East markets. However, price competition in these markets is intense.

3. **Formation of low-carbon premium**: Australian and Norwegian low-carbon products can sustain a price premium of EUR 50–100 per tonne. This reinforces incentives for suppliers to invest in carbon reduction.

## Market Implications and Investment Positioning

### Beneficiary: EU-Based Steel and Materials Producers

**ArcelorMittal (MT)**
- EU steel production capacity: approximately 55 million tonnes/year
- Current carbon intensity: 1.8 tonnes CO₂/tonne (close to EU average)
- Margin improvement possible from 2025 onward through CBAM protection effects
- Risk: Chinese low-cost aggressive tactics, Middle East new capacity entry

**Heidelberg Materials (HEI.DE) - Cement**
- EU cement production capacity: 180 million tonnes/year
- Clear protective effect within the region
- However, heightened competition from African imported cement warrants attention

### Disadvantaged: Smelters in Export-Oriented Countries

**Nippon Steel & Sumitomo Metal (Japan)**
- Japanese steel has relatively low carbon intensity (1.6 tonnes CO₂/tonne)
- Limited CBAM impact, though relative price competitiveness will weaken

**Chinese Steel (Baosteel, CNPC-affiliated)**
- 20–30% cost increase expected for CBAM-affected exports
- Near term: Price reductions in response, margin pressure
- Long term: Investment in low-carbon technology required

## Conclusion: Regulation-Driven Structural Change

The CBAM year-one transition period was a time for data collection and corporate adaptation. From October 2025 onward, as the compliance phase takes effect:

1. **Tarification becomes real**: CBAM certificates linked to ETS prices become actual costs.
2. **Supply chain restructuring**: Low-carbon product premiums strengthen and exports from high-carbon countries decelerate.
3. **Investment opportunity**: Margin improvement for EU-based steel and materials producers and premium expansion for low-carbon technology leaders will be key investment themes.

If current carbon prices (EUR 80–100 per tonne) are sustained, additional steel costs will settle at 25–30%, cement at 48–63%, and aluminum at 47–53%. This is not merely a regulatory burden but an inflection point redefining the economics of the global materials industry.

Investors must now evaluate not only supply-demand dynamics and price but also carbon intensity and corporate CBAM readiness.
