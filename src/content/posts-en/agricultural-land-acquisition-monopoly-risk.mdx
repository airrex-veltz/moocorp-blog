## Why Institutional Investors' Agricultural Land Monopolization Is Becoming the Seed of a Global Food Crisis

Large asset managers and private equity funds are acquiring agricultural land on a massive scale in Canada, Australia, and Ukraine. It is not merely asset ownership but rather a process of excluding farmers and building financialized revenue structures where global food security is being threatened. **DBA** (agricultural commodities ETF), fertilizer companies **AGU** and **CF**, and other beneficiaries are increasingly positioned to profit from supply distortions within this restructured system of land ownership.

## Rapid Financialization of Agricultural Land Ownership

Over the past three years, global agricultural land M&A has grown at an annual rate of 7–10%. Canadian farmland prices have risen 40% since 2020, and international funds have begun entering in earnest as Ukraine rebuilds its farmland after the war. According to U.S. Department of Agriculture data, over 8% of U.S. farmland is already foreign-owned, with one-third of that purchased within the last five years. In Australia, 12% of the grain belt acreage is owned by Asian funds. These institutional investors are pursuing hybrid operating models that combine "seasonal revenue generation" and "futures hedging."

## Why Agricultural Land Monopolization Is Accelerating Now: Four Factors

**1. Strengthened Status of Agricultural Land as an Inflation Hedge Asset**: The persistence of high interest rates by global central banks and weakening currency values have driven surging demand for tangible assets. Agricultural land is recording total returns of 8–12%, comprising annual capital appreciation of 3–5% plus agricultural leasehold rental income of 5–7%. Against the backdrop of bonds yielding 4–5%, farmland holds overwhelming appeal. Major global funds have begun allocating 3–8% of their target asset allocation to agricultural land.

**2. Intensified Supply Vigilance After the Ukraine Crisis**: Ukraine accounts for 12% of global wheat exports and 40% of sunflower oil exports. Since Russia's invasion in 2022, supply instability has become structural, and global food companies and funds have been strategically securing farmland in Ukraine and Kazakhstan under the pretext of "supply chain security." Eastern European farmland prices collapsed 40% immediately after the war but recovered 70% within two years, with institutional investor acquisitions as the key driver of price increases.

**3. Vertical Integration of Global Fertilizer and Seed Supply Chains**: **CF** (CF Industries) and **AGU** (Agrium) are pursuing control over agricultural production costs in an era of rising fertilizer prices. As the integration of farmland ownership and leasing structures grows, they can directly manage demand for fertilizer and seeds. Over the past two years, fertilizer companies have acquired over 60 mid-sized farmland portfolios, and through standardization of farming methods, they are creating "essential demand" for their products.

**4. Combining Futures Market Profit Strategies**: Fund structures have emerged that simultaneously operate actual farmland ownership and agricultural commodity futures short-selling positions. They control actual cultivation volume while simultaneously shorting in the futures market assuming supply shortages, seeking arbitrage profit. The reason major institutional short positions in the corn futures market hit all-time highs (net 5 million contracts) in the first half of 2024 is precisely this structure. **DBA** and similar commodity index funds generate returns from this volatility.

## Yet the Weaknesses of Food Monopolization: Structural Traps

**Technology Gap and Declining Cultivation Efficiency**: Agricultural land acquired by institutional investors tends to show lower returns than local farms. This is due to management know-how gaps, insufficient soil management, and lack of regional climate adaptation capability. Among funds that acquired Ukrainian farmland, 20% abandoned cultivation within two years and converted to pure leasing.

**Geopolitical Risk and Regulatory Pressure**: Governments around the world have begun regulating foreign institutional investors' agricultural land monopolization. Canada pursued a ban on foreign farmland purchases in 2023, and Australia is expanding protective measures through designation of "strategically important agricultural land." India and Vietnam have also codified limits on the percentage of foreign ownership of farmland.

**Long-Term Grain Price Weakness Risk**: As climate technologies and AI-based precision agriculture mature, the risk of global grain supply oversupply intensifies. Agricultural land that institutional investors entered based on assumptions of supply constraints risks being undervalued in an era of supply surplus.

## Summary

Financialization of agricultural land guarantees short-term returns for fertilizer companies and commodity index funds but deepens global food system instability. If the supply constraint scenario collapses due to regulation and technological progress, institutional investors' farmland portfolios cannot avoid valuation losses. As the conflict between food security and financial returns intensifies, government regulation will certainly grow stronger.
