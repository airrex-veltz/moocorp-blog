## The Southeast Asian Semiconductor Boom Created by US-China Tech Competition

As technology export restrictions on China tighten, semiconductor manufacturers are flocking to Southeast Asia. **TSMC**, **Samsung**, and **Intel** factories in Singapore, Vietnam, and Thailand go beyond simple manufacturing facilities—they are restructuring the region's entire economic landscape.

## The Great Migration of Tech Supply Chains

**TSMC** has built research and development and testing centers in Ho Chi Minh City and Da Nang since 2023, while **Samsung** invested $3.5 billion in Binh Phuoc Province, Vietnam in 2024 to begin advanced memory chip production. **Intel** has strengthened its Asian presence following recent reductions at its Illinois facility. These investments reflect the convergence of US semiconductor purchasing power security, Japan's Chips Act matching funds, and EU processor self-sufficiency strategies. Over the past three years, semiconductor-related FDI into Southeast Asia has exceeded $20 billion.

## Why Southeast Asia Now: Four Key Factors

**1. Escalating US-China technology competition**: The United States has banned high-end chip technology supplies to China entirely. As TSMC and Samsung concede some Chinese market share, they are eyeing Southeast Asia as an alternative outlet. The Korean, Japanese, and US coalition now faces an imperative to build a "secure supply chain."

**2. Implementing post-COVID supply chain diversification**: The pandemic proved how dangerous single-source dependency is. Global foundries now treat operating production facilities in three or more countries as essential risk hedging. Southeast Asia's geopolitical neutrality—not aligned with either the US or China—is a key attraction.

**3. US and Japanese government subsidies and tax incentives**: The US Chips and Science Act (2022) allocated $54 billion to support domestic semiconductor production investment. Japan is offering tax incentives for Southeast Asian hubs in the name of supply chain stabilization. These subsidies drive 30–40% of global foundries' decision-making on entering Southeast Asia.

**4. Low costs and abundant labor**: Southeast Asian manufacturing costs run 40–60% of Taiwan and Japan levels. Singapore's highly skilled workforce combined with Vietnam and Thailand's young demographic and relatively low wages yield higher profitability. Governments actively compete in recruitment—Singapore has a $5 billion fund, Vietnam offers corporate tax breaks.

## Structural Strengths and Weaknesses: Is This a Boom or a Trap?

**Strengths—emerging as a regional economic engine**: Semiconductor factories take two years to build and employ over 1,000 workers each. Vietnam and Thailand saw exports jump 15% in 2024 alone from these investments. Singapore is projected to derive 2.1% of its 2025 GDP growth from semiconductor sector investment. Rising tax revenue, technology transfer, and emerging subcontracting ecosystems are transforming the region's industrial landscape.

**Weaknesses—limited by advanced technology gaps**: Southeast Asian foundries still depend on TSMC and Samsung for processes below 7nm. Weak internal R&D capacity means technological independence remains distant. Moreover, over 80% of chip demand is export-oriented, making the region vulnerable to global downturns and regulatory tightening. Political instability—Thailand's coup history, Vietnam's privatization challenges—also shapes long-term investment decisions.

## Summary

Southeast Asia is no longer merely a Plan B for China avoidance; it is emerging as a strategic semiconductor supply chain hub. As US-China competition intensifies, these investments will continue. However, technological self-sufficiency and political stability will be the critical inflection points for sustained growth.
