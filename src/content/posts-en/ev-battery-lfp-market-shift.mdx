## The Cost Crisis in EV Batteries and the Choice of Chemistry

As EV battery costs decline, the industry is rapidly shifting from conventional high-performance NCM/NCA chemistry to cheaper LFP technology. This is not merely a technical competition but a structural transformation determining the mass-market viability of the EV sector.

## Why Is Battery Technology Diverging Now?

For the past 15 years, the core of lithium-ion batteries has been energy density. NCM and NCA chemistry, combining elements like nickel (Ni), cobalt (Co), and manganese (Mn), were mainstream, enabling longer ranges with less weight. This was essential for premium EVs—luxury sedans, SUVs.

But as EVs moved into the mass-market segment, the situation reversed. Consumer priorities shifted from 'driving range of 500 km per charge' to 'purchase price under $200,000.' Since batteries represent a significant portion of EV cost, reducing battery costs became a matter of survival for all manufacturers. This is where LFP (lithium iron phosphate) chemistry emerged.

## Why the Shift to LFP Now: Four Factors

**1. Overwhelming Cost Advantage**: LFP cell raw material costs are significantly lower than NCM. Iron and phosphate are abundant and easily accessible, with no expensive transition metals like cobalt or nickel. The manufacturing process is also simpler, yielding higher production efficiency. OEMs can pack more capacity into LFP-based EVs at the same price point.

**2. Aggressive Adoption by Chinese OEMs and Battery Makers**: China was not a first-mover in NCM chemistry. Instead, it focused early on LFP development, with battery makers like BYD and CATL and OEMs like NIO and Xiaopeng aggressively promoting LFP platforms. As a result, they gained significant advantages in price and availability, and LFP supply chains expanded through mass sales in Asian markets.

**3. Reassessment of Stability and Thermal Safety**: LFP was once emphasized only for its 'low energy density' disadvantage. However, evaluation criteria have recently shifted. LFP is far more resistant to overcharging and overheating, with less thermal runaway risk. As EV fire incidents increased and insurance and safety regulations tightened, manufacturers began marketing LFP's inherent stability alongside cost savings.

**4. Supply Risk Materialization of Critical Minerals**: Nickel production is concentrated in a handful of countries like Indonesia and the Philippines, while cobalt is centered in Congo. As geopolitical tensions, mining bans, and environmental regulations intensify, even advanced-market manufacturers can no longer ignore supply chain risks. LFP fundamentally mitigates these risks.

## Yet Technology Divides on Two Poles: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**LFP's Strengths**: Price, stability, and supply-chain independence. Mass-production infrastructure is established, and technology to compensate for energy density limits through pack design and battery management software is mature. LFP is likely to become the standard for entry-level EVs and long-range commute batteries.

**NCM/NCA's Strengths**: Still superior in performance and range. In North American and European premium segments and performance car markets, energy density remains the competitive benchmark. It achieves greater distance with the same weight and enjoys advantages in fast charging. The technology is highly mature, and supply chains are geographically dispersed globally.

## In Conclusion

As battery cost competition intensifies, the EV market has shifted paradigms from 'energy density competition' to 'choice of price and stability.' LFP and NCM/NCA are likely to divide roles rather than compete directly. Mass-market and budget segments are rapidly moving to LFP, while premium and performance markets will continue to rely on high-density chemistry. Within this framework, the supply-chain choices of battery and automakers will become the key variable determining future competitiveness.
