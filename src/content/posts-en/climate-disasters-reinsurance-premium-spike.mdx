The reinsurance renewal cycle has arrived. The insurance industry's nervous system watches the window when reinsurance contracts are rewritten each year. 2026 is different. Accumulated climate-related losses over the past two to three years align precisely with a period of heightened reinsurance rate increase demands. This is not merely a reinsurer's issue. It pressures primary insurers' rate-setting decisions and extends influence to platform-embedded insurance products consumers subscribe to.

## Reinsurance Renewal Cycle Meets Climate Costs

Insurers pass the risks they insure to other insurers. This is reinsurance. When major loss events—floods, heavy rains, typhoons—occur, the impact flows from primary insurers through to reinsurers.

Reinsurance contracts typically have one-year terms. Early in the year (especially January-March) is the main renewal window. Reinsurers then review last year's or the past few years' loss records and propose new premium rates. If losses are low, rates remain stable or decline. If losses are large, rates rise.

The trend over recent years is clear: the frequency and intensity of climate phenomena are breaking historical records in successive events. Loss experience recorded by insurers reflects all of this. In the 2026 early renewal table, that accumulated loss data is projected.

## Why Reinsurance Rates Are Rising Now: Four Structures

**1. Cumulative Effect of Climate Losses**: It's not individual events but pattern change. Insurance mathematics is probability-based. It predicts future losses from historical data. But if the past 30 years of data no longer forecast the future well, insurers must reframe their probability models. This necessarily demands higher rates. It's not simply "losses were large last year" but "potential risk is larger"—a judgment backed by evidence.

**2. Capital Pressure on Reinsurers**: Regardless of size, insurers must maintain capital reserves to absorb losses. It's both a regulatory requirement and fundamental to credit standing. If a reinsurer assumes larger loss exposure than before, the same capital cannot cover more business. Therefore, it must either raise rates or reduce coverage amounts. Generally, rate increases are preferred.

**3. Chain Rate Increases in Primary Insurers**: When reinsurers raise rates, who bears the cost? Insurance executives avoid absorbing it themselves (though some do amid intense competition). Instead, they pass it to end consumers—the insurance buyer. Auto insurance, fire insurance, and liability insurance rates rising follows this path.

**4. Transmission to Platform-Embedded Insurance**: Today, insurance isn't sold only on insurer websites. E-commerce platforms, ridesharing apps, subscription services "embed" insurance and present it to consumers. These products are ultimately underwritten by insurers and access the same reinsurance market. Therefore, underlying reinsurance rate increases affect the price and terms of platform products. Consumers see shipping insurance on an app, but the same reinsurance dynamics operate behind it.

## Structural Positions of Insurers

**Berkshire Hathaway B (BRK/B) Strength**: Berkshire's insurance operations (GEICO, reinsurance, and property-casualty) have a massive capital cushion. Losses occurring each year are offset by Berkshire's investment gains. For a primary insurer, reinsurance rate increases are losses, but Berkshire can absorb the overall impact through integrated operations. Moreover, long-term capital strategy freedom allows it to flexibly handle short-term profitability pressure.

**Renaissance Reinsurance (RE) Distinctiveness**: A pure-play reinsurer. It buys and sells primary insurers' risks but doesn't contract directly with consumers. Therefore, it faces the climate loss impact most directly. But simultaneously, it wields the greatest rate-setting power. As the reinsurance market stiffens toward excess demand, reinsurers assume price-setter positions.

**XL Capital (XL) Mixed Position**: Operates both primary insurance and reinsurance. Therefore, it faces pressure from both directions. Reinsurance rates rise while primary insurance customers resist rate increases. To maintain profitability between them requires efficiency improvement or selective underwriting.

## Summary

The early 2026 reinsurance renewal is only one cycle, but the environment it meets is unlike the past. Climate phenomena changes are shaking the foundational assumptions of insurance mathematics, pressuring the entire rate-setting chain up and down. How insurers respond depends on their capital structure, business portfolio, and customer characteristics. If climate losses become a structural trend, the pricing and supply method of insurance itself will also change.
