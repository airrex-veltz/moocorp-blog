Since 2025, the most common question in corporate boardrooms has been this: "Do we really need to deploy AI right now?"

At first glance, this seems odd. It's been two years since GPT-4 launched, Microsoft has pushed Copilot into all Office products, and Google has made Gemini the default in its cloud services. By that logic, the fastest-moving companies should have already moved past the initial phase, and followers should naturally be catching up. But reality is different. The performance of early adopters is 'mixed.'

## The Quiet Reassessment of Early Adopters

Let's start with the financial sector. Large banks like JP Morgan Chase, Bank of America, and Goldman Sachs have conducted massive generative AI pilots over the past 18 months. Automating legal document review, accelerating deal analysis, deploying customer service chatbots. Every time internal reports leaked, numbers like "40% productivity improvement" came attached.

But in Q3 2025 earnings calls, a peculiar pattern emerged. Investors asked, "Is AI-driven cost savings actually reflected in P&L?" and CFOs' answers became consistently cautious. Analysis of bank disclosures reveals that productivity gains from the pilot phase dilute by more than 50% when scaled to enterprise-wide deployment.

The three core reasons are clear.

**First, data quality problems are uncontrollable.** Automated legal review works well when processing 100 contracts for a small team, but when scaled to 5,000 across the enterprise, error rates spike due to legacy formats, country-specific variations, and irregularities in past agreements. Banks concluded that AI productivity gains don't outweigh legal risk.

**Second, organizational change costs far exceed expectations.** Redesigning business processes to be AI-friendly requires spending 2 to 3 times the initial budget on front-line employee training, governance rule restructuring, and oversight system building. According to a McKinsey survey (Q2 2025), 60% of AI-adopting companies cited "excessive organizational change costs" as a major delay reason.

**Third, the sustainability of competitive advantage is unclear.** Generative AI models themselves are commoditized. Microsoft Copilot, Google Gemini, and OpenAI API have similar costs and performance. Therefore, even if one company deploys first, competitors can automate the same task with the same tool six months later. It becomes difficult to justify ROI.

## The Convergence of Late Followers

This has changed decision-making at late-adopter companies.

Even in 2024, "We must deploy quickly or fall behind" FOMO (Fear of Missing Out) was rampant between CIOs and CEOs. So many companies started pilots. But from mid-2025 onward, the atmosphere shifted in reverse. "That company has been doing this for two years and is still in the pilot phase? We should be more cautious" logic prevailed.

Actual statistics support this. According to Forrester's "2025 Global CIO Survey":
- Companies that have delayed enterprise AI deployment plans: 12% (2024) → 34% (2025)
- Companies explicitly stating "Wait until ROI is confirmed": 41%
- Companies that have cut or frozen AI budgets: 28%

This is not mere delay but **strategic observation.** Late followers are learning from early movers' trial and error.

## The Flattening of SaaS Company Growth Curves

This shift has directly impacted SaaS company growth.

**Microsoft** suffered the most direct damage. In 2024, analysts projected Copilot subscription services would become a $5B revenue stream. However, in Q2 2025 results, Microsoft did not disclose Copilot Pro (consumer) subscriber numbers and admitted that enterprise Copilot adoption was progressing "slower than expected." Enterprise-wide Copilot deployment contracts actually stayed at 40% of projections.

**Google Cloud** faces a similar situation. Duet AI (later integrated into Gemini) was initially hailed as the flagship of "enterprise productivity revolution," but actual adoption among enterprise customers remained at 20-25%. Most organizations continue to respond with "We'll pilot, but full-scale deployment is for next year."

What's more interesting is the reaction of **Salesforce, Adobe, and Atlassian** — application companies that embedded AI in their software but find enterprise customers delaying activation of those features. In its March 2025 earnings call, Salesforce announced that "Einstein AI adoption has been delayed by eight months compared to expectations."

## The Economics of Waiting for Verifiable ROI

Why is this happening? What companies are waiting for is not simply 'technology stabilization' but **evidence of measurable ROI.**

There are hidden costs underlying AI adoption:
- **Model fine-tuning costs**: Retraining models with company-specific data is far more complex than expected
- **Data preparation costs**: Cleaning legacy system data consumes 50-70% of initial project budgets
- **Monitoring and regulatory compliance**: Personnel and tools to monitor AI decisions and validate bias
- **Failure costs**: When AI can't handle a specific task, the cost of humans doing it again

These costs don't surface in pilots. Pilots typically run within constrained scope — 200 people, one process, three months. But when scaled to 5,000 people, 15 processes, and continuous operations, the cost structure changes entirely.

Early adopters have experienced this, and later followers are watching.

## Market Reorganization

This phenomenon is reshaping the competitive landscape of AI vendors.

**NVIDIA** is paradoxically the biggest beneficiary. As companies pivot toward "building models specialized with our data," GPU demand is shifting from generative AI service calls to **on-premises/private model training.** NVIDIA's H100 and H200 are these customers' top choices.

**Oracle** is taking the path of deeply embedding AI into its ERP systems. As companies lose confidence in "generic AI," they converge toward "AI that understands our business logic," an environment favorable to ERP vendors.

**Microsoft and Google** must endure a near-term slowdown in Copilot/Gemini enterprise conversion rates. But the long-term strategy is a shift to cloud infrastructure businesses (Azure OpenAI Service, Vertex AI). When companies move from "generic SaaS add-on costs" to "private model building infrastructure," spending on cloud capacity (GPU, storage, networking) actually increases.

## Outlook for 2026

How long will this 'waiting state' persist?

The situation may worsen until mid-2026. The "first-year operational results" from the very earliest adopters will be finalized in Q1-2 2026, and current signals suggest the verdict will be "better than some feared, but not as good as hoped." At that moment, late followers will likely become even more cautious.

Instead, rapid adoption could begin from 2026 onward in specific vertical industries. In domains like financial trading, drug discovery, and semiconductor design — where **ROI calculation is clear** — AI will penetrate rapidly. But this will be "targeted high-value process automation," not "enterprise-wide AI deployment."

In conclusion, the "waiting for validated ROI" that began in 2025 is not merely a delay but a signal of **paradigm shift in corporate AI adoption.** A transition from optimistic technology adoption to economically-driven due diligence. This can extend SaaS company growth curves by two to three years, and only companies that have strengthened their competitiveness during that interval will remain.
