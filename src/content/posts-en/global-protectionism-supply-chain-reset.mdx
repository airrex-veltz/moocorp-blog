## Global Supply Chains Are Being Redesigned for 'Localization'

Rising protectionism and strengthened regional regulations are fundamentally transforming the production structures of multinational enterprises. This shift creates short-term pressure on manufacturing profitability but is establishing a new standard for supply chain diversification.

## Three Indicators to Read Manufacturing Weakness

**XLI** (State Street Industrial Select Sector SPDR ETF) traded at $162.67 as of November 6, 2026, recording a 3-month decline of -11.64%. **IYM** (iShares U.S. Basic Materials ETF) closed at $179.90 on the same day, down -5.37% over three months. In contrast, **RWM** (ProShares Short Russell 2000, which bets against small-cap stocks) rose +10.30% over the same period. The divergence among these three indicators reveals the asymmetric impact that shifts in the global trade environment are having on different industries: manufacturing and raw materials are declining, while assets that bet on small-cap weakness are rising.

## Four Pathways for Protectionism to Reshape Structure

**1. Tariffication of costs**: As the United States, Europe, India, and other major markets erect high tariff walls, the competitiveness of traditional low-cost countries is weakening. Companies have no choice but to turn to local production to circumvent these tariffs. This is not merely an increase in sales costs but a transformation in investment scale and payback periods. Cross-border optimization design is disappearing, and multi-location, small-scale factory operations are becoming the standard.

**2. Strengthening of regional regulations and localization requirements**: An increasing number of countries are explicitly mandating self-sufficiency criteria in strategic industries such as EV batteries, semiconductors, and pharmaceuticals. These regulations directly destroy the efficiency of a single global supply chain. Supply sources become dispersed, redundant investments increase, and per-unit production costs rise.

**3. Fixed costs of supply chain diversification**: The previous model—where a single factory supplied the world—is shifting to a structure requiring production, storage, and distribution across three regions simultaneously. This transition triggers a surge in fixed costs: equipment duplication, workforce training, and inventory management. Economies of scale work in reverse, eroding profit margins.

**4. Profitability pressure and reinvestment obligations**: As costs climb, selling prices remain locked in by competition. Companies must increase capital expenditure on new facilities, but resources are constrained. Dividend payments and share buybacks are likely to shrink, signaling a decline in investor expected returns.

## Between Long-term Stability and Short-term Suffering

**Structural strengths**: When supply chains localize, transportation costs and transit times fall. The speed of response to demand shifts accelerates. Exposure to geopolitical risk—changes in particular countries' politics, logistics disruptions—becomes more diversified. Over the long term, this can yield a more resilient system.

**Structural weaknesses**: For now, all systems face rising costs. The burden of redundant investment, loss of scale, and inventory pressure operate simultaneously. Small and mid-sized manufacturers lack the capital to absorb these costs and may become targets for restructuring. This explains why XLI's weakness aligns with RWM's strength (the bet against small-cap outperformance).

## Summary

Protectionism ends the 'age of efficiency' and opens the 'age of stability'. But the transition will be marked by rising costs and margin compression. To adapt to change, companies must invest substantial capital; investors should watch for the timing of financial burden relief rather than near-term earnings improvement.
