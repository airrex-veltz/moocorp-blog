## Battery Material Cost Squeeze: OEMs Face Quarterly Price Renegotiations

Global EV battery supply chains are being buffeted by nickel and cobalt futures volatility. Major battery material suppliers are passing hedging costs to their customers, forcing automakers (OEMs) to renegotiate prices quarterly or surrender 8-12% margins.

## Why Nickel and Cobalt Futures Volatility Destabilizes Battery Supply Chains

Cathode materials hinge on nickel (Ni), cobalt (Co), and manganese (Mn) chemistry. LFP (lithium iron phosphate) is stable but lacks energy density; NCM/NCA variants deliver higher performance but are hypersensitive to nickel and cobalt prices. In the first half of this year, nickel futures ranged $7.5-9.2 per barrel, while cobalt swung $26,000-31,000 per ton. This directly governs 35-40% of battery pack costs. Battery makers such as SK Innovation, LG Energy Solution, and CATL cannot absorb all this volatility.

## Why Battery Supply Contracts Are Destabilizing Now: Four Factors

**1. Hedging Costs Structurally Passed to OEMs**: Battery suppliers secure raw materials through 3-6 month futures contracts, but volatility has exceeded 40% annually over the past 18 months, pushing options premiums to 2-3% of battery pack unit costs. Suppliers once absorbed these; now they explicitly shift burden via "Cost Pass-Through" clauses to OEMs.

**2. End of Long-Term Fixed-Price Contracts**: The automotive industry standard of 3-year fixed supply agreements has become rare. Major OEMs—Hyundai, Kia, BMW, Volkswagen—now accept only Quarterly Price Adjustment (QPA) clauses. While this reduces battery suppliers' uncertainty, it forces OEMs to postpone production planning and margin management quarter by quarter, breeding a vicious cycle.

**3. Dual-Source Supply Chains and Mining Risk in Indonesia and Congo**: Over 60% of nickel comes from Indonesia; 70% of cobalt from the Democratic Republic of Congo. Tighter Indonesian mining regulations and labor strike concerns in Congo have widened futures spreads. Battery suppliers must contract with multiple miners to avoid single-source risk, further inflating hedging costs.

**4. Prices Remain "Sticky" Despite Softening EV Demand**: Global EV sales growth in 2025 fell 30% short of expectations, yet battery pack prices never reached predicted lows. Supply tightness from India and Indonesia outweighs weak demand. Battery suppliers cannot protect margins, and OEMs resort to litigation over renegotiation costs.

## Battery Material Crisis Resolution Still 3-5 Years Away: Structural Weaknesses

**LFP Expansion Is Short-Term Stopgap, Not a Cure**: More OEMs are shifting to LFP to sidestep price volatility, but long-range and premium EVs still require NCM/NCA chemistry. Tesla and Mercedes-Benz premium models reject LFP dependence. Battery diversification alone cannot eliminate nickel and cobalt hedging costs entirely.

**Nickel Mining Investment Lags, Prolonging Supply Deficit**: Global nickel mining investment has expanded gradually since 2023 with high-pressure technology (HPT) deployment, but new mines take 4-6 years to commission. As a matter of time, nickel premiums likely persist through 2026-2029.

**Hedging Expansion Amplifies Institutional Speculation**: As battery pack volatility became a profit opportunity, hedge funds and commercial banks surged nickel and cobalt futures positions. Now financial trading volumes outpace industrial demand in driving volatility. Consequently, futures prices swing regardless of supply shortfalls.

## Summary

With battery material hedging costs locked in, EV supply chains now face a vicious cycle: fixed-price agreements yield to quarterly renegotiations, creating production uncertainty for OEMs. Expanding nickel and cobalt mining investments and advancing LFP technology require at least 3-5 years.
