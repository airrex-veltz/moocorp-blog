## Green Hydrogen Subsidy Cliff: Funding Depletion and Project Economics Collapse in 2027

As temporary subsidies supporting green hydrogen projects in Europe and the U.S. deplete around 2027, electrolyzer manufacturers and hydrogen producers face the risk of demand collapse. In the absence of long-term purchase agreements, early-entry economies now face stranded asset risks.

## From Temporary Subsidies to Permanent Mechanisms: Structural Policy Gaps

Over the past five years, the EU's Innovation Fund and the U.S. Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) incentives have supported the early profitability of green hydrogen projects. As of 2024, the European Commission disbursed approximately EUR 10 billion in subsidies, while the U.S. provided production credits of up to $3 per ton. The problem is that most of these funds are expected to be depleted or scaled back after 2027–2028 without reauthorization. Currently, no permanent price mechanism—such as carbon tax linkage or long-term feed-in tariffs—has been enacted to guarantee the carbon-neutral economics of hydrogen.

## Why Hydrogen Project Profitability Is Deteriorating Now: Four Pressures

**1. Overlapping Subsidy Expiration**: Most smaller projects anticipate funding depletion by 2027–2028. The European Commission has left the size of the Innovation Fund post-2030 undetermined, and the U.S. requires congressional reauthorization to extend IRA credits. With long-term planning impossible, new production facility investment is already in decline.

**2. Widening Green Premium Gap**: The production cost of green hydrogen without subsidies remains more than double that of gray hydrogen. Industrial hydrogen consumers (refineries, chemical companies) are price-sensitive, and new long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) have plummeted since 2024. Electrolyzer orders in Germany and the Netherlands have also declined by more than 25% year-over-year.

**3. Electricity Price Volatility Risk**: 70% of green hydrogen economics depends on power purchase costs. When renewable energy prices fluctuate, hydrogen producers without fixed-price PPAs face margin compression. Over the past three years, European spot power prices have fluctuated more than threefold, and this uncertainty is paralyzing long-term investment decisions.

**4. Policy Competition in Competing Regions**: As low-cost solar and wind regions—Australia, Chile, Saudi Arabia—enter hydrogen production in force, Europe and the U.S. are losing their premium position. Australia has already reached production costs competitive with gray hydrogen, and these countries' subsidies are longer-lasting and more consistent. Early-entry firms risk losing global competitiveness.

## Why Hydrogen Anyway: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Long-term hydrogen demand is real**: Hydrogen demand in hard-to-decarbonize sectors such as steel, chemicals, and refining is expected to grow more than threefold by 2050. Short-term policy gaps do not negate the long-term vision. However, the payback period of facilities built today (15–25 years) misaligns with policy uncertainty.

**Stronger carbon regulations raise hydrogen's value**: As regulations such as the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) tighten, the premium for green hydrogen over gray hydrogen becomes clearer. But to capture that regulatory benefit, producers must endure until the 2027 subsidy cliff, and deteriorating cash flows make it likely that smaller projects will drop out.

## Conclusion

The 2027 subsidy cliff represents not merely a funding shortfall but a collapse in policy confidence. Declining electrolyzer orders are already visible, and some projects in early-entry economies risk stoppage before completion. Unless Europe and the U.S. enact permanent mechanisms—such as carbon-linked pricing or long-term feed-in tariffs—before 2027, long-term hydrogen transition targets will remain difficult to realize.
