## Water Treatment Chemicals Bottleneck Worsens as U.S. Water System Modernization Accelerates

As funds from the U.S. Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act flow into projects, drinking water system improvements are surging. In the process, demand for coagulants like polyaluminum chloride (PAC) and ferric sulfate is far outpacing supply capacity. Chemical companies are responding with capacity expansion investments, but timelines exceed one year, making near-term price increases and delivery delays inevitable.

## Infrastructure Act Funding and Stricter Water Quality Standards

In 2021, the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA) allocated $55.2 billion to drinking water and wastewater treatment infrastructure. The funds focused on lead pipe replacement and aging water treatment facility modernization, prompting hundreds of municipalities to issue orders within a single year. Annual demand for PAC and ferric sulfate reaches approximately 1.8–2.0 million tonnes, but North American production capacity currently stands at roughly 1.5 million tonnes annually. The shortfall relies on imports, primarily from China, but rising shipping and customs clearance delays continue to strain supply chains.

## Why the Supply Crunch Now: Four Key Factors

**1. Policy-driven demand concentration**: After infrastructure funding was allocated, municipalities rushed orders simultaneously. While 2024 orders increased 35% year-over-year, chemical manufacturers' capacity expansion plans span 2025–2027. This timing mismatch has amplified near-term supply pressures.

**2. Stronger Chinese export controls**: China is intentionally limiting PAC exports as it raises domestic water quality standards. With North America dependent on imports for over 40% of supply, reduced Chinese availability hits hard. Indian suppliers have also cut export volumes due to rising domestic demand.

**3. Rising feedstock prices (sulfuric acid and alumina)**: As basic chemical prices began rising in late 2024, coagulant manufacturers' margins have come under pressure. Weakening economics for new production lines have delayed investment decisions, creating a vicious cycle.

**4. Tightened environmental and quality standards**: The EPA's strengthened PFOA and PFOS regulations have required existing product re-certification. Some small and mid-sized chemical makers' products failed, reducing the supplier base and concentrating orders among trusted vendors.

## Despite the Crunch, Long-Term Opportunities Remain: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Supply-side strengths**: Major North American chemical makers (chlor-alkali complexes) have strong access to feedstocks like hydrochloric acid and alumina needed for PAC and ferric sulfate production. Once expansion schedules materialize, baseline production capacity can recover quickly. Moreover, Infrastructure Act funding extends through 2031, providing a stable demand anchor.

**Weaknesses and risks**: Developing countries' smaller water treatment plants may face funding constraints, limiting demand growth. Additionally, as climate change drives more extreme precipitation events, wastewater systems could become overwhelmed, leading to chemical overuse and subsequent demand collapse. Sustained price increases could accelerate shifts toward alternative coagulants (natural coagulants, membrane filtration) or technologies.

## Summary

Infrastructure investment expansion is driving structural demand growth in water treatment chemicals, but a 1–2 year supply shortage phase is unavoidable. While supplier profitability will benefit during this period, long-term competitiveness depends on the pace of capacity expansion.
