## U.S. and European Localization Policies Shake Asian Contract Manufacturers' Business Structure

The fastest change unfolding in the global medical device market is geopolitical segmentation. As the U.S. and Europe concentrate investment in "trusted suppliers," Asian contract development and manufacturing organizations (CDMO) face the end of the low-cost contract manufacturing era and are being forced to transition toward higher technology sophistication and in-house product development.

## The Structure of Supply Chain Reconfiguration: Then and Now

In the past, the medical device supply chain was clear. Advanced-economy brand companies handled design and certification, while Asian manufacturers managed mass production through cost competition—a division-of-labor system. This model operated on cost optimization and economies of scale. However, beginning in the early 2020s, post-pandemic supply chain disruption shocks and intensifying U.S.–China technology competition began to shake this structure.

The U.S. has expanded support beyond semiconductors to medical devices and precision manufacturing following the CHIPS and Science Act. The European Union, under its Strategic Autonomy declaration, is driving the establishment of production capacity for critical medical devices within the EU. Japan and Taiwan have already secured competitive advantages in high-value medical device manufacturing. The result is straightforward: Asian CDMOs that relied solely on low wage levels now face the dual burden of declining orders and margin pressure.

## Why Supply Chain Reconfiguration Is Happening Now: Four Factors

**1. Regulatory Risk Materialization**: Export controls on critical defense and medical components are strengthening to unpredictable levels. Like Taiwan-sourced semiconductors, medical device components face growing likelihood of landing on geopolitical restriction lists. Customers (advanced-economy medical device companies) are accelerating re-sourcing to domestic and allied manufacturers to reduce country-of-origin risk.

**2. Regulatory Standard Duplication**: U.S. and EU regulations have begun requiring different "trust systems." An increasing number of high-value medical devices now require final production within specific countries. For CDMOs, this marks the end of the era when a single certification could secure global orders.

**3. Reversal of Manufacturing Cost Structure**: As advanced-economy re-shoring investments expand automation-based production, competition on simple labor cost differentials alone is no longer viable. Instead, lead time reduction, customs simplification, and local engineering support have become more critical factors. This directly erodes Asia's CDMOs' traditional cost advantage.

**4. Rising Customer Concentration**: Large medical device companies and government procurement are strongly demanding supply chain transparency and security, driving a shift toward long-term contract structures with selected partners. Room for new low-cost suppliers is shrinking, and already-selected firms face continuous pressure to improve technology.

## Survival Paths and Structural Constraints

**Transition to High-Value Operations Is Essential but Difficult**: CDMOs must raise their testing, validation, and regulatory documentation capabilities while maintaining trust with existing contract customers, while simultaneously increasing margins through in-house product development and specialized processes (custom assembly and system integration). This requires simultaneous R&D investment and talent acquisition—yet most companies lack capital accumulated from past cost competition.

**Intensifying Geographic Constraints**: The mere "adjacency" of Asian factories to Chinese, Indian, and Vietnamese supply chains has now become a risk factor. Facing technology leakage concerns and regulatory list risks, more companies are pursuing acquisition by advanced-economy firms or localization strategies (establishing subsidiaries within the U.S. or EU). This implies high fixed costs.

## Summary

The reconfiguration of the medical device supply chain is not a macroeconomic trend but an ongoing reality. For Asian contract manufacturers, this represents not merely cyclical downturn but the loss of viability in their business model itself. Firms that survive understand that crossing high technology barriers, internalizing regulatory complexity, and deepening collaborative relationships with customers is their only path forward.
