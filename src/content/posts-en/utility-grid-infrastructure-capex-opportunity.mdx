## Aging Transmission and Distribution Networks Drive Major Capital Expenditure — Utility Companies Face Inevitable Rate Increases

Large-scale replacement demand from aging power grid infrastructure is reshaping the financial structure of major US utility companies. As transmission and distribution equipment installed decades ago reaches the end of its design life simultaneously, leading power utilities must absorb unprecedented levels of capital expenditure (CapEx). As of September 18, 2026, **XLU**, a representative utility sector ETF, is down 6.63% over one month, reflecting underlying concerns about this investment burden and resulting rate increase pressure.

## Aging Infrastructure and the Capital Expenditure Wave

Most of the US power grid was installed between 1960 and 1980. Equipment approaching or exceeding its 50-year design life is reaching replacement timing simultaneously. Major utility companies must undertake modernization of transmission and distribution systems, adoption of smart grids, and construction of energy storage infrastructure over the next 5-10 years.

**NEE** (NextEra Energy) closed at $80.47 as of September 18, 2026, recording revenue growth of 12.4%, reflecting increased revenues from capital expenditure demand. Meanwhile, **DUK** (Duke Energy) and **SO** (The Southern Company) closed at $117.53 and $85.52 respectively, but are showing stagnant revenue growth of 1.1% and 0.1%. This suggests that despite investment burdens, both companies are experiencing margin pressure and profitability deterioration.

## Four Reasons for Utility Sector Weakness

**1. Long-term burden of capital-intensive investment**
To finance massive capital for facility investment, companies must issue debt or dilute equity in capital markets. In a rising interest rate environment, debt costs increase and are ultimately passed on to consumer rates. This structural investment deteriorates near-term profitability and puts downward pressure on share prices.

**2. Limitations of regulated revenue base**
US utilities can reflect investment costs in rates with regulatory approval, but the pace of regulatory approval lags implementation of investments. DUK's net profit margin of 16.0% and SO's 15.4% are significantly lower than NEE's 32.4%, reflecting profitability gaps across different regulatory environments. Consumer resistance to rate increases and political pressure slow the company's investment recovery pace.

**3. Public resistance to rate increases**
As transmission and distribution modernization costs are passed through to residential and industrial electricity rates, resistance from consumer groups and politicians is intensifying. If regulatory intervention limiting rate increase magnitudes increases, companies will not fully recover anticipated revenues. This uncertainty is reducing investors' expected returns on equity.

**4. Competition for capital with renewable energy transition investment**
Following government net-zero targets, utility companies must simultaneously invest in solar and wind infrastructure expansion. With replacement of aging transmission and distribution networks overlapping with renewable energy connection investments, capital becomes fragmented and near-term profitability recovery becomes difficult. NEE maintains relatively high revenue growth because it takes an active investment position in both sectors.

## Why Still the Utility Sector — Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strength: Monopoly position and regulated revenue base**
Power supply is inherently a monopoly business. Stable cash flows are guaranteed under regulated rate structures rather than competitive markets. The maintenance of PER in the 17-20 range reflects that utilities remain attractive assets for dividend-seeking investors.

**Weakness: Chronic capital pressure**
Over the next decade, cumulative CapEx reaching the trillions of dollars will inevitably lead to persistent increases in long-term debt. Rising capital costs will constrain dividend growth, dampening expectations of income investors. If rate increases are insufficient, profitability will not improve.

## Conclusion

Replacing aging power grids is an unavoidable challenge for the utility sector. In this process, corporate capital structures will deteriorate and the feasibility of rate increases will depend on regulatory and political variables. The current weakness should be viewed not as simple market correction but as a reflection of uncertainty over medium-term profitability recovery.
