## The Vicious Cycle of Ticket Profitability Caused by Sticky Interest Rates

The window for fuel hedging is closing. As interest rates remain stuck at elevated levels, margin costs on futures contracts have risen, and airlines' forward contracts for fixing long-term fuel prices are losing their economics. More vulnerable to sharp fuel price swings, the aviation industry now has no choice but to pass fuel costs directly to ticket prices. Yet amid slowing economic growth, there is little room for price increases.

## Structural Vulnerability of Aviation and Fuel Costs

Aviation transport is inherently fuel-intensive. Given airlines' thin operating margins, fluctuations in fuel prices—a major component of operating costs—heavily determine net income. In the past, airlines hedged by locking in prices on futures markets ahead of potential rises, or included fuel surcharge clauses in ticket prices to pass through fuel cost movements and share risk.

## Why Ticket Profitability Is Deteriorating Now: Four Factors

**1. Rising Hedging Costs from Persistent Interest Rates**: As rates remain elevated, margin calls on futures contracts have increased. For airlines to lock in fuel quantities a year ahead, more liquidity must be tied up, squeezing other operating funds. For smaller or emerging-market carriers, this cost translates directly into funding risk and limits access to futures markets.

**2. Wider Fuel Price Volatility and Price Discovery Challenges**: While rate increases have paused in a fixed-rate environment, geopolitical tensions and supply imbalances push fuel prices to wider swings. It becomes harder for airlines to predict average prices, and fuel surcharge hikes lag behind sharp price spikes. Conversely, when prices plunge, customer resistance prevents surcharges from being reduced.

**3. Ticket Price Rigidity**: As fuel surcharge practices weaken or disappear, airlines must raise base fares themselves. Yet amid economic slowdown, passengers increasingly seek low-cost carriers, and fare increases lead to demand destruction. With fixed cost structures, flight frequencies cannot be cut proportionally, so load factors fall directly.

**4. Economic Spillovers and Weak Demand**: In a high-rate environment, slowing economic growth simultaneously reduces business and leisure demand. Airlines face cost pressures while encountering insufficient demand, caught in a downward spiral where all three prior factors compound.

## Structural Weaknesses in Aviation

The aviation industry's cost structure is rigid. Aircraft are fixed assets, and beyond fuel, lease payments, landing fees, and maintenance are difficult to cut short-term. Unless fuel costs are passed completely to customers, airlines must squeeze margins while exposed to triple external shocks: exchange rates, interest rates, and oil prices.

Moreover, aviation supply is in excess. Intensifying competition from both legacy carriers and low-cost carrier expansion makes price increases ever harder. The margin for passing rising fuel costs to tickets continues to shrink.

## In Summary

Wider fuel volatility and higher hedging costs, both stemming from sticky interest rates, fundamentally threaten aviation profitability. Caught between an economic environment that prevents fare increases and a rigid cost structure, airlines' profit margins face relentless pressure.
