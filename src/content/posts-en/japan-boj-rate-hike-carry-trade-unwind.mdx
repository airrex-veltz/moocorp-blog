## Bank of Japan's Normalization Triggers Global Low-Cost Borrowing Liquidation

The Bank of Japan's rate hike signals are acting as a dual shock of yen strength and carry trade unwinding. As Japan—where low rates have been the norm for over 20 years—enters its normalization path, global funds borrowed in that currency simultaneously recognize losses.

## **FXY** and **EWJ**: What Exchange Rate Volatility Reveals

**FXY** (Invesco CurrencyShares Japanese Yen Trust) has traded in the $55.96–$59.90 range over the past six months (as of September 16, 2026). While this appears to be simple price movement, the range reflects yen exchange rate volatility. Over the same period, **EWJ** (iShares MSCI Japan ETF) traded between $80.41–$98.79 (as of September 16, 2026) and currently posts a P/E of 19.3. This suggests improving profitability among Japanese firms, yet exchange rate instability is simultaneously widening earnings volatility.

## Why Unwind Now: Four Factors

**1. Bank of Japan Normalization Path Uncertainty**: Markets accustomed to negative rates and quantitative easing for over 20 years face sudden rate hike signals. The more unclear the timing and magnitude of these signals, the greater the pressure to realize losses on existing low-cost borrowing positions.

**2. Yen Strength and Borrower Losses**: Rising rates strengthen the yen. Funds that borrowed in dollars or other currencies and must repay in yen incur foreign exchange losses. Unhedged positions in particular face immediate margin call risk.

**3. Global Liquidity Absorption Effect**: If Japan no longer supplies its currency, multinational hedge funds and institutional investors that financed through yen funding must recoup capital from other markets. This creates pressure to liquidate across a broad range of assets, from emerging markets to developed-market growth stocks.

**4. Volatility Spillover and Selectivity Risk**: Yen carry unwinding is not a one- or two-stock issue. Across global portfolios, currency hedging costs rise, and the profitability sustained by cheap carry financing deteriorates sharply in a cascade effect.

## Why **EWJ** Still Matters: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strengths**: Japanese firms' earnings foundation is solid. A P/E of 19.3 trades at a discount to U.S. levels (23–25), and yen strength reshapes export company performance. Semiconductor and automotive firms in particular have the ability to sustain profitability even with a stronger yen.

**Weaknesses**: Individual company strength and portfolio exchange rate volatility are separate concerns. **EWJ** encompasses a broad range—from Nagasaki mining to Tokyo finance—so its selectivity is limited in the face of carry unwind shocks.

## Takeaway

Bank of Japan normalization is an unavoidable trend, and yen carry unwinding has already begun. Korean investors must weigh simultaneous **FXY** surges and global liquidity contraction in an environment of widening exchange rate volatility. While Japanese company fundamentals remain strong, portfolio-level currency hedging costs are likely to erode near-term returns.
