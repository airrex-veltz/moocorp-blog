As developed countries experience intensifying population decline, a marked divergence is becoming evident in the global consumer goods market. We examine through data the background behind this phenomenon, where profitability varies significantly between companies even within the same downturn environment.

---

## Profit Divergence in Mature Markets: What the Data Reveals

Low birth rates and aging populations in developed countries represent not merely demographic decline but a fundamental restructuring of consumption patterns. As of November 2026, looking at the financial results of global consumer goods companies reveals how starkly this shift manifests.

**PM** (Philip Morris), transitioning toward alternative tobacco products, recorded a closing price of $197.88 as of November 6, 2026, with revenue growth of 5.5% on the reference date. **KO** (Coca-Cola), a beverage company, posted a closing price of $95.67 on the same reference date with revenue growth of 7.4%. In contrast, **MCD** (McDonald's) maintains a closing price of $276.52 while revenue growth stands at only 1.1%.

Why do consumer goods companies exposed to the same developed market follow such divergent paths? The answer lies in differences in how demographic change impacts each category.

## The Reality Facing Three Companies: Category-Based Demand Foundations

PM maintains growth despite tobacco being its core business due to pricing power. Aging populations tend to maintain existing consumption habits relatively strongly, and PM accommodates that demand at premium prices. As of November 6, 2026, PM's net profit margin is 24.8%, the lowest among the three companies, yet still a solid level for a consumer goods firm. Tobacco, owing to its addictive nature, remains less sensitive to business cycles and functions as a necessity with low price elasticity in developed countries.

KO's situation differs. The beverage market has far more substitutable alternatives than tobacco and is easily influenced by health trends. KO's 7.4% revenue growth reflects expansion of premium beverage lines—sports drinks, premium coffee, and premium water. Higher-income segments within the aging population show a preference for healthier, premium beverages. As of November 6, 2026, KO's net profit margin of 28.6% places it mid-range among the three companies, yet its growth rate is the highest.

MCD's 1.1% revenue growth rate warrants the most attention. Maintaining a closing price of $276.52 while revenue barely moves indicates market saturation in developed countries. However, its net profit margin of 32.4% ranks highest among the three. This suggests MCD is prioritizing efficiency improvements over quantitative growth. The franchise business model's advantages shine in a low-growth environment.

## Four Factors Determining Consumer Goods Profitability in the Age of Population Decline

**1. Redefining the Boundary Between Necessities and Discretionary Spending**

In an aging population, the definition of "necessity" shifts. Tobacco and beverages initially appear to overlap as necessities, yet function differently in consumption structures. PM's tobacco exhibits very strong stickiness among existing consumers. In contrast, MCD's food service involves more flexible consumer choice and resembles discretionary spending more sensitive to economic cycles. Elderly consumption patterns tend to be conservative, persisting with familiar goods rather than transitioning to new product categories. This foundation supports PM's 5.5% growth.

**2. Location of Pricing Power**

Elderly populations in developed countries are not price-sensitive, particularly for health- and habit-related products. PM leverages this to maintain high margins. KO is shifting toward premium segments within beverages, raising average prices. MCD faces the opposite pressure. Rising household burdens on the elderly—medical costs, long-term care expenses—combined with slow-growth wages limit pricing flexibility. MCD's 1.1% revenue growth appears to reflect this pressure.

**3. Asymmetry in Market Maturity and Geographic Composition**

All three companies have high exposure to developed markets, though to varying degrees. PM's developed market tobacco business is highly mature, supplementing growth through emerging market demand. KO maintains a more balanced geographic mix and can still capture growing beverage demand from emerging markets. MCD has the highest concentration in developed countries (particularly the U.S.) among the three, experiencing low-growth impacts most directly.

**4. Business Model and Fixed Cost Structure**

MCD's franchise model favors profitability but constrains revenue growth. It depends on improving efficiency of existing locations and raising transaction value per customer, yet elderly populations tend to reduce dining frequency itself. PM's manufacturing and KO's beverage manufacturing and distribution offer more leverage points. KO in particular benefits from high platform diversity—beverage sizes, formats, and channels—allowing faster adaptation to shifting elderly consumption patterns.

## Structural Strengths and Weaknesses: The Trade-off Between Profitability and Growth

**PM's Stability and Constraints**: Net profit margin of 24.8% reflects tobacco's high margins. Yet tobacco demand itself is declining in developed countries as a long-term trend; success in transitioning to alternatives (heated tobacco, nicotine pouches) will determine future growth. Current 5.5% revenue growth depends heavily on price increases and product mix improvements.

**KO's Balance**: Net profit margin of 28.6% and revenue growth of 7.4% represent the most balanced metrics among the three. Premium segment expansion within beverages is proving effective. However, this strategy's sustainability depends on whether elderly consumers maintain purchasing power. Should middle-class contraction accelerate in developed countries, premium beverage growth could also decelerate.

**MCD's Efficiency and Stagnation**: Net profit margin of 32.4% ranks highest among the three, yet revenue growth of 1.1% clearly demonstrates market saturation. As the food service market itself contracts in aging developed countries, MCD's premium positioning—quality enhancement rather than Fast Casual—shows limited results. Attempts to supplement through emerging market growth carry significant control risks inherent to the franchise model.

## Summary

In the age of population decline in developed countries, profit divergence among consumer goods companies is structural. Even in identical macroeconomic conditions, growth trajectories diverge sharply based on product necessity, pricing power, geographic composition, and business model. As of November 6, 2026, the differences among PM's 5.5%, KO's 7.4%, and MCD's 1.1% revenue growth rates serve as indicators of how successfully each company is adapting to this transformation.
