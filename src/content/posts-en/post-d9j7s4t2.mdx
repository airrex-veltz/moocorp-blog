## Water Scarcity Is Reshaping the Location Economics of Major Industries

Water is a core input for semiconductor etching, chemical process cooling, and food & beverage manufacturing. As water stress intensifies in global manufacturing hubs—India, Taiwan, the Middle East, and the U.S. Southwest—it is driving direct production cost increases and serving as a major variable in supply chain restructuring. Regional water availability is becoming a structural constraint that determines corporate location choices, capital investment decisions, and technology pathways.

## Direct Cost Impact of Water Scarcity by Industry

**India** Intensifying water stress is directly pressuring the operating costs of chemical and food & beverage companies. Biotech, pharmaceutical, and agricultural processing firms in Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu are strengthening investments in adjustment charges for water resource constraints and alternative water sources (desalination, rainwater harvesting). **Taiwan** When semiconductor supply-demand pressure peaks, water supply restrictions emerge as an additional risk. This is because water consumption in chip production at foundries like **TSMC** is very substantial. **United States** As the Southwest drought persists, water allocation disputes in the Colorado River Basin are intensifying, raising uncertainty in pharmaceutical and ethanol production and microchip assembly facility operations.

## Why Is Industry Structure Being Reshaped Now: Four Pressure Factors

**1. Surge in Water Prices and Regulatory Tightening**: Water tariffs in water-stressed regions are trending 5–10 times higher than advanced country standards. In Nanded, India, many areas have seen rates rise from 40–50 rupees per cubic meter (approximately 500 won) to 90 rupees (1,100 won). Simultaneously, environmental regulation strengthens, with industrial groundwater extraction restrictions and stricter wastewater discharge permit conditions accumulating operational costs. Investment in corporate water recycling technology and wastewater treatment infrastructure is shifting from optional to mandatory.

**2. Geographic Shift Signals in Supply Chain Hubs**: Expansion plans for manufacturing bases in water-scarce regions like Taiwan and India are being delayed or scaled back. Semiconductor and chemical firms are accelerating new investments in regions with relatively abundant water supply—southern Japan, southeastern Korea, and coastal Southeast Asia. TSMC's expansion of fabs in Japan and the U.S. signals a water scarcity avoidance strategy.

**3. Strengthening Competitiveness in Advanced Water Treatment Technology**: Semiconductor and chemical firms are expanding investments in vacuum distillation, reverse osmosis desalination, and integrated water-energy recycling systems. For companies located in water-scarce regions, water treatment facility operating costs now represent 20–30% of total water costs, raising the added value of technology-holding firms.

**4. Intensifying Tracking of Global Water Supply Chain "Hidden Water (Water Footprint)"**: Food & beverage, agricultural-based chemical (fertilizer, pesticide), and pharmaceutical companies are being pressured to disclose supply partners' water usage. As ESG ratings, investor requirements, and government regulations overlap, the cost structures of companies shifting away from or localizing water-intensive supply sources are being reshaped.

## Nevertheless, Water Scarcity Is Redefining Long-Term Industrial Competitiveness

**① Rising Location Competitiveness of Water-Rich Regions**: Preference for high-value manufacturing locations in high-latitude and maritime climate regions—Japan, Scandinavia, Canada, New Zealand—is strengthening. Water cost advantages partially offset rising labor and energy costs.

**② Technology Innovation Compresses Cost Gaps**: Even companies in water-scarce regions are reducing water dependency by 30–50% through closed-loop water systems and ultra-low water processes. However, high initial capital expenditure means this is progressing primarily among large enterprises.

**③ Uneven Diffusion Speed Due to Regulatory Conflicts**: Even in water-scarce regions, regulatory frameworks differ between developed and developing countries, causing structural reshaping to proceed unevenly. This means the shift may occur faster in food & beverage and textiles than in infrastructure industries (chemicals, semiconductors).

## Conclusion

Water scarcity is not a simple environmental issue but an economic constraint that determines global industrial location policy, supply chain composition, and technology investment direction. Companies in high water-stress regions are facing rising costs, regulatory tightening, and reassessment of location choices, with a high likelihood of leading to industry structure reshaping on a 5–10 year timeframe.
