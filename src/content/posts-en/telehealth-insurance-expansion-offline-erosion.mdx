## Telehealth Insurance Coverage Expansion Erodes Offline Medical Facilities' Outpatient Revenue Structure

National Health Insurance's decision to expand telehealth items under insurance coverage appears on the surface to be a positive signal for improving healthcare access. However, the result is a structural change that fundamentally destabilizes the outpatient revenue base of offline medical facilities, particularly small clinics and private practices. This represents not greater efficiency in the healthcare system but rather a 'revenue shift' within the regional medical ecosystem.

## The Healthcare Utilization Mechanism of Telehealth Insurance Expansion

When telehealth becomes covered by insurance, patient choice expands. Chronic disease management, repeat prescriptions, and consultation-based care are all areas where in-person visits are not strictly necessary. Patients can now avoid waiting times, reduce transportation costs, and save time. With insurance support, the difference in out-of-pocket costs becomes significant. When remote consultations become equal to or cheaper than in-person visits, economically rational patients gravitate toward the more convenient option.

When this choice happens collectively, the number of outpatient patients at individual clinics declines. Small healthcare facilities that depend on regular patients (those managing hypertension, diabetes, etc.) for their primary revenue are hit hardest. This is not simply a 'patient decline' but rather a contraction of 'stable revenue calculated at insurance rates.'

## Why Are Offline Medical Facilities' Revenues Now Under Pressure: Four Factors

**1. Rising acceptance of telehealth due to insurance coverage expansion**: The addition of covered services changes the behavior not only of patients but also of healthcare providers. Doctors who previously focused on in-person care now include remote consultations in their service portfolio. Hospital-level and above institutions already provide partial telehealth services. As insurance reimbursement rates increase, the gap between this technology and existing systems narrows rapidly. As a result, new options for competition with small and medium-sized medical facilities become more widespread in the market.

**2. Worsening revenue structure relative to healthcare facility density**: South Korea's healthcare facility supply is in a state of excess. In particular, clinic density in urban areas is high. When insurance-covered telehealth is introduced, it becomes a zero-sum game. Outpatient patients lost by one facility shift to remote consultations from another. Existing offline facilities' fixed costs—rent, maintenance, and personnel—do not decrease even as revenues decline.

**3. Deteriorating fixed cost recovery**: Medical facilities, especially small clinics, carry a high proportion of fixed costs. Building rent, medical equipment depreciation, and staff salaries occur regardless of patient volume. When outpatient numbers decline, revenue drops proportionally, but fixed costs remain unchanged. Profitability deteriorates rapidly, particularly for facilities that have invested in new buildings or expensive equipment.

**4. Reduced workforce utilization and margin pressure**: As patients shift to telehealth, existing clinical staff—nurses and medical assistants—see their utilization rates drop. Working hours remain the same, but productivity declines. Simultaneously, price competition intensifies for the remaining patients. As telehealth providers enter the market, offline clinics face downward pressure on reimbursement rates. The result is margin compression.

## Yet Not Everyone Faces the Same Impact: Structural Weaknesses and Strengths

**Regions and facility types that amplify weaknesses**: Regional healthcare imbalances intensify. Large urban medical institutions and telehealth platforms can expand remote services through economies of scale and technological investment capacity. Meanwhile, small clinics in rural areas lack competitiveness in the telehealth market while simultaneously losing local patients. Facilities dependent on routine care repetition—such as prescription refills—experience revenue decline faster.

**Adverse feedback loops that exploit weaknesses**: Patient decline → increased staff turnover → declining care quality → further patient migration creates a negative spiral. Small facilities in particular lack the structural buffer assets to escape this downward cycle.

## Conclusion

Telehealth insurance expansion represents progress in patient accessibility, but it is a structural change that directly erodes the outpatient revenue base of existing offline medical facilities. This means the healthcare system is not becoming 'more efficient' but rather experiencing a 'revenue shift.' The unintended consequence of policy may be increased polarization in the medical facility ecosystem and widening regional disparities.
