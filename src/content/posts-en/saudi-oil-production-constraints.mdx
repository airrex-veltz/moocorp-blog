## Saudi Oil Well Depletion Reshapes Global Oil Market

Saudi Arabia depends on oil exports for more than 90% of national revenue. This structure has been sustained for half a century thanks to massive oil fields and the technology to extract them. But in recent years, the situation has been changing rapidly. As Saudi Aramco's oil wells deplete faster, the balance of global oil supply has begun to waver.

### The Reality of Saudi Production Capacity

Saudi Aramco is the world's largest oil company. But the world's largest oil field is also a finite resource. Oil well depletion is a natural decline phenomenon, and in Saudi Arabia's case, this process is accelerating faster than expected. Even as extraction technology advances, oil field pressure decreases over time. New oil fields must be discovered or existing ones must be reinforced, but Saudi Arabia has lagged in this investment.

A larger problem is the structural shift in demand. Saudi Arabia is also experiencing rising domestic consumption. In the Middle East, where energy prices are cheap, electricity, desalination plants, and petrochemical industries are growing rapidly. This directly reduces the volume of crude oil available for export. In other words, even if actual extraction volumes remain stable, the amount supplied to the global market decreases—a paradox.

### Vulnerability in Global Supply Chains

Saudi crude oil holds a special position in the global oil market. This is because it serves as the benchmark for price determination. If Saudi Arabia, the major OPEC member with the world's lowest extraction costs, reduces production, other oil-producing nations face limited options.

U.S. shale oil has high extraction costs. Russia and other Middle Eastern nations each face their own constraints. Ultimately, Saudi Arabia's reduced supply means there is nowhere to substitute. The contraction of low-cost production sites creates structural upward pressure on global oil prices.

As of September 21, 2026, looking at the three-month stock price trends of energy companies, this pressure is reflected in the market. ExxonMobil (XOM) recorded a gain of +15.06% over the three-month period, Chevron (CVX) recorded +17.36%, and ConocoPhillips (COP) recorded +17.03%. This is a signal that the industry broadly is showing strength.

### Signals of Improved Profitability

An interesting point is that alongside rising stock prices, revenue growth rates are also steep. Chevron's revenue growth rate reaches 53.5% on an annual basis, and ExxonMobil's is 44.1%. This is not merely stock speculation but rather reflects that actual corporate profits are expanding. In a supply-constrained environment, the value of extraction from existing oil fields also increases.

Meanwhile, ConocoPhillips (COP) has a net profit margin of 14.4%, which is higher than other companies, reflecting competitiveness in extraction cost management and asset portfolio selection.

### Deepening Structural Tension

Saudi Arabian oil well depletion is not simply a technical problem. It is a phenomenon in which three layers of pressure act simultaneously:

First, physical limits—oil fields are finite and pressure decreases.

Second, rising domestic demand—Saudi Arabia itself requires more energy.

Third, geopolitical volatility—instability in Middle Eastern conditions increases the risk of supply disruptions.

When these three factors overlap, the global oil market becomes a state without surplus or resilience. Even small shocks can cause large price swings.

### Time for Rebalancing

Ultimately, the decline in Saudi production capacity forces structural change in the global energy market. While the transition to renewable energy is underway, the vast majority of the global economy still depends on oil. In an environment where supply cannot keep pace with demand, price becomes the adjustment mechanism, and this improves profitability in the energy industry.

Whether the current rise in energy company stock prices is a temporary phenomenon or a signal of long-term structural change will be proven by time. What is certain, however, is that the decline of Saudi Arabia, which has been at the center of global oil supply, is accelerating. This means a reorganization of the entire energy market.
