## Companies Are Leaving the Cloud: The Wave of AI Infrastructure Self-Build

Over the past six months, Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon have invested billions of dollars in expanding their own GPU clusters. Large enterprises seeking to avoid cloud provider lock-in and reduce operating costs have launched an escape from 'vendor lock-in'. The results have defied expectations. As cloud providers' GPU supply monopolies break down, semiconductor companies' pricing power surges, and the global semiconductor supply chain structure is being fundamentally restructured.

## The Economics of Enterprise Data Center Internalization

A company's decision to build its own AI infrastructure is not merely a technology choice but an economic strategy. Training generative AI models requires high-performance GPUs (particularly NVIDIA's H100, H200), and using cloud providers adds a markup of 30–50%. Large enterprises make tens of millions of inference calls per year, so this markup translates into hundreds of millions of dollars in cumulative losses.

The bigger problem is vendor lock-in. Dependence on a single cloud provider's APIs and frameworks makes switching to another provider cost billions of dollars. Therefore, enterprises with high technological independence are more likely to choose building their own clusters despite higher initial capex.

## Why This Trend Is Accelerating Now: Four Driving Factors

**1. GPU Supply Recovery and the Onset of Real Price Competition**: For the past two years, H100s were in near-critical shortage, with cloud providers buying in bulk from NVIDIA and then selling to customers with a 20–40% markup. As supply began increasing in mid-2025, enterprises realized that buying chips directly and building data centers was economically viable.

**2. Declining Technical Barriers Through Maturing Open-Source Models**: As open-source models like Meta's Llama and Mistral have reached production-grade performance, enterprises have less reason to depend on closed APIs like Azure OpenAI or AWS Bedrock. Training and fine-tuning their own models has become technically feasible.

**3. Tightening Regulations and Data Protection Requirements**: The EU's AI Act, China's algorithm review, and the US AI executive order are raising regulatory risks for enterprises uploading sensitive data to third-party clouds. Companies in finance, healthcare, and government sectors are being forced toward on-premises training.

**4. Acceleration of 'Internalization' by Hyperscale Enterprises**: As giants like Meta and Google begin designing and manufacturing their own AI chips (Google's TPU, Meta's MTIA), the entire ecosystem is receiving a signal of 'self-sufficiency'. If Meta lowers its H100 dependence, NVIDIA's and cloud providers' bargaining power plummets.

## Structural Conflicts and Opportunities Created by This Phenomenon

**Deteriorating Cloud Provider Profitability**: AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud are still trying to capture software margins through AI services (SageMaker, Copilot), but declining GPU rental revenue pressures overall margins. Some are already transitioning to 'AI-dedicated data centers', a space with high initial capex but uncertain medium-to-long-term profitability.

**Semiconductor Companies' Shift in Bargaining Power**: NVIDIA is expanding direct sales channels with enterprise customers to reduce its dependence on cloud providers. As direct sales volumes increase, NVIDIA gains stronger negotiating leverage with manufacturers. As a result, GPU chip prices are likely to actually decline.

**Geopolitical Supply Chain Fragmentation**: Building one's own clusters ultimately means committing to long-term supply contracts. US companies will gravitate toward NVIDIA (California), Chinese companies toward Huawei's proprietary chips, and European companies toward local solutions like Graphcore. This accelerates the 'fragmentation' of the GPU market and raises the risk of regional semiconductor supply chain fractures.

## Conclusion

Enterprise AI infrastructure internalization may appear to pursue 'technological independence' on the surface, but the real driver is economics. Cloud providers' markup structures and vendor lock-in risks are pushing companies into direct capex investments. In this process, semiconductor companies like NVIDIA gain pricing power, but simultaneously the risk of regional supply chain fragmentation grows.
