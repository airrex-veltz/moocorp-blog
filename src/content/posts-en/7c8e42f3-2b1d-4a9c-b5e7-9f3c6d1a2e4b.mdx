## Why Wage Increases Do Not Make Automation a Weapon

Vietnam's shoe manufacturing wages are rising. Southeast Asia is no exception. Historically, this region has served as a base for advanced economy manufacturing with low-wage labor. As that advantage fades, manufacturers are rushing toward automation. But automation protects against only one thing—the pace at which remaining margins shrink as wage-driven cost growth cools.

## The Dilemma of Low-Wage Industries

Shoe manufacturing is a paradigmatic low-cost industry. Since competitive leverage is wages, rising wages directly trigger profit collapse. The reason Vietnam and similar countries became global suppliers in this field—and the same reason they become difficult to rely on—is wages. When wages rise simultaneously across Southeast Asia, regional differentiation disappears. Two options remain: automate or surrender profitability.

## Automation Is Not Competitive Advantage: Four Reasons

**1. Technology commoditization**: Shoe manufacturing automation technology is already accessible to both advanced and developing economies. Patent or technology barriers are not high, so when one player invests, others quickly follow. When everyone automates identically, cost advantage vanishes.

**2. Capital recovery is impossible**: Margins in low-cost industries are thin to begin with. There is no room to recover capital invested in automation equipment. Moreover, automation happens only once. As technology evolves and competition intensifies, continuous additional investment is required, but there are no margins to bear that cost.

**3. Wage growth rate exceeds automation pace**: Wages rise annually, but automation is a multi-year project. Equipment installation, workforce retraining, line optimization—wages have already risen in the interim. The cost increase automation tries to contain grows faster than automation investment.

**4. Intensified regional competition**: When all Southeast Asian countries invest in automation, relative advantage remains wages. Yet the only option now is migration to lower-wage countries. But those countries face identical automation pressure, triggering a vicious cycle.

## Margin Collapse Is Irreversible

Automation investment expands fixed costs. Fixed cost expansion in low-cost industries is risky. If demand falls, there is no way to recover it. Moreover, as automation technology commoditizes, product prices fall. When prices fall and fixed costs rise, profitability collapses doubly.

This is the reality of "low-wage-based industry standardization." Everyone invests in technology anyone can do, everyone uses the same technology, cost competition becomes fiercer, and margins eventually fall to unrecoverable levels. Automation does not solve the problem. The structure itself, not technology, is locked in.
