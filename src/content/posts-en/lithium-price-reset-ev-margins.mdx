## Lithium prices drop 30%, signal margin restructuring in automotive

In the first half of 2026, lithium prices fell over 30% compared to the same period last year. As production ramped in Australia and Chile and Chinese battery makers flooded the market, ton prices dropped below $9,000. On the surface, all EV makers should benefit, but reality is different. **F** (Ford) and **GM** (General Motors), traditional large automakers, can immediately convert this price decline into margin, while Tesla and emerging EV startups struggle to lower their already-reduced battery purchase prices further.

## Battery contract asymmetry

Lithium accounts for roughly 15–20% of total battery pack costs. However, how price declines translate to final selling prices depends on each company's negotiating power. **F** and **GM** included "pass-through clauses" in their 2025–2026 long-term battery supply contracts. If raw material prices fall, purchase unit costs automatically decline. Tesla has already locked in battery cell costs below $110/kWh, and startups are bound to higher prices on short-term contracts. As a result, raw material savings become battery makers' profit or disappear under stronger negotiators' pressure.

## Why incumbent makers are reviving now: four pressure factors

**1. Chinese battery makers' price war**: CATL and BYD supply battery cells at low prices on the spot market to gain global share. This acts as added downward pressure on long-term contract prices. **F** and **GM**, managing multiple suppliers, have more choices.

**2. EV demand slowdown**: Global EV sales growth in 2026 has dipped from a forecast 20% to around 12%, loosening battery supply. Oversupply fuels price drops, and incumbents can harvest that as margin.

**3. Favorable regulatory environment**: Carbon deadlines in the US and Europe have been pushed back, extending EV transition timelines. Incumbents can protect internal combustion profit while minimizing EV conversion costs. Startups face cash burn pressure.

**4. **TSM** semiconductor supply stabilization**: As EV control chip (MCU, SoC) shortages clear, battery pack maturity rises and incumbent automakers' quality reputation recovers. **TSM** is prioritizing 28nm process allocation to long-term customers like **F** and **GM**.

## Structural advantage: incumbent competitive edge

**Scale dominance**: **F** sells 5 million units annually, **GM** 4 million. The power to pressure suppliers through total battery purchase volume is incomparable to startups. If lithium falls by $1, a single unit saves hundreds of dollars—nearly a full margin band.

**Established distribution network bypass**: **F** and **GM** sell EVs through already-established dealer networks. Startups must invest in new channels like online and concept stores. Lithium cost savings convert existing distribution advantage into margin.

**Structural weakness: startup isolation**: Emerging EV makers are diversifying battery supply but still rely on one or two suppliers. Negotiating power is weak, and competitive footholds remain only in innovation—high energy density, durability.

## Summary

Lithium collapse is industry-wide tailwind for EVs, but due to battery purchasing structure and scale differences, incumbents pocket more gains. **F** and **GM** have high odds of improving H2 2026 EV lineup margins by 5–10 percentage points. Semiconductor suppliers like **TSM** also benefit from growing long-term customer demand. Emerging EV makers must offset scale disadvantage through battery innovation and differentiated user experience.
