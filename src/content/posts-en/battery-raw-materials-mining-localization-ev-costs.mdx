## Electric Vehicle Battery Wars: Supply Chains Reorganized by Mining Localization

Surging battery demand driven by expanding EV adoption is intensifying geographic concentration in raw material mining. The localization of nickel and cobalt mining—two critical minerals—goes beyond simple resource price increases, fundamentally reshaping automakers' cost structures. Supply chain reorganization has already begun.

## The Heart of the Battery: The Role of Nickel and Cobalt

Modern lithium-ion batteries consist of four key components: cathode material, anode material, electrolyte, and separator. Nickel and cobalt, contained in cathode material, are critical factors determining electrochemical energy density. In particular, nickel boosts energy density while cobalt ensures structural stability. Meeting the three simultaneous objectives of increased battery capacity, extended driving range, and cost reduction requires precise proportioning of these two elements.

Mining of these minerals is highly localized. Deposits are concentrated in just a few countries—Indonesia, the Philippines, the Congo, Russia, Australia, and others—while mining infrastructure, political risk, and environmental regulations vary significantly from country to country. This characteristic is the source of supply chain inflexibility.

## Why Cost Pressure Is Intensifying Now: Four Factors

**1. Accelerating Battery Demand Curve**

EV market growth has exceeded expectations. It is not simply a matter of sales volume. Battery capacity per vehicle is also increasing. As energy storage systems (ESS) and grid-stabilization batteries grow in parallel, nickel and cobalt demand has surpassed supply. There is no prospect of supply-demand imbalance being resolved in the short term.

**2. Mining Nations' Supply Chain Control Strategies**

Mineral-exporting nations have begun raising export taxes or restricting exports altogether. Indonesia is limiting nickel ore exports and promoting domestic battery manufacturing. The Congo has tightened conditions for cobalt mining license renewal. These countries prefer exporting batteries or refined products rather than raw ore. This creates direct pressure on manufacturers to shift focus to local production.

**3. The Manufacturer's Supply Diversification Dilemma**

Automakers want to increase suppliers to avoid single-source dependence. However, developing new mines takes years. Expanding production at existing mines is also blocked by environmental permitting. Thus, realistic options are limited. Some manufacturers have adopted "hybrid strategies"—investing in secondary batteries (recycling spent batteries) or increasing the proportion of cathode materials that reduce cobalt content—but these too are not short-term solutions.

**4. Prolonged Timeline for Alternative Technologies**

Next-generation technologies that fundamentally change battery chemistry—solid-state batteries, sodium-ion batteries, and others—are in development with targets beyond the 2030s. Theoretically, these could reduce nickel and cobalt dependence, but mass production requires additional time. In the interim, lithium-ion batteries will certainly dominate the market. This means the three factors above will operate for at least five years or longer.

## Intensifying Structural Weaknesses in Supply Chains

**Polarized Bargaining Power**: Mining nations' negotiating position strengthens. Battery manufacturers and automakers have no choice but to accept price increases in the absence of alternative suppliers. The price gap between short-term and long-term contracts widens.

**Politicization of Risk Costs**: Policy changes in mining nations—export restrictions, mining license cancellations, stricter environmental regulations—can occur without warning. This complicates capital investment decisions and undermines long-term contract terms.

**Limits of the Circular Economy**: Nickel and cobalt recovery through recycling is technically possible but economically unviable. If costs are higher or quality inconsistent compared to newly imported minerals, manufacturers will not adopt it. Thus, excess demand continues to depend on new mining.

## Summary

The electric vehicle battery revolution has created a new minerals war. As localized mining, intensified mining-nation intervention, and limited alternative pathways converge, supply chain inflexibility is becoming structural. Resolving this tension requires technological transition (next-generation batteries), improved recycling economics, or new governance between mining nations and manufacturers. In the interim, cost pressure is unlikely to ease.
