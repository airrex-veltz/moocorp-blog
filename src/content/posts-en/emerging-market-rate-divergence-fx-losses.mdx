While emerging-market central banks are cutting rates, the United States alone maintains high rates. This policy divergence has widened currency fluctuations significantly, and as a result, is visibly inflating foreign exchange conversion losses for global multinational corporations. Both US companies and emerging-market companies now show no sign of reducing currency losses on a quarterly basis.

The indicators clearly reveal the current situation. **UUP** (US Dollar Index tracking ETF) closed at $29.13 on November 6, 2026, up 3.52% over three months. Over a longer timeframe (six months), it is approaching the upper end of the $27.30–$29.25 band. This signals that the US interest rate stance is supporting a stronger dollar. Meanwhile, **IEF** (7–10 year US Treasury ETF) is at $91.86 (as of November 6, 2026), up 2.69% over one month, showing that longer-term rates remain elevated.

Demand for dividend-paying assets is sending mixed signals. **XLU** (Utilities sector ETF) has risen 5.02% over one month to $43.13 (as of November 6, 2026) and is valued at a P/E of 17.7. Capital chasing dividend income appears strong, but the structure has currency losses eating into realized gains. Meanwhile, **GLD** (Gold ETF) has declined 7.46% over three months to $372.52 (as of November 6, 2026), suggesting that capital flight from emerging-market weakness is limited.

The first pressure factor is **renewed dollar strength**. As UUP rises 3.52% over three months and hovers near its high, demand to acquire dollars continues as long as US rates remain elevated. Emerging-market currencies cannot escape relative weakness, which consequently increases interest burdens for emerging-market companies carrying dollar-denominated debt. At the same time, if US multinational companies hold emerging-market assets, their valuations contract in dollar terms.

The second is **capital withdrawal signaled by emerging-market rate cuts**. Lowering rates means yields on emerging-market assets decline, prompting asset managers to redirect capital to advanced economies. This capital outflow drives emerging-market currency weakness, and elevated US rates accelerate the process. As a result, FX losses accumulate in emerging-market funds and global funds with high emerging-market weightings.

The third is **the collision between dividend appeal and currency pressure**. Assets with high dividends, such as utilities and financials like XLU, are undervalued relative to their yields in a high-rate environment. Institutional investors track dividend income, but simultaneously, earnings calculated in emerging-market currencies lose value when converted to dollars. As FX losses are disclosed during quarterly dividend announcements, the actual value of dividend income comes into question.

The fourth is **the visibility of currency losses in corporate financial statements**. At current exchange rates, companies' asset and liability translation losses accumulate each quarter. The longer elevated rates persist as shown by IEF, the greater the dollar-denominated valuation losses on emerging-market assets. For companies with production facilities or sales operations in emerging markets in particular, as these losses become explicit in headquarters reports, shareholder sentiment comes under pressure.

**Structurally, the strong and weak diverge.** Multinational corporations without dollar debt and with US-centered operations benefit in this environment. Conversely, companies with large emerging-market revenue shares or high emerging-market asset holdings have no choice but to see FX losses swell in their financial statements during this period. That emerging-market central banks are signaling a willingness to cut rates suggests the likelihood of this gap narrowing in the near term is low.

Should the current trend persist, dividend pressure on multinational corporations is likely to intensify. Companies seeking to maintain high dividends may reduce emerging-market operations or cut costs to offset currency losses. Alternatively, there is a risk that persistent disclosure of quarterly FX losses will send negative signals to share prices. For this gap to narrow, either emerging-market policy rates must rebound or US rates must fall. Until then, expanding FX losses will remain a key driver of corporate earnings volatility.
