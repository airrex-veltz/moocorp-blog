# Shipping Carbon Regulations and the Pass-Through of Green Fuel Conversion Costs to Freight Rates

The international shipping industry stands at a critical juncture in its transition to carbon neutrality. As regulations tighten—including the International Maritime Organization's (IMO) 2050 carbon neutrality target and the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM)—shipping companies face the adoption of green fuels as an imperative choice. This article examines how the additional costs incurred in this process reshape freight rate structures and impact profitability.

## Current State of International Carbon Regulations

The IMO has set a target to reduce the carbon intensity of international shipping by 40% relative to 2008 levels by 2030, with carbon neutrality by 2050. More immediately, since January 2023, the Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) regulations have been tightened in phases, and from 2026, a carbon pricing mechanism component is expected to be added to the Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) system.

The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its transitional phase in January 2024 and is expected to begin requiring shipping companies to purchase emission allowances from 2026 onwards. This will significantly increase carbon costs for cargo destined for European ports.

The South Korean government has also committed to participating in maritime decarbonization and has implemented such measures as strengthening the Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEDI) for vessels and regulating fuel sulfur content (IMO 2020) in phases. The focus is now shifting to support policies for green fuel adoption.

## Cost Structure of Green Fuel Conversion

Green fuels under consideration include methanol, ammonia, and hydrogen, but methanol and liquefied natural gas (LNG) currently represent the most practical options.

**Methanol**: Vessel acquisition costs rise approximately 10–15% compared to sulfur fuel oil, while fuel costs transition from bunker oil at $800–1,000 per barrel to methanol at $600–800 per ton (with approximately 1.5 times higher volumetric consumption), requiring a comprehensive assessment of the overall impact.

**LNG-Powered Vessels**: Acquisition costs are 20–25% higher than comparable sulfur fuel-powered vessels. LNG fuel costs exhibit high volatility per ton but have trended at or slightly above bunker oil levels in recent years.

Retrofitting existing vessels is far less efficient than new construction, making large-scale conversion impractical; as a result, the transition will necessarily occur through selective ordering of eco-friendly new vessels. This also implies that shipbuilders will see a selective increase in new orders.

## Impact on Freight Rates

The mechanism for passing through carbon costs operates through two channels.

**First, Direct Carbon Cost Addition**: When the EU CBAM is fully implemented, cargo destined for European ports is expected to incur carbon costs of approximately $50–200 per ton. Converting this to per-TEU basis for container vessels yields $100–500. In the current tight freight rate environment, this represents a substantial cost increase.

**Second, Green Fuel Premium**: Higher operating costs of green vessels must necessarily be reflected in freight rates. Replacing 30–40% of the global fleet with green vessels is estimated to require 5–7 years, during which the green fuel premium will exert upward pressure on freight rates.

Carbon costs are particularly burdensome on long-distance routes (such as Asia–Europe), making freight rate increases more likely on these lanes. By region, the ranking of carbon cost burden is expected to be: EU-bound routes > Americas-bound routes > intra-Asia regional routes.

## Reshaping Shipping Company Profitability

Green fuel transition will likely polarize shipping company profitability.

**Advanced Shipping Companies**: Early adoption carries a significant capital burden, but offers the advantage of regulatory compliance leadership and the ability to meet customers' ESG requirements. Gradual fleet renewal also enables operational cost efficiency.

**Late Movers**: Once regulations clearly tighten, late entrants may be forced to order green vessels in bulk, incurring cost pressures from concentrated ordering during vessel price appreciation.

Meanwhile, a divergence will also widen between companies able to pass through carbon costs to freight rates and those unable to do so. Large container lines and long-haul chemical tanker operators enjoy pricing power, while small bulk carriers and niche-route specialists face weak bargaining positions and may have to absorb carbon costs entirely.

## ZIM and DAC's Positioning

**ZIM (Zim Integrated Shipping Services Ltd.)**: Stock price $29.67 (as of October 2, 2026), up 25.12% over the past three months. With a P/E ratio of 25.8, ZIM maintains a business structure centered on general cargo and containers. Net profit margin of 2.1% is in line with industry averages, while revenue growth of 8.9% slightly exceeds market growth.

Given ZIM's significant exposure to Asia routes with high container import/export volumes, it is highly exposed to EU carbon regulations. The initial capital burden of green vessel adoption could be reflected in its P/E ratio.

**DAC (Danaos Corporation)**: Stock price $164.88 (as of October 2, 2026), up 32.48% over the past three months, significantly outperforming ZIM. A P/E ratio of 5.6 suggests relative market undervaluation, while a net profit margin of 51.3% represents the industry's highest level. Though revenue growth of 4.7% lags ZIM, cash generation from its high profit margin is significantly superior.

DAC's high profit margin is attributed to efficient operation of its existing fleet and expanded spreads in ship chartering. Its financial capacity to absorb green fuel conversion costs is superior to ZIM's.

## Investment Perspective During the Regulatory Transition

Tightening carbon regulations offer near-term freight rate upside but in the medium to long term, cost structure improvement is the critical factor.

Shipping companies face three strategic choices:
1. **Early Investment Strategy**: Order green vessels ahead of regulatory tightening to secure both early-mover cost advantages and regulatory compliance leadership.
2. **Follow-On Strategy**: Wait for technology and pricing to stabilize before concentrated investment.
3. **Portfolio Diversification**: Avoid concentration in specific routes or vessel types; reallocate the fleet toward routes and vessel types with lower regulatory exposure.

Synthesizing current market conditions and individual shipping companies' financial positions suggests that large carriers with high profit generation and low P/E ratios enjoy a more favorable position during the carbon regulatory transition. However, this will largely depend on regulatory direction, technological advancement, fuel cost trends, and other uncertainties.

## Conclusion

Maritime decarbonization represents not benevolent regulation but a signal of market restructuring. Initially, profitability will improve through freight rate increases, but in the medium to long term, outcomes will be determined by progress in green fuel technology and infrastructure, as well as each shipping company's investment timing.

Amid the wave of regulations, companies with sound financial conditions and superior operational efficiency are likely to survive, and they are expected to secure profitability as market concentration intensifies. From an investor's perspective, the focus should be on structural shifts in cost competitiveness, not mere freight rate cycle recovery.
