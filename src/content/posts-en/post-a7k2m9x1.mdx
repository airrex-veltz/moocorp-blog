## The Economics of Taiwan's Semiconductor Dominance

Over the past 20 years, the global semiconductor industry has shown a sharp trend toward geographic concentration. In the advanced foundry sector, commonly referred to as the "chip frontier," TSMC (Taiwan) holds approximately 54% of the market share. When Samsung is included, Korean companies account for over 70%. Yet what deserves greater attention is not merely production volume but the concentration of technological capability. Advanced process nodes below 5 nanometers are virtually a TSMC monopoly, a reality reflected in assessments that as of 2023, TSMC's technological capabilities led competing peers by 2–3 years. This structure is not coincidental but the result of 30 years of economic choices and technological accumulation.

TSMC's dominance stems from two strategic choices made since its founding in 1987. First, it chose pure foundry specialization over vertically integrated memory chip manufacturing. This meant committing to capital investments equaling 15–20% of Taiwan's GDP annually on manufacturing facilities. In 2023, TSMC's capital expenditure reached USD 36.6 billion, far exceeding Intel's USD 22.5 billion. Second, it benefited from the geopolitical distance Taiwan provides. Since the Cold War's end, the United States has recognized Taiwan's importance as an East Asian semiconductor production hub, allowing TSMC to position itself as a trusted partner to American companies (Apple, Qualcomm, AMD, and others). Consequently, over 95% of the world's fabless semiconductor design companies have partnered with TSMC.

This concentration created clear cost advantages from an economic efficiency standpoint. TSMC's per-chip manufacturing cost is 20–30% cheaper than competitors, stemming from economies of scale and accumulated technological learning curves. As companies move to advanced processes, capital requirements grow exponentially (building a single 3-nanometer fab costs roughly USD 20 billion), and TSMC's high utilization rates and diversified customer base distribute these risks. Intel, meanwhile, failed in foundry partnerships with IBM and GlobalFoundries during the same period, and when it announced its foundry pivot in 2021, it already trailed TSMC by two or more generations. Samsung similarly could not dedicate over USD 20 billion annually to foundry infrastructure, constrained by cash flows from memory chip production.

## Geopolitical Risk Materialization

Yet from 2020 onward, this efficiency structure revealed geopolitical vulnerabilities. First, there is supply chain disruption risk. The 2021 Samsung-SK Hynix foundry power outage, 2023 TSMC fan defect causing partial production halts, and the absence of diversified suppliers for critical semiconductor equipment (EUV lithography tools, etching systems, and others) demonstrated that a single technical failure or logistics blockade could paralyze the global electronics industry. The 2021 global chip shortage was a concrete example of this risk, with the automotive sector alone suffering direct losses exceeding USD 210 billion.

Second, the U.S.-China technology rivalry has directly targeted Taiwan's semiconductor corridors. The 2022 U.S. CHIPS and Science Act, 2023 Japanese semiconductor export restrictions, and heightened Chinese military exercises around Taiwan have weaponized TSMC's supply chain. Much as the Ukraine war disrupted neon gas supplies (Ukraine accounts for 70% of the world's ultra-high-purity neon), tensions across the Taiwan Strait have reached the level of threatening the entire semiconductor industry's survival. For U.S. policymakers and major defense and technology companies, TSMC dependence is no longer a matter of efficiency but of national security.

Third is the supply-demand imbalance risk. As TSMC concentrates production capacity on high-margin products (iPhone, GPU, data center chips), low-margin semiconductors (automotive, IoT) face shortages. From 2021 to 2023, automotive chip shortages cost the auto industry over 3.5 million units of lost production, mostly from TSMC's mature 28-nanometer and older processes. Yet TSMC continues prioritizing capital investment in advanced nodes for profitability reasons. This is a rational business decision, but from an industry-wide perspective it creates structural distortion.

## Industry Realignment: The Economics of Policy-Driven Investment

In response, since 2023 the United States, Europe, and Japan have massively increased policy-driven investment in semiconductor self-sufficiency. The U.S. CHIPS and Science Act provided USD 52.4 billion in direct subsidies. The EU Chips Act allocated EUR 43 billion. Japan included 23 trillion yen in its investment plans. Even South Korea announced K-Semiconductor investments of KRW 1.1 trillion. These investments cannot be explained by economic logic alone.

Concretely, Intel received USD 8.5 billion in CHIPS Act support to begin constructing fabs in Arizona and Ohio, yet the investment's projected economic return stands below 10%. Were TSMC to build comparable facilities, per-unit manufacturing costs would be 30–40% lower. Samsung similarly is constructing foundry fabs in Texas, with operational cost gaps alone estimated at USD 50 billion annually. This goes beyond "domestic industry protection" into territory of "uneconomic investment for geopolitical redundancy"—aiming for minimum national self-sufficiency rather than global optimization.

The result has initiated structural realignment. First, process segmentation. TSMC and Samsung will maintain dominant positions in cutting-edge nodes below 5 nanometers, but mid-tier processes (28–7 nanometers) will see expanded foundry capacity from Intel and IBM, while mature nodes (28 nanometers and above) gain market share from Chinese players (SMIC) and emerging Southeast Asian fabs. Such a structure would have been unthinkable just three years earlier.

Second, major customers are moving to multi-sourcing strategies. From 2024, Apple has begun outsourcing portions of M4 and A18 chip production to Samsung's 3-nanometer process. AMD has contracted with Intel Foundry for chip manufacturing. Even Nvidia shows signs of seeking alternatives to TSMC exclusivity. These companies are not voluntarily choosing TSMC alternatives; rather, U.S. government pressure and policy subsidies to Samsung and Intel create sufficient incentive.

## Winners and Losers: Asymmetric Adjustment

In this process, economic gains and losses distribute asymmetrically.

**TSMC:** In the short term, it remains the overwhelming beneficiary. Of 2023 revenues of USD 76.6 billion, operating margins exceed 50%. Through geographic diversification (India and Germany investments), it mitigates geopolitical risk while securing its highest-margin customer base (Apple, Nvidia). However, as policy pressure expands U.S. and European fab investment obligations, margins will gradually decline. TSMC's Arizona fab reportedly costs over USD 50 billion annually more to operate than its Taiwan facilities.

**Intel:** Intel's foundry transformation is impossible without U.S. government support (USD 8.5 billion). Quarterly losses accumulated throughout 2024, and foundry operations are expected to need 3–5 years to narrow the technology gap with TSMC. However, under the policy objective of strengthening U.S. semiconductor self-sufficiency, continued government support is likely. In essence, it has become an organization surviving on political necessity rather than market efficiency.

**Samsung:** Samsung's foundry strategy is weakened by portfolio conflicts with memory semiconductors. Intensifying price competition in memory chips makes large-scale foundry investment difficult. Samsung's foundry operations posted losses in 2024. Yet by securing South Korean government investment incentives and USD 3.7 billion from the U.S. CHIPS Act, it maintains survival footing.

**SMIC and Emerging Fabs:** China's state-owned SMIC cannot import advanced equipment due to U.S. export controls, yet it is rapidly expanding market share in mature processes (28 nanometers and above). As of 2023, SMIC's operating margins reached the low 20%, lower than TSMC's 50% but gaining competitive advantage in segments with lower technological barriers.

## Economic Costs and Structural Distortions

The core question is the impact of this "security-justified investment" on economic efficiency. According to the World Bank and IMF estimates, the ongoing semiconductor industry geographic dispersion policies will increase annual global semiconductor production costs by USD 30–50 billion over the next five years. This translates directly into higher consumer prices for PCs, smartphones, and servers, potentially worsening digital access in developing nations.

Also emerging is the risk of over-investment from excess policy-driven competition. As Intel, Samsung, GlobalFoundries, UMC, and other players simultaneously expand foundry capacity, foundry supply is forecast to exceed demand by over 20% by 2028. This risks repeating the structural over-investment cycles of the late 1990s semiconductor industry.

Finally, there is potential slowdown in technological innovation velocity. TSMC's monopoly clearly created inefficiencies, yet it simultaneously enabled capital concentration on technological development. As industry dispersion proceeds, each fab will focus on its own process improvements while potentially falling behind in tracking the latest global technologies. In the end, the global semiconductor industry's innovation pace faces slowdown risk.

## Conclusion: The Mismatch Between Policy and Markets

Geopolitical realignment of the semiconductor supply chain is now unavoidable reality. Yet the divergence between each nation's "security first" policy and "cost minimization" economics is widening. TSMC remains the most efficient semiconductor manufacturer, but political risk now offsets its superiority. As a result, the world is shifting toward a more expensive and slower semiconductor industry, and the economic costs of this transition will eventually manifest as diminished technological innovation and weakened global competitiveness. For investors, this means betting on long-term structural shifts while accepting deteriorating near-term investment efficiency (ROIC).
