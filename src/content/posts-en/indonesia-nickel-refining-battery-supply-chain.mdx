## Indonesia's Nickel Refining Redesigns Global Battery Supply Chains

Indonesia is forcing local refining capacity through its ban on raw ore exports, rapidly reshaping the supply map for global battery manufacturers. With metal refining concentrated in a single country through vertical integration from ore to metal, traditional mining giants like **VALE** and **RIO** face shifting supply relationships and eroding negotiating power.

## Indonesia's Export Ban and Refining Industry's Explosive Growth

Indonesia holds approximately 24% of global nickel reserves. Since 2020, it has banned raw ore exports entirely, driving investment in local refining and hydrometallurgy. The results have been dramatic. Indonesia's refining capacity grew from roughly 9 million tons in 2020 to 18 million tons in 2025—doubling in five years. Meanwhile, ore sales from VALE (Brazil) and Rio Tinto (Australia) have stalled or declined.

Global nickel supply from Indonesia now accounts for roughly 45% as of 2024. Battery makers like Samsung SDI and LG Energy (South Korea), CATL and BYD (China), can no longer secure supply from diverse sources through ore intermediaries. Instead, they must enter direct contracts with Indonesian refiners or make local investments.

## Why This Shift Is Intensifying: Four Pressure Factors

**1. Crumbling barriers to refining technology**: Early refining expertise was dominated by China and Russia, but five years of local investment have equipped Indonesian refiners with commercial-grade capability. Raw ore alone is no longer competitive.

**2. Diverging environmental standards**: Australian and Canadian mines face constraints from ESG regulation, while Indonesia maintains favorable regulation by framing localization as 'green economy.' This deepens battery makers' preference for shorter supply chains.

**3. Sharpening battery price competition**: Nickel accounts for 10–15% of electric vehicle battery costs. Battery makers want to escape the multi-layer margin structure that runs from ore import through refining to finished cells. Long-term contracts with refiners enable 1–3% cost reduction through eliminating middlemen.

**4. Geopolitical supply chain realignment**: The U.S. and EU preference for 'allied-nation' supply chains weakens the Indonesia–Australia–Canada axis. A China–Indonesia–Taiwan ore-to-refining-to-battery route emerges as an alternative, causing VALE and RIO to lose South Korean and Japanese customers.

## Why VALE and RIO Still Persist: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Ore scale and quality**: VALE and RIO mines hold advantages in size and low-cost production. Indonesian ore falls short in nickel content, so certain battery chemistries still need premium ore. Yet this advantage is narrowing steadily.

**Refining technology gaps are temporary**: Indonesian hydrometallurgical refining still shows lower yields for premium products (purity above 99.99%). Yet this gap is expected to close within five years.

**Weakness—concentrated sourcing risk**: Battery makers now face greater exposure to Indonesian political risk. In 2023, volcanic eruption and heavy rains halted refining for two months.

## Conclusion

Indonesia's localization policy is eroding VALE and RIO's near-term supply position. Yet the technology gap and regulatory asymmetry will not persist forever. Battery makers will grow reliant on Indonesian refining while maintaining ore import diversification for supply redundancy. The result: both sides weaken, but Indonesia gains relative advantage.
