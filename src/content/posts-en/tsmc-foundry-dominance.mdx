## TSMC Reinforces Foundry Dominance Through Technology Monopoly

TSMC's dominance in the semiconductor foundry market is strengthening. Its lead in cutting-edge process technology and overwhelming profitability are widening the gap with competitors, and this extends beyond mere market share to reshape the industry structure itself. However, whether this advantage is sustainable or harbors structural weaknesses will be crucial to future investment decisions.

## TSMC's Foundry Dominance Structure

**TSMC** (closing price $424.37 on October 26, 2026, as of October 26, 2026) maintains extraordinary profitability of 51.1% net profit margin even as AI chip designers' options narrow. Its revenue growth rate this year reaches 50.9% annually. **INTC** ($90.47) operates at a net loss with -19.8% net profit margin and revenue growth of just 25.4%. **SSNLF** ($65.21), meanwhile, shows a healthy 30.8% net profit margin but lower than TSMC, and despite outstanding 130% revenue growth, its smaller base means the absolute gap in revenue and profit versus TSMC is larger. Market capitalization comparison is even starker. TSMC stands at $2.27 trillion, INTC at $493.7 billion, and Samsung at $424.6 billion—TSMC alone exceeds both competitors combined.

## Why TSMC Dominates Now: Four Key Factors

**1. Cutting-Edge Process Technology Monopoly**: In advanced high-density processes of 3 nanometers and below, TSMC leads competitors by 1–2 years. As AI chip competition concentrates on cutting-edge node scaling, the structure requiring passage through TSMC has become entrenched. This technology gap, built on investment scale, research experience, and accumulated process knowledge, is difficult to close.

**2. Investment Capacity and Economies of Scale**: TSMC's market capitalization and cash flow overwhelm competitors' investment capacity. Intel faces constraints on large-scale investment due to losses, while Samsung's dispersed investment between memory and foundry limits its ability to concentrate on foundry. TSMC can pour tens of billions of dollars annually into foundry equipment alone.

**3. Customer Lock-In Structure**: Moving a chip designed at TSMC to a competitor's process requires re-optimizing the entire mask set. This creates a 'lock-in' effect demanding months of time and hundreds of millions of dollars. Therefore, designers stick with TSMC if its performance suffices.

**4. Geopolitical Concentration and Supply Chain Restructuring**: U.S. sanctions on China and security tightening concentrate developed-nation foundry demand more heavily on TSMC. A paradoxical situation has emerged in which the U.S. and EU, wanting advanced foundry capacity domestically, are effectively forcing equipment investment by TSMC. This further strengthens TSMC's customer stickiness.

## Yet TSMC Persists: Structural Strengths and Limitations

**Sustainability of Technology Leadership Unclear**: U.S. chip sanctions and ASML equipment supply restrictions may slow TSMC's next-generation process development. Conversely, if Intel and Samsung concentrate investment, they could narrow the gap. The pace of innovation remains unpredictable.

**Fundamental Geopolitical Risk**: TSMC is located in Taiwan. If geopolitical tensions escalate, customers will likely demand risk diversification. Indeed, efforts to attract TSMC plants to the U.S. and Japan prove this. Yet since TSMC operates these facilities too, it only increases management burden rather than providing fundamental solutions.

## Summary

TSMC dominates the foundry market through compounded advantages of technology, scale, and customer lock-in, and as of 2026, this advantage is strengthening. However, the technology gap offers no sustainability guarantee, and geopolitical risk remains. What matters for investors is not TSMC's current advantage, but discerning where competitive dynamics will lead in 3–5 years and how regulation and sanctions will shape that trajectory.
