## Regulatory Costs Reshape Recycling Industry Pricing Structure

The expansion of extended producer responsibility (EPR) regulations is fundamentally reshaping the revenue structures of plastic waste management companies. Cost increases from expanding the scope of regulated items are making the traditional collection model—gathering collection, sorting, and incineration fees from municipalities and consumers—unsustainable.

**Waste Management** (**WM**) and **Republic Services** (**RSG**) recorded declines of -11.98% and -4.77%, respectively, over the past three months (as of October 1, 2026). While the broader industry moved in the same direction, the difference in the performance of these two companies suggests divergent margin pressures stemming from their differing paces of regulatory adaptation.

## The 'Cost Inversion' Structure of Waste Management

Traditionally, waste management companies offset collection costs through dual revenue streams: municipal fees and government subsidies plus scrap revenues from recycling material sales. However, EPR expansion breaks this equation.

As producers assume direct or outsourced recycling responsibilities, more stringent standards for sorting, cleaning, and storage are being enforced. WM's current net profit margin is 11.1%, while RSG's is 12.9% (as of October 1, 2026). To maintain profitability margins while absorbing additional regulatory costs, both companies face inevitable margin pressure in other segments. Indeed, WM's P/E ratio is 28.9, comparable to RSG's 30.1, yet expectations for earnings improvement are weakening.

## Why This System Is Shaking Now: Four Factors

**1. Cost Impact of Expanding Producer Responsibility**: Historically, municipalities bore much of the recycling cost. EPR expansion shifts this burden to producers. For waste companies, this creates a paradox: revenue sources narrow while processing standards rise. In particular, mandatory material-based plastic sorting requires investment in sorting labor and technology.

**2. Intensifying Volatility in Scrap Revenues**: Recycling material prices are sensitive to global variables like oil prices. Simultaneously, as regulations define more volume as mandatory recycling materials, quality variation widens. Low-grade recycled plastic cannot be sold, leaving only processing costs to accumulate.

**3. Infrastructure Modernization Pressure**: Meeting new sorting and cleaning standards requires substantial facility upgrades. Rising annual capital expenditures (CapEx) immediately pressure margins. Both WM and RSG have increased their CapEx ratios over the past two years, and this is now being reflected in near-term cash flow and stock performance.

**4. Exit of Small and Mid-Sized Operators**: Small recycling processors cannot afford regulatory compliance costs and are exiting the market. While this increases market concentration in the short term, it simultaneously intensifies unit price competition. Large operators survive on scale, but the path to margin improvement narrows further.

## Why WM and RSG Despite These Challenges: Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strengths**: The scale of both companies (WM market cap $81.6 billion, RSG $64.1 billion) gives them the capital and credit to absorb regulatory investments. Their integrated collection, processing, and sales networks also create cost structures that smaller competitors cannot replicate. Even as profit margins narrow, absolute earnings can be preserved.

**Weaknesses**: Yet earnings growth is slower compared to revenue growth rates (WM 4.0%, RSG 4.6%) and net profit margins (11.1% and 12.9%, respectively). If regulations expand further, margin improvement becomes financially almost impossible. Investors have already reassessed this, which is why stock prices have declined ahead of the fundamental deterioration.

## Conclusion

EPR expansion forces a 'role redefinition' for waste companies. The transition from cost collector to technology investor is unavoidable, yet this shift erodes near-term margins. Even large operators like WM and RSG cannot escape this structural pressure, and their stock prices already reflect that reality.
