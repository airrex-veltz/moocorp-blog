Natural gas is both a key raw material and a cost in nitrogen fertilizer production. This is why global nitrogen fertilizer manufacturers are sensitive to input costs. Energy costs represent a significant share of production costs. This structure governs margin fluctuations at major companies like **NTR** (Nutrien Ltd.) and **MOS** (The Mosaic Company).

Look at recent market signals. As of November 4, 2026, **NTR** trades at $71.75, up 8.31% over the past three months. **MOS** trades at $21.31 but is down 8.42% over the same period. Entirely different trajectories.

This divergence stems from differences in corporate fundamentals. **NTR** posts a net profit margin of 8.7% and annual revenue growth of 4.0%. **MOS** has a net profit margin of −5.2%—operating at a loss—and revenue is down 6.0% year-over-year. One number alone reveals how different the two companies' current positions are.

The spread between natural gas costs and grain prices exerts a dual squeeze on both companies' margins. When natural gas prices rise, the manufacturing cost of nitrogen fertilizer goes up. Simultaneously, when grain prices fall, farmers' incomes shrink, shrinking fertilizer demand. Conversely, when grain prices rise, farmers' willingness to pay for fertilizer increases. Yet if natural gas costs are high, suppliers' margins tighten. In this structure, scale and diversification matter.

**NTR** is far larger at a market cap of $34.2 billion. A P/E of 14.5 shows the market recognizes fair value. The company's size and diverse business segments act as a cushion defending margins against price volatility. It operates with less sensitivity to single-product price swings.

**MOS**, by contrast, has a market cap of $6.8 billion—one-fifth of **NTR**'s. A negative net profit margin means the company is losing money selling fertilizer at current market prices. This signals either high natural gas input costs or insufficient selling prices. Compounded with revenue decline (−6.0%), structural challenges are severe.

Analyzing margin sensitivity in the nitrogen fertilizer industry reveals the following. When natural gas prices rise, manufacturing costs rise too. If selling prices are fixed, this increase translates directly into margin compression. When grain prices fall simultaneously, fertilizer demand also suffers. Both sales volume and margins tighten together. In this dual-shock structure, corporate fundamentals become decisive.

**NTR**'s positive recent performance (up 8.31% in three months) likely reflects better cost management or pricing power. Large companies more easily absorb volatility with revenues from diverse regions and customer bases. **MOS**'s decline (−8.42%) reveals vulnerability to either high natural gas costs or grain weakness, or both.

Future margin restructuring hinges on three variables. First, the direction of natural gas futures prices. Second, the persistence of grain price levels. Third, each company's pace of cost efficiency improvement. **NTR** holds an advantageous position across all three variables due to its scale. **MOS** faces potential restructuring or portfolio realignment if margins do not recover.

At this point, investors view **NTR**'s 4.0% revenue growth and 8.7% net profit margin as relatively healthy signals. **MOS**'s loss-making status and revenue decline operate as avoidance signals. The recent trajectories of the two stocks make clear how differently nitrogen fertilizer margin restructuring affects each company.
