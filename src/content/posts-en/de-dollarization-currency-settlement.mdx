The foundation of international settlement is undergoing fundamental restructuring. As BRICS members expand local currency settlement and China's yuan internationalizes, financial institutions and multinational firms long dependent on dollars face new foreign exchange risks. This shift is not merely a political gesture but a structural reconfiguration redrawing the map of trade and finance.

## Why Dollar Influence Wavers

**UUP** (Invesco DB US Dollar Index Bullish Fund) tracks U.S. dollar strength. As of 2026-10-23, the closing price was $28.96, rising 0.94% over one month and 1.26% over three months. The six-month range spanned $27.26 to $29.25.

These figures suggest the dollar remains strong. Yet beneath this stable price, a fundamental challenge to dollar dominance lurks. Small price movements mask rapidly shifting underlying conditions—a signal that structural environment is transforming.

## BRICS' Local Currency Expansion as Signal

BRICS members (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, plus recent entrants) deliberately expand local currency use in intra-member trade. This directly challenges the privileged status the dollar has held as international settlement's vehicle currency. Since 2022, this trend has accelerated.

The significance of local currency settlement runs deep. First, it reduces the need to convert to dollars in trade. Second, both parties can distribute foreign exchange rate risk. Third, it signals reduced dollar dependence to international capital markets.

Indian-Russian rupee-ruble trade settlement and Chinese-Brazilian yuan-real transaction expansion exemplify this. Each appears small, but combined they erode dollars' share in international payment mechanisms.

## China's Yuan Accelerates Internationalization

China pursues more aggressive expansion. The yuan advances in SWIFT transaction share, foreign exchange reserve composition, and international bond issuance denomination. This reflects not accident but systematic policy.

Yuan internationalization means diversified choice. International firms can now raise corporate funds and settle transactions in currencies other than the dollar. Particularly in Asia-Pacific, yuan transaction costs increasingly undercut the dollar.

For firms historically dependent on dollars, strategic choice now emerges. Dollar-settled transactions carry different exchange risk than yuan. The yuan responds directly to Chinese government policy. Alongside currency risk comes distinct political risk, unlike dollar risk.

## Currency Risk Reconfiguration for Dollar-Dependent Firms: Four Pressures

**1. Operational Complexity from Multi-Currency Settlement Expansion**

Multinational firms once invoiced and settled primarily in dollars; now they increasingly face local currency payment demands. Chinese suppliers prefer yuan settlement, and BRICS members insist on home currency.

This compounds foreign exchange hedging costs. Firms must simultaneously manage multiple currency positions. Each requires separate analysis and risk monitoring. Overall dollar rate risk alone suffices no longer.

**2. Selective Pressure to Reduce Dollar Holdings**

As national de-dollarization accelerates, global dollar supply and demand gradually decline. Central banks of emerging markets increasingly lower the dollar share of foreign exchange reserves.

This directly affects financial institutions. As institutions holding dollar assets begin rebalancing, dollar liquidity can deteriorate faster than anticipated. Lending demand for dollar funds falls while demand for dollar-raising loans grows.

**3. Currency Mismatch from Expanded Yuan-Denominated Assets**

Firms increasingly conduct business and earn revenue in yuan. Yet company liabilities often remain dollar-invoiced. This widens currency mismatch.

Specifically, when subsidiaries in China or Southeast Asia must repay U.S. dollar debt with yuan revenue, they face foreign exchange volatility. If the yuan weakens, dollar-converted costs rise. If corporate hedging inadequately covers this, profits shrink directly.

**4. Policy Risk and Emerging Currency Constraints**

The dollar enjoys backing from a clear central bank and fiscal authority—the United States. Markets respond within a predictable structure regardless of U.S. monetary policy choices. Emerging-market currencies like the yuan, ruble, and Indian rupee, by contrast, prove more sensitive to government capital controls, exchange rate adjustments, and sanctions.

When firms earn yuan and seek to reconvert to dollars, Chinese capital controls may obstruct the process. Or if geopolitical tensions rise, currency trading itself may face restriction. Such risks exceed foreign exchange risk alone—a political risk premium becomes necessary.

## Structural Strengths and Weaknesses of Dollar Reserve Currency Status

**Strengths: Enduring Depth and Liquidity**

The dollar remains the core of international finance. Most SWIFT transactions involve dollars, and more than half of international bond issuances occur in dollars. This depth and liquidity does not erode overnight.

For firms to genuinely transition to alternative currencies, those currencies must match the dollar's depth and liquidity. Currently the yuan and other emerging currencies do not. Therefore, cost-benefit analysis keeps the dollar central.

**Weaknesses: Eroding Confidence Between Nations and Geopolitical Risk**

Dollar dominance rests not on technical superiority alone but on trust in the United States. Yet as America weaponizes sanctions and asset freezes through monetary policy, that trust fragments. Russian dollar asset freezes and Iranian seizures symbolize the politicization of "dollar control."

This provokes BRICS nations. If assets can freeze overnight due to American policy shifts, reducing dollar dependence serves national interest. This reflects rational choice.

## Conclusion: New Currency Risk Landscape

De-dollarization is becoming financial reality, not declarative political gesture. UUP's modest rise suggests dollars remain strong, but payment structure undergoes broad transformation beneath the surface.

Firms now enter a world where dollar exchange risk alone proves insufficient. Exposure to emerging currencies—yuan, rupee, ruble, Brazilian real—expands. Each carries distinct volatility and policy risk.

Financial institutions must strengthen multi-currency position management capabilities. Corporate finance teams must fine-tune asset and liability currency composition. Traditional dollar-centric hedging strategies are now merely one option among several.
