## Quantum Processor Commercialization Accelerates, Creating New Capex Cycle in Semiconductor Equipment and Cooling Technology

As quantum computing transitions from laboratories to data centers, companies manufacturing equipment and ultra-low temperature cooling solutions are gaining attention. This is because processor yield improvement and cooling technology scaling are creating a new investment cycle in the semiconductor industry.

## Physical Challenges in Quantum Processor Manufacturing

Most quantum processors operate only at ultra-low temperatures near absolute zero (typically in the millikelvin range or below). While this is essential for protecting quantum states, it presents unprecedented manufacturing challenges.

Because qubits within the processor are extremely sensitive to environmental noise, much stricter process control is required across circuit microstructure, metal purity, and insulation properties compared to conventional semiconductors. Even trace impurities or minor defects can cause yields to plummet, which means manufacturing equipment precision must be elevated to an entirely new level.

## Why the Quantum Chip Supply Chain Is Moving Now: Four Factors

**1. Accelerating Yield Improvement Competition**: Quantum companies encounter yield limitations whenever they attempt to move beyond the prototype stage and mass-produce commercial-scale multi-qubit chips. Overcoming this requires next-generation equipment at each process step—precision etching, thin-film deposition, and metal deposition. Consequently, semiconductor equipment manufacturers have incentive to invest in quantum chip-specific process development.

**2. Essential Scaling of Ultra-Low Temperature Cooling**: Most quantum computers today use liquid helium or dilution refrigerators for cooling. As cloud quantum services proliferate and qubit counts increase, demand for cooling capacity grows exponentially. Maintaining hundreds of qubits stably on a single chip requires cooling performance and reliability to reach new standards.

**3. Novel Silicon Materials and Heterojunction Technology**: Quantum processors cannot rely on conventional CMOS processes alone. Depending on implementation approach—superconducting qubits, ion traps, topological qubits—novel materials such as niobium, tantalum, and SiGe heterolayers are required. Large-scale sourcing and high-purity processing of these materials become new investment targets.

**4. Realization of Post-Quantum Cryptography Demand**: Driven by concerns that quantum computers could break modern encryption, the U.S., European, and Chinese governments along with major institutions are advancing post-quantum cryptography standards. This process accelerates new chip design and deployment, with manufacturing capacity shortage becoming a concrete bottleneck.

## Yet Structural Risks Are Substantial

**Uncertainty in Technology Evolution Path**: The optimal quantum implementation approach has not yet been determined. Multiple technologies—superconducting, ion, photonic, topological qubits—are competing in parallel, and if one method becomes dominant, companies that invested in equipment for alternative approaches cannot depreciate those assets.

**High Initial Investment Barriers for Manufacturing Equipment**: Developing quantum chip-specific process equipment requires years of R&D and investments in the billions of dollars. Justifying such upfront investment is difficult while the market remains small.

**Cooling Technology Limitations and Cost**: Ultra-low temperature cooling has low energy efficiency and high maintenance costs. An unresolved challenge is that commercialization requires reducing cooling unit costs by tens of times their current levels for widespread adoption.

## Summary

Manufacturing equipment and cooling solution demand from quantum chip commercialization represents a real opportunity. However, with technology paths not yet settled, whether equipment and cooling makers bet on which approach remains a high-judgment question. Early leadership will go to companies that move first, but significant technical and commercial trial-and-error is needed before quantum technology becomes mainstream.
