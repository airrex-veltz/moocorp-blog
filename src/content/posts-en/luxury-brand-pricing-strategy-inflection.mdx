## Luxury's Crossroads

The luxury industry faces a simple yet profound dilemma. Consumer spending among the wealthy is cooling amid economic slowdown. Luxury brands must now choose one of two paths: protect net earnings through price increases, or sacrifice near-term profit to capture market share. At this exact inflection point, the strategies of global luxury players diverge sharply.

## The Fate of Two Strategies

As of October 2026, the industry contrast is stark.

**Tapestry Inc. (TPR)** has chosen growth. As of 2026-10-23, TPR's stock price stood at 110.37 USD, with a three-month change of −24.85%, trading near the lower band of its recent six-month range (109.79 USD). Yet year-over-year revenue growth reached 8.9%. Most tellingly: TPR maintains a net profit margin of 19.1%, a healthy level. This demonstrates that efficiency improvements and expanded sales have protected earnings without price increases.

**Capri Holdings Limited (CPRI)** has chosen margin defense. As of 2026-10-23, CPRI's stock price was 15.87 USD, up 4.48% over the preceding month and showing near-term recovery. Simultaneously, year-over-year revenue growth stood at −3.5%. Net profit margin is only 4.4%. CPRI's strategy is transparent: protect margins through price increases, accepting lower sales volume.

## Why This Difference Emerges?

The two companies' choices reflect not mere preference but **fundamental differences in market positioning**.

TPR owns Coach, Kate Spade, and Stuart Weitzman—brands occupying the entry point of luxury, the **accessible luxury** segment. Customers in this tier are more price-sensitive. TPR chose volume because under economic slowdown, as customers become price-conscious, demand for accessible luxury remains comparatively strong. Revenue growth through expanded sales is more realistic than maintaining prices.

CPRI owns Michael Kors, Jimmy Choo, and Versace. Michael Kors was once a leader in accessible luxury but has lost position in recent years. CPRI's −3.5% revenue decline reflects this erosion. Facing existing customer defection, CPRI has attempted to maximize loyalty among remaining customers through price increases. Yet a net profit margin of 4.4% shows this strategy has fallen short.

## Historical Lessons

This dilemma is not new. The 2008 financial crisis presented similar choices.

Back then, the luxury industry split into two camps: one pursued **price maintenance plus marketing intensity**, while the other was **willing to cede share to preserve margins**. The result became clear during the recovery period (2009–2011). Brands that held share rebounded faster. Why? During recovery, consumers return to brands with which they have built habits. By contrast, brands that raised prices to protect margins lost new customers, and those customers remained difficult to win back after recovery.

Today's environment differs. Unlike 2008, rapid recovery cannot be assumed. Geopolitical tensions, gradual inflation moderation, and explosive digital channel growth interact in complex ways. In such an environment, which strategy is correct?

## Current Risk Signals

TPR and CPRI each signal future pitfalls.

**TPR's risk is margin erosion.** In choosing growth, the company is exposed to intensifying price competition. As of 2026-10-23, TPR's P/E ratio stood at 15.2, far below CPRI's 20.3, suggesting the market believes TPR cannot sustain its growth rate. Once margins compress, accessible luxury customers become even more price-sensitive, creating a downward spiral.

**CPRI's risk is more immediate: accelerating customer loss.** A net profit margin of 4.4% signals short-term earnings protection at the cost of the long-term customer base. As of 2026-10-23, CPRI's market cap is 1.8 billion USD, roughly one-twelfth of TPR's 21.9 billion USD, indicating the market already discounts CPRI's future prospects.

## The Choice Is Already Clear

History and present circumstances offer the same lesson. In an economic slowdown, **near-term margins become a snare** for luxury companies. Raising prices today to protect profit tomorrow erodes customer foundation. Conversely, protecting share means accepting margin compression in the short term, but it positions a company for strength when recovery comes.

The choices of TPR and CPRI demonstrate this principle starkly. TPR's 8.9% revenue growth proves it the right course. CPRI's −3.5% revenue decline shows where the opposite path leads. The next quarter for luxury companies is already determined. The only question is whether every company learns this lesson.
