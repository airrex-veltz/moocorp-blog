## Financial Incentives Cannot Overcome API Cost Gap—US CAPEX Trap Before Indian Generics

Despite an influx of government subsidies, domestic production of pharmaceutical active ingredients (APIs) remains undercut by India's low-cost competition. While the US and EU have since 2023 encouraged domestic investment in semiconductors and batteries through the CHIPS Act and the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), the situation in pharmaceutical raw materials is different. Despite billions of dollars being invested ostensibly for supply chain security, drug manufacturers continue to choose Indian APIs. Why?

## API Industry Cost Structure: Economies of Scale and Absolute Labor Cost Advantage

API manufacturing is a fine chemicals industry. The chemical processes, cost management, and environmental compliance required to produce one gram of raw pharmaceutical material create an extreme cost gap between developed and developing countries. As of 2024, India supplies over 55% of global API volume. Skilled labor costs are one-sixth to one-eighth of US levels, and raw material and utility costs are also lower. Consequently, standard antibiotics (such as amoxicillin) from Indian API producers cost $5–10 per kilogram, while new US and European facilities exceed $20–30 per kilogram.

## Why Domestic Reshoring Is Blocked Now: Four Structural Pressures

**1. Without Economies of Scale, Cost Recovery Is Impossible**: API factories are based on large-batch production. Recovering initial CAPEX of $3–5 billion requires annual shipments exceeding hundreds of tons. Even if US and European pharmaceutical companies receive government incentives, they lack motivation to abandon their existing Indian supply chains. While drug manufacturers want supply chain diversification (dual-sourcing), they refuse to make new domestic facilities their primary source.

**2. Regulatory Approval Alone Takes 2–3 Years**: FDA and EMA validation, GMP (Good Manufacturing Practice) certification, and joint testing with pharmaceutical manufacturers take years. Even with government funding, actual revenue generation requires a 4–5 year wait. During this period, Indian competitors cut costs another 3–5%.

**3. Single-Customer Dependency Risk**: If an API factory depends on orders from only one or two pharmaceutical companies, the business ends if the contract is not renewed. This means it is difficult to secure dispersed demand from ten or more customers. Domestic new factories struggle to guarantee such multi-customer contracts, while established Indian factories already have them.

**4. Government Funding Condition Conflicts**: The CHIPS Act, IRA, and similar programs stipulate labor conditions, environmental standards, and wage floors. These actually undermine domestic competitiveness. This directly conflicts with the situation where Indian players have weak union pressure and looser environmental regulations while maintaining advanced-country-level technology.

## Why Domestic API Must Still Be Pursued: Supply Chain Risk and Geopolitics

**Supply Dynamics Volatility**: India faced the Nipah virus in 2023 and an antibiotic raw material shortage in 2025, with some API prices spiking 40%. Dependence on a single supply country has insurance value. Even if government incentives are imperfect, when geopolitical stability is priced in, domestic factory expansion is not a loss.

**Technology Sovereignty and Next-Generation Pharmaceuticals**: As the pace of new drug development accelerates, the need for customized API development and production grows. The Indian supply chain is optimized for standard generics. For the US and EU to secure high-value biotech drug raw materials domestically, today's CAPEX investments will pay dividends in ten years.

## Summary

Government incentives are insufficient because India's low-cost structure is fundamentally different. US and European manufacturers will only switch to domestic API supply if they perceive strategic benefit sufficient to accept 20-plus year contract lock-ins. It is possible with larger subsidies, accelerated regulation, and a focus on high-value APIs, but current policy remains middling.
