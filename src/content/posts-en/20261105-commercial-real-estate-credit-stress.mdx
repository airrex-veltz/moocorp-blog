## How Rising Rates Became a Debt Trap

Commercial mortgages on office and retail buildings financed during the pre-2022 low-rate era now face maturity and refinancing barriers. Property owners find themselves unable to renew debt terms in today's higher-rate environment, repeatedly pushed to the brink of default.

Credit risk in commercial real estate runs deeper than rising rates alone. A building's value is determined by dividing the cash flow it generates by the market's cap rate. When cap rates rise, that same cash flow produces a lower valuation. Higher rates signal higher required yields for investors, which erodes the collateral value of the building itself. Debt remains fixed while the collateral backing it shrinks—a vicious cycle has begun.

## Why Commercial Property Defaults Are Accelerating Now

**1. Rapid deterioration of debt service capacity**: Office and retail rents depend directly on tenant income. Remote work expansion post-pandemic has raised office vacancies, while online retail growth has eroded the value of physical retail locations. At refinancing, buildings generate lower cash flows than before, yet face significantly higher interest rates. More properties fail to meet lenders' minimum debt service coverage ratios.

**2. Tenant mix shifts and limited rent growth**: Owners seek to improve cash flows by raising rents, but the market won't bear the increases. In a low-growth economy locked into high rates, tenants lack capacity to absorb rent hikes. As major anchor tenants vacate or refuse lease renewal, the revenue foundation of the property itself destabilizes.

**3. Inability to sell**: Owners trying to sell and pay off debt often find themselves in negative equity—the sale price cannot cover the mortgage balance. Trapped in a triple bind: unable to sell, unable to afford refinance rates, and unable to improve cash flow through rent increases. Default or negotiated loss become the only paths forward.

**4. Contagion and signaling effects**: Early defaults signal the market, prompting lenders to tighten commercial real estate lending standards further. This creates immediate credit pressure on vulnerable owners. Deteriorating market sentiment becomes self-fulfilling, accelerating actual financial crisis.

## Structural Weaknesses in Commercial Real Estate Credit

**Structural vulnerability: The limits of fixed-rate borrowing**

In the past, low-rate loans transferred interest-rate risk from borrowers to lenders. Now those loans are maturing into today's rate reality. The threshold where refinancing becomes impossible keeps falling. With building cash flows declining simultaneously to rising rates, commercial real estate credit risk stands at historic highs.

**Differentiated risk by property type**

Office and retail face fundamental demand erosion risk. Industrial properties like logistics centers and multifamily housing enjoy relatively robust demand, while downtown offices and traditional retail corridors face structural decline. Lenders' valuations reflect this: identical loan-to-value ratios yield dramatically different credit quality by property type.

## Conclusion

The commercial real estate mortgage crisis is not a problem of rate levels but rate structure. Debt borrowed at low rates must be serviced in a high-rate environment, while collateral values simultaneously decline and the properties' cash generation weakens. When structural demand weakness in office and retail compounds this, credit stress becomes severe. Microlevel signals—tenant departures, remote work spread—are accumulating into systemic risk.
