## Why Food Companies' Profit Margins Are Under Pressure: The Mismatch Between Raw Material Price Volatility and Consumer Price Increases

Prices of major raw materials like corn, wheat, and dairy are fluctuating over monthly cycles, destabilizing food companies' profit structures. As of November 16, 2026, the extreme divergence in profitability among companies within the same industry reflects this reality.

## Current Profitability Divergence Among Food Companies

As of the November 16, 2026 closing, net profit margins of major food companies are as follows. **KO** (Coca-Cola) maintains the industry's highest level with a net profit margin of 28.6% and a 3-month stock price gain of 12.44%. **MKC** (McCormick) shows a solid net profit margin of 19.4%, but its stock has fallen 13.90% over the past three months, indicating the market's dampened outlook. **MDLZ** (Mondelēz) lags at a net profit margin of only 10.2%—less than half—and its 2.3% revenue growth rate suggests the limits of pricing power. The worst performer is **GIS** (General Mills), with a net profit margin of -4.9% in a loss-making state and revenue declining 2.8% year-over-year.

This vast gap within the same industry reflects the influence of raw material price volatility.

## Four Reasons Raw Material Price Volatility Shakes Food Companies' Breakeven Points

**1. Timing mismatch between rising costs and consumer price increases**

Even when raw material prices rise, companies cannot immediately raise selling prices. As retailers consider contract renewals, consumer sentiment, and competitor responses while implementing price increases gradually, commodity costs may fall in the meantime. Conversely, when costs fall but prices do not drop, margins increase but trade relationships and consumer trust decline. This timing mismatch amplifies quarterly earnings volatility.

**2. Hedging through long-term contracts vs. spot price divergence**

Large food companies secure raw materials through 6-month to 1-year futures contracts. Once contract prices are fixed, even if spot prices rise, costs remain unchanged—but when spot prices fall, contract prices become comparatively expensive. Depending on how precisely a company structures its hedging strategy, the same environment can yield profits for one firm and losses for another.

**3. Limits on consumer price elasticity**

As everyday food prices rise, consumption volumes fall. This effect intensifies during economic downturns. In developed markets, discretionary products (candy, snacks) struggle to command premium prices, while essential beverages (Coca-Cola) show greater resistance to price hikes. This divide determines the fate of mega-brands like KO versus smaller food companies. If raising prices produces minimal volume loss, profits rise—but if volumes also decline, revenue falls instead. MDLZ's low growth rate of 2.3% suggests limited pricing power.

**4. Differences in cost management capability**

Even as raw material prices rise, some companies endure through supply chain efficiency, shrinkflation, and cost-cutting innovation. Others cannot withstand such pressures due to rigid cost structures and weakened competitiveness. GIS's loss-making state (a negative 4.9% net profit margin) reveals structural weaknesses that cannot be recovered through pricing alone. By contrast, MKC achieved recent revenue growth of 17.4%, successfully passing costs to consumers, yet its P/E ratio of 8.5 (the lowest) signals that the market doubts this acceleration is sustainable.

## Structural Strengths and Weaknesses: Scale and Brand vs. Cost Structure

**KO's pricing power**
Coca-Cola, the world's largest beverage company, enjoys such strong brand value that volume losses from price increases are minimal. Its net profit margin of 28.6% is the evidence. Even when raw material prices rise, consumers bear the cost, so near-term cost pressures do not break long-term earnings.

**MKC's growth versus valuation disconnect**
McCormick, with spices and seasonings, enjoys low consumption volumes and high price elasticity. The company succeeded in raising prices with 17.4% revenue growth, yet its P/E of 8.5 reflects analyst consensus that "this growth cannot be sustained." If cost pressure returns, margin rates risk collapsing.

**MDLZ and GIS in turmoil**
Mondelēz in chocolate and snacks, and General Mills in cereals, operate in discretionary consumer goods where price increases directly translate to volume declines. MDLZ's 2.3% revenue growth means price increases (mostly successful) were offset by volume drops, while GIS's losses signal lost balance. As consumption patterns shift (pursuit of healthier foods, declining snack consumption), companies can no longer rely on price increases alone.

## Summary

Amid raw material price volatility, the competitive factor for food companies is shifting from "how quickly can we raise prices" to "how precisely can we manage costs and retain consumers." Even within the same environment, the diverging fortunes of well-positioned firms like KO and weakened ones like GIS reflect not mere business cycles but structural industry change.
