## Mechanism of Rising Office Vacancy in Global Business Districts and Dividend Pressure on REITs

Commercial real estate vacancy rates in major business districts worldwide—New York, London, and Tokyo among them—are surging, intensifying dividend adjustment pressure on commercial real estate investment trusts (REITs). This is more than a decline in rental income; it signals structural vulnerabilities in the REIT business model itself.

## Structural Drivers Behind Rising Vacancy Rates

Since the pandemic, acceptance of remote work, reduced office space demand from tech companies, and normalization of hybrid work arrangements have led to cumulative decisions by firms to downsize their leased spaces. Meanwhile, new supply continues while demand recovery lags, intensifying the supply-demand imbalance. Particularly, concentration of demand on prime office buildings is accelerating higher vacancy in ordinary B-grade and C-grade buildings.

## Four Structural Drivers of Dividend Adjustment Pressure on REITs

**1. Declining Rental Revenue and Shrinking Operating Income**: Rising vacancy creates lost rental income during vacant periods, while new leases command lower rates than before. Renewing leases with existing tenants increasingly requires rate reductions to retain them. This directly contracts REIT operating cash flow.

**2. Rising Maintenance Costs**: Longer vacancy periods mean fixed costs—equipment maintenance, security, cleaning—persist regardless of occupancy rate. Vacant spaces still require management, worsening cost efficiency. Simultaneously, the need for capital investment in renovations to regain competitiveness increases.

**3. Conflict with Debt Repayment Obligations**: Many REITs have financed property acquisitions and improvements through borrowing. Dividend policy assumes the ability to service debt obligations. When deteriorating cash flow makes it difficult to meet both dividends and debt service, credit ratings fall and debt refinancing becomes harder.

**4. Contradiction Between Dividend Policy and Capital Preservation**: For investors, REITs' appeal lies in high dividend yields. Yet in downturns, maintaining dividends requires either depleting capital or taking on additional debt. Cutting dividends risks stock price declines, which weakens capital-raising ability. Both options are unattractive.

## Structural Strengths and Weaknesses

**REITs' Inherent Weakness**: REIT structures carry a legal obligation to distribute most earnings as dividends. During downturns, dividend pressure persists even as cash flow shrinks, giving REITs lower structural resilience than general real estate companies.

**Risk of Asset Value Decline**: Falling rental revenue ultimately affects the valuation of real estate assets themselves. Declining valuations → reduced collateral value → shrinking borrowing capacity → difficulty in additional capital raising. A vicious cycle.

**Constrained Choices**: The longer recovery takes, the more REITs must choose among portfolio restructuring (selling weaker assets), operational restructuring (workforce reductions), or changing dividend policy. None of these are positive for near-term stock prices.

## Conclusion

Dividend pressure on commercial real estate REITs is not mere cyclical adjustment but a signal of market restructuring driven by structural changes in how people work. Caught between maintaining their appeal as dividend-paying investments and addressing structural cash flow deterioration, the REIT industry likely faces intensifying competitive survival pressures.
