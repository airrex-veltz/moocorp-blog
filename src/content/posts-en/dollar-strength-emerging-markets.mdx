The phenomenon of U.S. long-term Treasury bonds simultaneously weakening amid dollar strength has recently come into focus. Typically, dollar strength leads to safe-asset preference, which should push Treasury prices higher, but reality is the opposite. This paradoxical movement is working as a mechanism connecting the Federal Reserve's interest rate policy signals to emerging market currency weakness.

**Treasury Weakness and Rising Yields**

The U.S. long-term Treasury ETF **TLT** closed at $81.49 as of 2026-11-24, down 0.99% over the past three months. Having declined from a six-month high of $86.07 to the current level, the decline in Treasury prices directly means rising yields. Short-term, there has been a modest bounce of +0.19% over one month, but the medium-term trend is clearly weakening.

This signals that the market is reflecting expectations that the Federal Reserve will maintain or raise rates. As Treasury yields rise, the opportunity cost of new capital increases, creating a calculation that funds that should be devoted to risky asset investment must instead be redirected to Treasuries. Paradoxically, in an environment of dollar strength, 'safe-asset preference' is appearing not as Treasury strength but as dollar strength. The rising Treasury yield also means that holding dollar cash becomes more attractive than investing in physical bonds.

**Sharp Decline in Emerging Market Stock Prices**

The decline in emerging markets is even steeper. Despite being undervalued at a P/E of 8.6, the China large-cap ETF **FXI** recorded substantial declines of -3.32% over one month and -11.59% over three months, closing at $31.43 on the same date. It has fallen from a six-month high of $36.55 and is now trading near the low of $31.18.

This goes beyond simple Chinese economic weakness. In an environment of dollar strength, both emerging market investors and international capital prefer dollar-denominated assets over local currency assets. Even if emerging market central banks defend their currencies with foreign exchange reserves, they struggle to withstand the pressure of international capital returning to dollars. Ultimately, a vicious cycle forms: emerging market currency weakness → increased external debt servicing costs → stock price declines.

**Rising Treasury Yields Squeeze Emerging Markets**

The situation can be summarized as follows. First, rising U.S. Treasury yields (TLT price decline) reflect Federal Reserve tightening signals. Second, this increases dollar borrowing costs, raising emerging market external debt burdens, since most emerging market debt is dollar-denominated. Third, emerging market asset weakness creates currency depreciation pressure. Fourth, local investors flee increasingly to dollars. As this cycle repeats, emerging market central banks' foreign exchange reserves become depleted and borrowing conditions worsen.

The simultaneous occurrence of Treasury weakness and dollar strength differs from past patterns. Historically, dollar strength has been interpreted as 'safe-asset flight,' meaning Treasury strength. Currently, Treasuries are weak but the dollar is not weaker, and this situation persists, suggesting that emerging market currency weakness pressure may continue.

**Emerging Market Policymakers' Dilemma**

This structure forces a difficult choice on emerging market central banks. If they raise rates to defend their currency, economic growth slows. Conversely, if they lower rates, their currency weakens further. Currently, FXI's P/E of 8.6 suggests undervaluation, but for its value to recover in this environment, either the dollar strength phase must clearly reverse or emerging market economic fundamentals must improve markedly.
