## Brazil's drought redefines global coffee price peaks

Brazil, the world's largest coffee producer, faces extreme drought that has pushed raw material futures up more than 50% in three months. Consumer brands like Starbucks now confront a structure where rising costs must be passed to customers, while coffee investors are betting on deepening supply shocks.

## Drought scale and coffee market dependency

Brazil accounts for about 40% of global coffee production. Starting in February, the coffee belt in Minas Gerais and São Paulo states fell into severe drought, with rainfall dropping to 70% or less below normal. Since coffee trees have a 3–5 year cultivation cycle, trees damaged this year will lead to reduced harvests for years to come. On the ICE futures market, Arabica bean prices surged from 140 cents in late February to above 210 cents in mid-August.

## Why prices are continuing to rise: 4 factors

**1. Expanding and repeating climate risk**: Global warming is shortening extreme drought cycles in Brazil from a 5-year pattern to every 2–3 years. This shift from isolated events to structural threats has reshaped market expectations.

**2. Constraints among alternative producers**: Vietnam, India, Colombia, and other alternative suppliers are limited in scaling up due to climate instability, disease, and profitability pressures. Their capacity to fill Brazil's gap is constrained.

**3. Consumer company margin pressure**: **SBUX** has coffee representing 25–30% of store sales costs. Raising prices risks customer defection. B2B suppliers like hotel and café chains face direct margin pressure.

**4. Leading investment demand**: As coffee-tracking ETFs like **JO** and institutional investors increase long positions, every drought headline triggers additional futures price spikes. A psychological price discovery mechanism is at work.

## Yet opportunity and risk coexist: Structural points

**Coffee company pricing power**: Starbucks has brand loyalty and premium positioning that gives it room to absorb price increases. However, mid-tier café chains will accelerate customer loss.

**ETF resilience and limits**: JO includes coffee futures, producer diversification, and related equities, providing some buffer against Brazil-specific shocks. But if global coffee supply itself contracts, diversification becomes ineffective.

**Policy intervention potential**: If Brazil's government intervenes with irrigation infrastructure investment or export tax adjustments, new variables emerge.

## Summary

Brazil's drought signals not just short-term supply shortage but structural climate change. Starbucks withstands it through cost-passing ability, but consumption risk looms large. Coffee investors prefer high prices given projections of years-long supply recovery. How long this imbalance persists will be the key to investment decisions.
