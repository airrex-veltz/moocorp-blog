Marcus had spent forty-three years on the floor of the Exchange, his voice hoarse from shouting bids and offers, his hands scarred from the friction of paper and the occasional fist-bump of a sealed deal. The pit had been his cathedral—a place where fortunes pivoted on a widened eye, where a man's word was his bond, where he could read the market's temperature by the sweat on another trader's forehead.

By 2024, the pits were mausoleums.

The migration had been gradual, then sudden. First came the electronic systems in the '90s, but they'd coexisted with the floor. Then the microwave networks, the fiber-optic cables running beneath Manhattan, the high-frequency trading firms with their servers in armored boxes, executing ten thousand trades per second—trades that ended before Marcus's eye could even register the bid. The human element, the thing that had always mattered most, had become noise in the statistical model.

The floor itself had changed. Where once it had thrummed with kinetic energy—the roar of competing voices, the scrape of shoes on worn tiles, the urgent flutter of hand signals—now it was sepulchral. The air hung heavy with absence. Fluorescent lights flickered over empty pits. The old wooden furniture, worn smooth by decades of elbows and knees, sat abandoned. Cleaning crews came more often than traders now.

He watched the younger traders disappear first, lured by the algorithms and the promise of a working life that didn't pulverize your voice by noon. The firms kept a skeleton crew on the floor for regulatory theater and the occasional large institutional order that couldn't be fully algorithmic. Marcus remained because he didn't know how to be anything else. He was the last of his kind, a living artifact in a museum that nobody visited.

His daughter had asked him years ago what he did for work. He'd tried to explain it—the patterns, the relationships, the way you could sense a trend breaking before the data confirmed it. She'd said, "So it's gambling?" He'd never quite answered that. How could he explain that it wasn't luck but discipline? That every wrinkle on another trader's face was data? That the market was a conversation if you knew how to listen?

By his final morning, even Marcus recognized the irony. The floor was cleaner than it had ever been. No crumpled sell tickets scattered like autumn leaves. No roaring crowd. Just a few dozen people in jackets, scrolling through smartphones, looking like they'd rather be anywhere else. The air felt sterile, like a place preparing for something. Like a deathbed before the priest arrives.

The order came through at 11:47 AM on a Tuesday—a massive institutional buy, a Fortune 500 company hedging a currency exposure. The algorithm couldn't price it cleanly because the size created micro-delays in the liquidity chain. A human intermediary was needed, something almost extinct. The electronic traders paused, waiting for the math. For a brief moment, the old hierarchies reasserted themselves: they looked to the floor, to where experience still lived.

Marcus stepped forward, his knees protesting, his palms beginning that familiar tingle.

His hands knew what to do before his mind could catch up. He'd spent four decades reading the micro-expressions on the sell side, feeling the texture of panic versus conviction in the market's movements. He sensed the slight overhang in the book, the dealers who were nervous about inventory, the ones who could absorb volume without bleeding the spread. He could feel it in his bones, in the way his heartbeat accelerated, the knowledge that comes not from calculation but from years of standing in this pit, breathing the same recycled air, living the same rhythms.

But that wasn't what mattered.

Not the algorithm. Him. In that moment, standing in a cathedral gone silent, Marcus understood that he had done something no machine ever would: he had earned faith.

Outside, the market continued its infinitesimal transactions, each one faster than thought. Somewhere in the digital depths, his final trade was already archived, already compressed into the aggregate data that made the system work.
